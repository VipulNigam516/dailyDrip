Quality B+, value Full: a business that earns 33% on its operating capital with no debt, held to a 91-statement record of promises it keeps on money and misses on dates, at a price 17% above our central value of ₹2,019

Caplin sells generics in Latin America from its own warehouses (79% of FY2026 revenue of Rs 2,187 Cr, 92% of segment profit) and, since 2016, injectables and eye drops to the US from Caplin Steriles (21% of revenue, 7% of profit). Operating margin is 34.8%, profit after tax Rs 641 Cr (Rs 650 Cr on the company’s basis), free cash reserves Rs 1,471 Cr, borrowings Rs 4.6 Cr. We read 26 of the 27 known earnings calls in full, in two passes (the commitments, then the analyst questions alone), plus both AGMs, the whole forum thread and thirteen annual reports with their tables.

Four findings drive the conclusion. (1) Every point of the seven-year improvement in pre-tax margin is treasury income: operating margin is 95 bp below FY19 and other income is 235 bp above it; the operating gain is a recovery since the FY23 trough, about half shared with peers. (2) Profit is booked faster than cash arrives: ten-year cash conversion is 0.76x, receivable days went from 30 to 138, every stated benchmark (about 90, 92–93, below 120, 100–120 with room for 10 more) has been exceeded, a first credit loss of Rs 22.97 Cr appeared in FY26, and the Rs 2,726 Cr of ‘liquid assets’ the Chairman cites includes Rs 1,255 Cr of receivables and inventory. (3) Management is reliable on money and unreliable on dates: of 91 dated statements 24 were kept, 30 slipped, 15 broke and 22 are open; plant, API, oncology and US timelines slipped 1–3 years in nearly every case, the US$100 million target date was abandoned, and the FY22-revenue-becomes-FY28-profit frame, which needs 42% a year, was dropped without announcement. (4) Governance is weaker than the accounts: three promoters on a ten-seat board, a promoter-run CSR trust, 38% of institutional votes against the CFO’s appointment as a director, a Form 483 with ten unclassified observations, and, on 26 calls, not one question about related parties, the auditor, ratings or concentration.

Valuation leads with the reverse DCF: at the current margin the price needs 21% revenue growth for ten years, or a 9.6% discount rate for our base case; no operating margin up to 40% reaches it on base growth. Scenario DCF: bear ₹994, base ₹1,517, bull ₹2,163 (weighted ₹1,548); exit-multiple cross-check ₹1,348, ₹2,443, ₹3,728 (weighted ₹2,491); central value ₹2,019. The analysis changes below about ₹1,550 (Cheap), at ₹2,050 (Fair) and above ₹2,600 (Stretched). This is not a buy or sell call.

01The business: a Rest-of-World cash machine, a US injectables build that earns 7% of profit, and a Rs 1,471 Cr treasury that earns 14%

What it sells, where the profit really comes from, who owns it, and how far the moat claims can be checked

Caplin Point Laboratories (Chennai) sells generic medicines that it makes in India or buys from China into Latin America through its own subsidiaries (Guatemala, Nicaragua, El Salvador, Honduras, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Panama, Colombia, and since 2024 Chile and Mexico). Since 2016 it has also built Caplin Steriles, a plant at Gummidipoondi near Chennai that makes injectables and eye drops for the US. FY2026 (year to March 2026) consolidated revenue was Rs 2,187 Cr, of which the segment note puts Rest of the World at Rs 1,734 Cr (79.3%) and the USA at Rs 453 Cr (20.7%). Profit after tax was Rs 641 Cr on the Screener basis used throughout this report and Rs 650 Cr in the company’s own statements (the company figure is before minority interests; the same gap exists in FY22, Rs 308 Cr against Rs 299.8 Cr).

How the Latin American business works, in management’s words

The model is to stock, not just ship. Each country subsidiary holds inventory in its own warehouse and sells to pharmacies, co-distributors and governments. The Chairman put the stock policy this way on the Q4 FY2026 call:

“Hence, we always keep the goods in our subsidiary warehouses for a minimum of six-months stock in the form of stock in the warehouse, stock-in transit, and work-in progress.”

— C. C. Paarthipan, Chairman, Q4 FY2026 earnings call, 14 May 2026

The Vice-Chairman responsible for Latin America, Ashok Partheeban, gave the one channel split the company has offered on any call (Q4 FY2026). It is the answer to a question first put on 8 Aug 2023, when the Chairman declined to give it because “disclosing everything leads to self-victimization” (Q1 FY2024 call).

“So, in each of these countries, we have two or three strong co-distributors, they sell around about 10% to 12% of our sales, and the rest of it all is us going directly to the pharmacies.”

— Ashok Partheeban, Vice-Chairman, Q4 FY2026 earnings call, 14 May 2026. He also said about 18% to 20% of Central American business is institutional (government).

What management says the moat is, and why it needs a scepticism flag. The Chairman’s own account is that the product is a commodity and the edge is distribution (Q1 FY2026 call, 7 Aug 2025):

“it is not the generic that really gives you the money, it is the business model that actually makes you this kind of actually cash flow and profits.”

— C. C. Paarthipan, Chairman, Q1 FY2026 earnings call, 7 Aug 2025

That is plausible and it is also unchecked. Management says it is the only importer-replacer in the region; no call names a competitor, the Chairman said the products do not appear in IMS market-share data except in Guatemala, and no regulator or tender portal data was available to us (section 6). In the same answer he gave the only country-level number we found on any of 26 calls: Guatemala is about US$50 million of sales, against total Latin American sales of about US$150 million in FY2025, the nearest full year (Vice-Chairman’s letter; the figure was given in August 2025, so the bases differ slightly). That is roughly one third of the Latin American business in one country with a stable currency (the quetzal averaged 7.83 per US dollar in 2012 and 7.68 in 2025, World Bank). Analysts asked for the country split on five calls and were refused or given only a rank order (section 8); none put single-country dependence to management.

“Yes, our products do not figure in the IMS, except in one country, Guatemala , where we are next to the multinationals, because that is where this is a country where the population is only 17 to 18 million. But we do a business of $50 million there.”

— C. C. Paarthipan, Chairman, Q1 FY2026 earnings call, 7 Aug 2025. Company claim; no independent market-share data was found for any of Caplin’s countries.

Sourcing is part of the model and has a China leg. Vivek Partheeban said on the Q2 FY2026 call that almost 35% of products are sourced out of China and exported directly to Latin America. At the 25 Sep 2026 AGM the Chairman said that China-linked business, which had been “45 to 50%” of the whole, has come down to 25 to 30%; the sentence does not say whether this is a share of sales or of sourcing, so we do not use it as a number.

Caplin Steriles and the family of claims around it

Caplin Steriles Limited (CSL) has six manufacturing lines today by the AGM count (five on the Q4 FY2026 call, six plus a seventh coming on the Q1 FY2027 call; section 12), 59 approved ANDAs of which 15 were bought from third parties in January 2026 (CFO, AGM), and since November 2024 a US own-label arm, Caplin Steriles USA (CSU), which management says earned a 26.2% EBITDA margin on about US$11 million in its first full year. CSL revenue was about Rs 473 Cr in FY2026 (+29%); the USA segment in the audited accounts is Rs 453 Cr. The wholesale, GPO and hospital-system customers are a handful of large buyers; management says its partners keep about half the profit on licensed products.

Two outside investors sit inside this subsidiary. Eight Roads Ventures India LLP (70%) and F-Prime Capital Partners Life Sciences Fund VI LLP (30%) hold 7.46 crore Series A compulsorily convertible preference shares of CSL (Rs 74.58 Cr face value, with Rs 141.99 Cr of securities premium on preference shares in reserves, Rs 216.6 Cr in all; Note 15). Caplin shows them inside equity attributable to owners rather than as a minority, and the conversion terms were never disclosed: asked on the first call in Feb 2019 whether “25% is the maximum they could get”, Vivek Partheeban replied that “several parts of the deal is confidential”. In May 2021 he said the structure “is 75-25 and it will remain exactly the same”. The practical consequence is that up to a quarter of CSL’s eventual value may belong to Fidelity’s funds rather than to Caplin shareholders, and the profit attributable to Caplin owners (Rs 641 Cr) is stated before any such claim. We carry it as a valuation sensitivity (section 14), not as a finding of misstatement.

Ownership. The scrutinizer’s report for the 25 Sep 2026 AGM gives 7.60 crore shares: promoter group 5.364 crore (70.57%), institutions 0.597 crore (7.86%), other public holders 1.640 crore (21.57%), from 104,458 shareholders on record. The family’s control is absolute on any ordinary or special resolution; section 12 shows how the institutions voted when they had the chance.

What this section changes

Read Caplin as three things, not one: a Rest-of-the-World cash machine, mostly Latin America (79% of revenue, 92% of segment profit before tax, perhaps a third of the Latin American sales in one country), a US injectables business that is 21% of revenue and 7% of profit but absorbs nearly all the capital being deployed, and a Rs 1,471 Cr treasury that supplies 14% of profit. Most of the debate on calls is about the second; most of the value is in the first and the third.

Era by era

FY17–FY19: the model changes. Revenue rose from Rs 402 Cr to Rs 649 Cr and receivables from Rs 33 Cr to Rs 160 Cr. The company stopped relying on customer advances and began financing tender buyers; the stock fell about 40% in a week in May 2018 when the FY18 receivables jump was published (ValuePickr thread, summary grade). The first ever earnings call was on 11 Feb 2019, about three weeks after the exchange filing of Fidelity’s investment in Caplin Steriles (18 Jan 2019).

FY20–FY22: acquisitions, COVID, and the frame. The channel partners were bought out in FY20 (inventory Rs 37 Cr to Rs 238 Cr), margins fell, then recovered as COVID shortages let Caplin sell where others could not (Chairman, Q4 FY2020 call: “So the current situation is in favor of Caplin”). The FY2021 annual report then reported that FY15’s revenue (Rs 254.8 Cr) had become FY21’s profit (Rs 251.4 Cr), and the FY2022 annual report set the next frame: FY22 revenue becomes FY28 profit (section 9).

FY23: the stall. Operating margin hit the decade low of 30.1% and quarterly EBITDA stood near Rs 100 Cr for four quarters. On the Q1 FY2023 call Sachin Kasera put it directly, and the Chairman’s reply was to change the comparison group:

“in the last four quarters, our operating profit is now standing at close to around INR100 crore. And while I understand that it has been challenging, but that is not the track record that we as investors are used to at Caplin.”

— Sachin Kasera, Svan Investment, Q1 FY2023 earnings call, 5 Aug 2022. The reply: ‘if you try and compare our growth with other companies’ growth of our size ... then there won’t be any issues, I think. Am I right?’

FY24–FY26: moderate growth, rising margin, rising cash and receivables. Revenue growth of 15.5%, 14.4% and 12.9% came with operating margin rising from 30.1% to 34.8%, free cash reserves rising from Rs 774 Cr to Rs 1,471 Cr, and receivable days from 98 to 138. At the Q4 FY2026 call (14 May 2026) the Chairman summarised the five years in the company’s own terms:

“Revenue doubles from INR 1,085 crores to INR 2,300 crores in FY25-26. Liquid assets tripled from INR 928 crores to INR 2,726 crores. Free cash reserves tripled from INR 470 crores to INR 1,471 crores.”

— C. C. Paarthipan, Chairman, Q4 FY2026 earnings call, 14 May 2026

Two of those three figures need a footnote. Revenue of Rs 2,300 Cr is total income including Rs 116 Cr of other income (operating revenue is Rs 2,187 Cr). And ‘liquid assets’ of Rs 2,726 Cr is not liquid: it is free cash reserves of Rs 1,471 Cr plus receivables of Rs 826 Cr plus inventory of Rs 429 Cr, which sum to Rs 2,726 Cr to the rupee. The CFO confirmed the composition on the Q1 FY2027 call (“primarily consist of cash, receivables and inventory”). Of the Rs 1,798 Cr rise in ‘liquid assets’ since FY21, Rs 797 Cr is receivables and inventory. The free cash reserve, which is liquid, tripled; the headline overstates what a shareholder could draw.

What this section changes

The ten-year record is a high-margin, no-debt compounding story with one flaw that never goes away: profit is booked faster than cash arrives. The flaw is old (it began when the company stopped being paid in advance in FY18), partly deliberate, and now larger than any single year suggests: trade working capital has grown from 21% to 44% of sales since FY19.

04Margins: seven years of improvement are treasury income; the operating gain is since FY23, and the Steriles margin is not in the accounts

Operating versus financial: what the margin bridge says, why gross margin rose, and which definition of EBITDA the company is using

Start with the definition, because the company and the pipeline mean different things by EBITDA. Caplin’s reported EBITDA for FY2026 is Rs 876.4 Cr, which the directors present as 38.1% of total income; it includes Rs 115.5 Cr of other income, almost all of it treasury (interest Rs 66.3 Cr, realised gains Rs 16.8 Cr, fair-value gains Rs 2.1 Cr, foreign-exchange gains Rs 18.6 Cr). The CFO said so at the 25 Sep 2026 AGM:

“Our reported EBITDA includes income from treasury; setting that aside, operating EBITDA grew by about 18% to ₹761 crores, and the operating margin improved from 33.4% to 34.8%.”

— D. Muralidharan, CFO and Whole-Time Director, 35th AGM, 25 Sep 2026

This report uses the 34.8% (operating profit before other income, Screener basis) throughout. The distinction matters in one exchange. On the Q2 FY2026 call an analyst asked whether a 40% operating margin is possible; the Chairman said “after two years, yes, it’s definitely possible” with a condition about commoditisation. On the company’s own definition the group is at 38.1% already, so the answer promises nothing; on the operating definition it would need 5 more points than Caplin has ever sustained (the best year was 36.2% in FY18).

Why gross margin rose from 54.9% to 60.4%

The company has promised and beaten a 55–56% gross margin since Nov 2020 (KEPT, beaten by four to five points; ledger section 10). The explanation given on the calls does not carry the weight put on it. The CFO said on the Q1 FY2025 call that Caplin Steriles had risen from 11% to 16% of revenue “of high margin” and that “that is also reflected in the gross margins going up from 55.3% to 59.6%”. But on the next two calls he gave Steriles’ own gross margin as roughly 55% (Q1 FY2025) and 63% (Q2 FY2025), against about 55% for the rest of the group (Q4 FY2023: 54.5–55%). Moving five points of revenue from a 55% business to a 55–63% business adds at most 0.4 point to the group. Backing Steriles out, the rest of the group’s gross margin rose from about 55% to about 60%.

“That is also reflected in the gross margins going up from 55.3% to 59.6%.”

— D. Muralidharan, CFO, Q1 FY2025 earnings call, 7 Aug 2024. He had just said Caplin Steriles contributed 16% of revenue, against 11% the year before.

The rest of the group’s gain therefore came from price, product mix and the exchange rate, none of which is quantified on any call. The Vice-Chairman’s letter in the FY2026 annual report claims Latin American gross margin rose from 67% to 71% and profit before tax from 42% to 45%, and says the company “could have brought a larger top line this year” and chose “to sell better products at better prices”. That is a single-source management claim. Its figures do not tie to the audited accounts: a 71% gross margin on most of the group cannot produce a 60.4% consolidated margin with the US business at 55–63%, and the audited Rest-of-World segment margin before tax is 36.4%, not 45%. What this section changes

The growth debate has been framed around the next 18 months. The record says the more useful reading is that every window has rolled by a year or more when it ended, that the long-range anchors (Rs 4,000–5,000 Cr revenue by FY29; FY22 revenue as FY28 profit) have been reduced or dropped without being formally withdrawn, and that the only unconditional growth statement left is 20–25% after FY2028–29. A valuation can use double digits to FY29; it cannot use the old anchors.

No credit rating of Caplin Point Laboratories was found. The phrase ‘credit rating’ in the FY2018–FY2026 reports refers only to the banks and counterparties with which cash is held (“held with banks with good credit rating”), and no call from Feb 2019 to Aug 2026 mentions a rating of the company by an agency. Borrowings are Rs 4.6 Cr, and sanctioned working-capital limits are Rs 52.1 Cr (parent) and Rs 61.0 Cr (group) against Rs 0.6 Cr drawn, so an unrated status is plausible, but that is an inference from negative evidence and not a statement by the company or an agency. This is a real gap in the evidence, because agencies are the one outside party that gets management access and is paid to write down what could go wrong. For Caplin we have to supply the agency’s questions ourselves, and the ones we would put are exactly those the calls leave untouched: the ageing of the Rs 849 Cr of receivables, the credit quality of a treasury book that is 41% NBFC and housing-finance debentures, concentration in Guatemala, the basis of the Caplin Steriles EBITDA, and the US regulatory exposure of a single site.

The one agency document: ICRA on Sunsole Solar

The returns on it so far are good. Which method we weight, and why

A DCF charges for working capital and a multiple silently assumes it away. Caplin’s defining feature is a trade working capital that grew from 21% to 44% of sales in seven years, so the DCF deserves more weight than the multiple would give it. But the DCF at 12% with 5% terminal growth is also demanding for a company with 33% operating returns on capital and no debt, whose peers trade at 29x EBITDA, and the discount rate that equates the base case to the price (9.6%) is not absurd for a dollar-linked cash generator. We therefore average the two weighted values and call the result the central value, Rs 2,019, with a range from the DCF-weighted Rs 1,548 to the exit-weighted Rs 2,491. The price is above the middle of the range and inside it.

Two cross-checks. On earnings, the base case implies FY28 profit after tax of about Rs 781 Cr (EPS about Rs 103, assuming other income of Rs 140 Cr), putting the price at 23 times FY28 earnings against 28 times FY26; that is not a lofty multiple for a 33%-return business, and it is why the exit-multiple method comes out near the price.
