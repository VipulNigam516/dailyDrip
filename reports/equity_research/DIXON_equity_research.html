Dixon Technologies (India) Ltd

India's largest electronics contract manufacturer: excellent at delivering volume, unproven at earning a better margin, and priced for a margin about three times the one it has reported for a decade

NSE/BSE: DIXON · BSE code: 540699 · ISIN: INE935N01020
Prepared: 13 October 2026 · Price: ₹12,660 · Market cap: ₹76,975 Cr · P/E: 37× trailing as reported (includes gains), 88× on FY26 profit ex-gain
The one-paragraph version
A high-quality operator on volume and balance sheet, a mediocre one on earnings quality and guidance, and a price that none of the DCF scenarios reaches

What the 18 calls show. Dixon has done what its contracts let it do: revenue rose from ₹12,192 Cr in FY23 to ₹48,873 Cr in FY26 (four times in three years), operating cash flow covered profit 1.33 times over the decade, and ICRA rates it [ICRA]AA (Stable). But of forty dated or quantified statements management made between May 2022 and July 2026, 9 were kept, 12 slipped, 14 broke and 5 are still unresolved. Everything that depended on a customer, a regulator or a partner slipped or broke; everything inside Dixon's own gates mostly held. Capex overshot its guide in every year tested, by 1.6 to 2.2 times.

What the numbers show. The FY23–FY26 pre-tax margin rose 141 bps, and none of it came from operations: EBITDA margin fell 44 bps while other income added 147 bps. That other income is largely a non-cash fair-value gain on a 6.5% stake in Aditya Infotech (₹460 Cr in FY25, confirmed in the annual report; about ₹690 Cr in FY26, inferred), which is 41% of FY26 reported profit. Production-linked incentives were ₹360 Cr, 19% of FY26 EBITDA, and mobile PLI-1 expired in March 2026. Q1 FY27 group EBITDA margin was 3.03% against 3.77% a year earlier, and management now says it does not expect improvement this year.

What the price needs. At ₹12,660 the DCF gives ₹1,845 (bear), ₹3,695 (base) and ₹8,130 (bull) per share. The reverse DCF says the price needs an 11.6% EBITDA margin for seven years at 15% growth (Dixon has never exceeded 5.2%), or a 35% revenue CAGR at a 4.3% margin. Even if management reaches its ₹1 lakh crore revenue ambition by FY30 at a 4.5% margin, the price still implies a 22× exit multiple on FY33 EBITDA.

Two-axis verdict. Quality: good business execution, thin and still-falling margin, earnings flattered by non-operating items, guidance credibility weak. Value: expensive on every method tried; the price is reached only by a 22× or higher exit multiple on the most aggressive case. Stance: do not buy at this price; a price near ₹6,500 (management's own case at a 15× exit) would start to pay for the plan. Monitorable triggers are in Section 15. This is analysis, not advice.

01The business: Dixon is paid a conversion charge on other people's bill of materials

What Dixon does, who it does it for, and why the revenue line says little about the profit line

Dixon is India's largest electronics manufacturing services (EMS) company. In Q1 FY27 it booked ₹15,557 Cr of revenue, 91% of it (₹14,179 Cr) in the “mobile and other EMS” segment. That segment is not only phones: management said in the same call that telecom products were about ₹2,100 Cr and IT hardware about ₹1,350 Cr of it, so smartphones, feature phones, hearables and camera modules together are roughly ₹10,700 Cr, or 69% of group revenue (derived).

The commercial model explains the thin margin. Management described it twice, six years of calls apart, in almost the same words. On the structure of revenue, the CFO said:

“the revenue is a function of the bill of material, the cost of goods sold plus our conversion charge. So if the bill of material... goes up because of increase in the memory prices. So accordingly, the revenue will go up.”

Saurabh Gupta, Director & Group CFO, Q4 FY26 call, 12 May 2026

On exposure to input prices, management said in October 2022 that in contract manufacturing “Dixon will not take any currency or commodity risk so that is immediately passed on”, and put that part of the business at “almost 80%” (Q2 FY23 call, 20 Oct 2022). ICRA's January 2026 rationale puts the pass-through (“prescriptive”) share at “~60–70% of business currently”, so the mix is moving toward the lower-margin side, which is also ICRA's reason for expecting the operating margin to “moderate to 3.5–3.7%”. A rupee of commodity inflation therefore raises revenue and lowers the reported margin without changing profit per unit: Q1 FY27 was the first quarter where that was visible, with smartphone volume down 22% year on year (7.5 mn against 9.6 mn) while the mobile segment's revenue rose 22% (₹14,179 Cr against ₹11,663 Cr).

Who the customers are

Dixon does not disclose customer shares. When asked in October 2025 for the share of revenue from anchor clients, management answered “That we generally don't, yes, we don't share” and then “That's confidential information” The best primary evidence is the rating agency:

“A major part of its revenues and operating profitability is derived from its top three customers — Motorola, Ismartu and Longcheer.”

ICRA rating rationale, Jan 2026, credit challenges

Ismartu (Transsion's Indian manufacturing arm: Tecno, Infinix, itel) is 50.1% owned by Dixon and 49.9% by Transsion, which is therefore a customer, a minority owner of a Dixon subsidiary and a partner at the same time (Section 12). Longcheer, an ODM, brings Oppo and Realme volumes and is moving into a 74:26 joint venture with Dixon. Vivo is the next anchor: a 51:49 JV that received its Press Note 3 approval on 8 July 2026 and is expected to add revenue from Q3 FY27. Motorola is the oldest and most contested relationship; what management has said about its share changed four times in two years (Section 09).

The group structure

ICRA's annexure lists sixteen consolidated entities. Mobile phones are made by Padget Electronics, a wholly owned subsidiary. Fully consolidated: Ismartu (50.1%), Kunshan Q Tech Microelectronics India (51%, camera modules, consolidated from 26 Sep 2025), Dixon Electro Appliances (51%, the Airtel telecom JV), Califonix (50%, control obtained in FY25). On a limited-consolidation basis only: Rexxam Dixon Electronics (40%, air-conditioner PCBs) and Lightanium Technologies (50%, the Signify lighting JV, started Aug 2025). Announced but not yet in ICRA's list: Longcheer (74:26), HKC (74:26, displays), Inventec (60:40, IT hardware), Vivo (51:49) and a Gemtek optical-transceiver JV (split not confirmed in the calls). Searching ICRA's list for Foxconn's Indian arm (Bharat FIH) finds nothing: the two companies are competitors in phone assembly, not partners.

Reading the business

Three things follow. Revenue is a function of customers' bills of material, so revenue growth and profit growth can diverge in either direction. The customer set is small enough that one contract decision moves the year. And a large share of the investment case rests on things that have not yet started: the Vivo JV (Q3 FY27), displays (trials Q3, commercial Q4 FY27), the Inventec JV (end Q3 or early Q4 FY27), the Longcheer JV, and the second mobile incentive scheme whose guidelines are still awaited.

Competition is real but poorly documented. A ValuePickr poster (31 Jan 2026) named Bhagwati Products, an unlisted contract manufacturer (Micromax's manufacturing arm, 49% bought by Huaqin), as growing 128% in a quarter when Dixon grew 3%, with customers including Oppo, Vivo and Motorola. This is a single forum source that this report could not verify, so it is treated as an allegation; Counterpoint's 2025 ranking, which put Dixon first among Indian smartphone makers with production up 89%, points the other way [medium confidence, secondary].

Why the Malik figure is low. Its denominator is the whole balance sheet. About 70% of Dixon's balance sheet is funded by liabilities that carry no interest (supplier credit and customer-linked payables: the float-funded share is 70% in FY26 and 78% in FY25, Section 05), so a ROCE that does not net them off measures the float, not the return. On a capital-employed basis (equity plus borrowings) Dixon's return is high. The honest reading is that ROCE is high because the business is financed by its suppliers, which is the working-capital finding of Section 05 seen from the other side, and ICRA says as much (“largely funded through supplier credit, resulting in a relatively high TOL/TNW ratio”).

The fair-value gain matters here too. On the module formula FY26 ROCE is 38.9% as reported and about 26.7% once the Aditya Infotech gain is stripped from other income (32.5% if the investment is also taken out of the denominator). The pipeline's tier does not change, but the 59% and 47% headline figures in the stored metrics overstate the operating return.

FY25 and FY26 profit include a mark-to-market gain

Dixon sold its 50% stake in the AIL Dixon security-camera JV to its partner Aditya Infotech in return for a 6.5% stake in Aditya Infotech (call of 30 Jul 2024). Aditya Infotech listed in August 2025. Dixon marks the stake to market each quarter through the P&L. The CFO described the first mark when it was unlisted:

“it's a notional gain only because we have taken a stake in that company, but that stake is an unlisted company right now which they have filed the DRHP”

Saurabh Gupta, CFO, Q2 FY25 call, 24 Oct 2024

FY25: confirmed in the annual report. Note 63 of the consolidated FY25 accounts records a fair-value gain of ₹459.98 Cr on the stake, taken as an exceptional item (the standalone accounts, note 54, show ₹489.5 Cr). It matches the two quarterly gains on the calls (₹210 Cr in Q2 and ₹250 Cr in Q4), and explains why other income jumped from ₹31.6 Cr in FY24 to ₹497 Cr in FY25: the gain plus about ₹37 Cr of ordinary other income. This closes a gap in the prior report, which could only say the jump was “plausibly” a gain.

FY26: inferred, not confirmed. The FY26 annual report is not in this pipeline's store. Reported PAT is ₹1,439 Cr and the company's adjusted PAT after minority interest is ₹845 Cr; the ₹594 Cr gap, grossed up for roughly 14% deferred tax, implies a pre-tax gain of about ₹690–695 Cr, which leaves about ₹40 Cr of ordinary other income out of the ₹734 Cr reported, the same as FY25. Asked about it on the Q2 FY26 call (17 Oct 2025), the CFO confirmed the quarterly mark: “we have to continuously now depending on how ... the market cap of that company behaves, we have to do it every quarter.” Treat the FY26 figure as an estimate (single-source inference from the reported-versus-adjusted gap).

The gain will return, and larger, in reported Q1 and Q2 FY27

Aditya Infotech traded at about ₹1,797 at 31 March 2026, about ₹3,645 at the end of June and ₹4,134 now (yfinance). If Dixon still holds all 73.06 lakh shares, the Q1 FY27 mark alone is about ₹1,350 Cr before tax. Management's Q1 call gave only ex-gain figures, so the reported number is not in the sources read; whether Dixon still holds the shares is unverified. Reported trailing earnings will look very strong in coming quarters, and yfinance's 37× trailing P/E already includes the first of it. This report values on the ex-gain figures and adds the stake at its after-tax value separately (Section 14).

Q1 FY27: revenue up 21%, profit down

Revenue was ₹15,557 Cr (Q1 FY26: ₹12,838 Cr). EBITDA excluding the gain was ₹472 Cr against ₹484 Cr, a margin of 3.03% against 3.77%. PAT after minority interest excluding the gain was ₹218 Cr against ₹280 Cr, down 22%. An analyst opened his question with the sentence that framed the quarter: “I know the profits are down Y-o-Y, but I think in a challenging quarter, you've done well” (Rahul Agarwal, Ikigai Asset, Q1 FY27 call). Management attributed the margin to the expiry of the first mobile incentive scheme in March 2026 and to higher selling prices from component inflation, which make percentage margins “optically lower”. The domestic smartphone market, by management's account, contracted 10–12%, and the call stated that “the domestic volumes are going to be kind of under pressure”.

04Margins: all of the pre-tax improvement is non-operating, and the promised expansion has been pushed out by about six quarters

A margin bridge, the production-linked incentive that flattered it, and a seven-call walk of what management said would happen next

Management has never claimed the business structure produces a high margin. The clearest statement came in July 2024:

“mobile assembly mobile manufacturing, it's a low-margin business. But it's an extremely high ROCE business. It's an extremely high asset to turnover business. Whereas when you venture into the component space, it's much more margin accretive, but the asset turnover is relatively lower.”

Atul Lall, Vice Chairman & MD, Q1 FY25 call, 30 Jul 2024

The strategic bet is therefore explicit: trade asset turns for margin by building components (displays, camera modules, mechanicals). The question this section tests is whether any margin has been delivered yet.

What would make this wrong

Part of the Q1 FY27 decline is arithmetic, not economics: if Dixon earns a fixed conversion charge per unit, higher memory prices raise revenue and lower the percentage. The call says so (“optically lower”), and mobile revenue per smartphone unit did rise sharply (segment revenue ex-telecom and IT up about 7% on volume down 22%, indicative only because the segment also holds feature phones, hearables and Q Tech). The disclosed data cannot split the 74 bps year-on-year fall between PLI expiry and bill-of-material inflation. The test is absolute: EBITDA ex-gain was ₹472 Cr against ₹484 Cr, so profit fell even if the percentage was partly optical.

05Working capital: negative because suppliers and customers are linked, which makes it a float rather than an efficiency

Net operating working capital went from +₹86 Cr to −₹1,020 Cr while sales rose twenty-fold; the cause is documented and the fragility is visible in Q1 FY27

The module's anomaly scan flags working capital as the largest movement in the series: net operating working capital fell from 3.5% of sales in FY17 to −2.1% in FY26 (−159% change). The three-year incremental working-capital intensity is −3.06%: every ₹100 of added sales has released about ₹3 of cash. A flag like this has to be traced to a cause before it is narrated; the cause is below.

The cause: back-to-back payables, stated by management and corroborated by ICRA

“In a prescriptive business or OEM business model clearly there is an understanding that when the customer will pay to the vendors only when the customer pays us. So there is a negative working capital. So for every receivable that you see there is correspondingly payable which is due there and that's the whole reason why both receivable and payable are looking on the higher side”

Saurabh Gupta, CFO, Q4 FY24 call, 15 May 2024 (answering Girish Achhipalia, Morgan Stanley)

ICRA, reading the same accounts from the outside, says the same in different words: Dixon “enters back-to-back payment arrangements with some of its suppliers, which are either a related party to its customers or are identified by the same”, creating “debtor and creditor for it from the same/related parties”, and “a part of DTIL's creditors for Xiaomi remains covered by bank guarantees (BGs) extended by the customers”. Two independent sources agree on the mechanism. They also agree on the consequence: the negative working capital reflects who bears the credit risk, not how fast Dixon collects. The annual reports add that two customers each held more than 10% of receivables at March 2024 and three at March 2025, which fits the concentration.

Promises on working capital, tested

Why the float matters

Non-debt liabilities fund 70% of the balance sheet in FY26 and 78% in FY25 (TOL/TNW 3.10 and 4.57 on the module's basis; ICRA's 3.3 for H1 FY26). That is good for returns and fragile in a downturn: payables must still be settled when orders slow. Q1 FY27 showed the mechanism in miniature, because a quarter of lower smartphone volumes coincided with an ₹800 Cr outflow while the supplier credit unwound. ICRA's adjusted net TOL/TNW, which nets back-to-back payables and customer-guaranteed creditors, is 1.0× at September 2025 (1.4× at March 2024), which is why it rates the structure comfortable (Section 10).

Contradiction between sources: ICRA's NWC is positive

ICRA writes of a “lean working capital cycle (NWC/OI of 0-3% over the last three years; 2.5% in FY2025)”, a positive number, while this pipeline's module computes net operating working capital at −0.4% of sales for FY25 and the company reports a cycle of −5 days. The definitions differ (ICRA's adjusts for items it treats as non-operating), and none of the sources gives the reconciliation. The direction of the conclusion is the same: working capital is small and well managed. The sign is not settled, so the −3.06% incremental intensity used in the valuation is shown with 0% and +3% sensitivities (Section 14).

Four narrative-management flags

1. Guidance that is later disowned

On the Q3 FY23 call (25 Jan 2023) a fund manager laid out the sequence: “In the beginning of the year, we started with Rs. 17,000 crores and kept on reducing it almost every quarter.” The MD's reply defined the problem rather than answered it:

“we are a B2B company. And we are closely associated with the budgeting exercise of our customers.”

Atul Lall, Vice Chairman & MD, Q3 FY23 call, 25 Jan 2023

Four months later an analyst pointed out that the FY24 mobile figure had gone from ₹8,000 Cr to ₹6,000 Cr. The answer: “So, I have not cut anything. I have not given any guidance for this year.” He then explained that the ₹6,000 Cr was the PLI ceiling, not a guide. That is a defensible distinction, but on the January call the same analyst had asked “you guided for Rs. 8,000 crores sales in mobile phones in FY '24, right?” and received “That's right.” In October 2025, asked to confirm a 40–45% growth guide for FY26: “First of all, we never give any guidance. We don't give any guidance.” Yet the same call confirmed a ₹1 lakh crore ambition and a 40–42 mn unit target. The ledger alone holds forty numeric statements, so management's practice is better described as giving targets while declining to be held to them.

2. Disclosure withdrawn when the numbers softened

From Q3 FY26 the telecom, IT hardware, hearables and Ismartu revenues were no longer reported separately. Asked why:

“No specific reason. This is a combined entity we look at and Ismartu is also nothing but mobile phone business only. So we decided to consolidate and show as part of the mobile business. In case you're looking for separate numbers, you can have a separate discussion. I'll give it to you.”

Management (answering Vipraw Srivastava, PhillipCapital), Q3 FY26 call, 29 Jan 2026

The sub-segment figures were available privately on request, which makes the call itself the less informative venue. In Q1 FY27 two of them (telecom about ₹2,100 Cr, IT hardware about ₹1,350 Cr) were given again, in answer to a question.

3. “Adjusted” and “optical” framing

From Q2 FY25 the headline figures on calls are PAT and EBITDA excluding the Aditya Infotech gain, and the Q2 FY26 quarter was presented on an “adjusted” basis (revenue ₹14,858 Cr, EBITDA ₹564 Cr). This is honest and helpful, since it separates the gain; the ValuePickr thread credits management for the disclosure (“To the management's credit, they have been voluntarily disclosing adjusted PAT figures”). The risk is the other direction: margin declines are repeatedly described as “optically lower” on bill-of-material inflation, which is true in part and cannot be tested from disclosed data (Section 04).

4. Questions declined on grounds of confidentiality

Anchor-customer share of revenue (“That's confidential information”, Oct 2025), the Vivo purchase consideration (“confidential”, Jan 2026), and the PLI-sharing arrangement (“commercially confidential”, Jan 2025). 11Capital allocation: capex always above plan, one very cheap acquisition, one costly one, and a free profit from an equity swap

Where the cash went, what it earned, and why asset turns fell from 17.7 to 10.3 times

Between FY17 and FY26 Dixon generated ₹5,040 Cr of operating cash flow, spent about ₹3,950 Cr on capex and ₹621 Cr on interest, paid ₹190 Cr of dividends (5% of reported profit, 6.8% of ex-gain profit) and raised no equity after the 2017 IPO. Employee options added some net worth, which ICRA notes. Every other rupee went to the balance sheet.

Capex: always above the plan, always ahead of the volume

Section 09 shows capex ran at 1.6 to 2.2 times its guide in each year tested (FY24 ₹569 Cr against ₹250–260 Cr, FY25 about ₹900 Cr against “lower than” ₹570 Cr, FY26 ₹1,058 Cr against ₹600–700 Cr). Management says explicitly that it builds ahead of contracts and that the contract compensates it for capex, not for margin. In May 2023 an analyst asked: “you are committing the capex in anticipation is there a fair amount of visibility” and the answer was “Yes, absolutely see otherwise no capex be.” (Q4 FY23 call, 23 May 2023). Asked about the anchor-customer contract by Pulkit Patni (Goldman Sachs) on the Q3 FY23 call (25 Jan 2023), management said that if volumes fall short the customer “compensates us for CAPEX investment and also certain fixed cost”, but that “you lose out on the profitability”. That is a fair protection for a plant, and none at all for margin.

Asset turns: the anomaly and its causes

Net fixed asset turnover (sales over net block plus capital work in progress) fell from 17.7 times in FY17 to 10.3 times in FY26, flagged by the module with the note to check it against margin for a subcontracting shift.

Three causes are sourced. First, capex ahead of volume, above. Second, mix: appliances, refrigerators (a ₹400 Cr plant with a four-year payback, Q1 FY24 call), lighting and components carry more fixed assets per rupee of sales than assembly (Atul Lall, Jul 2024: components are “much more margin accretive, but the asset turnover is relatively lower”, Section 04). Third, the mobile build-out itself. The ROCE comparison on the module's formula, which falls from 47.0% to 38.9% in FY26 and to about 26.7% without the gain, says the capital is earning less per rupee. Management's own ambition was higher:

“our aim of touching a 40% ROI is very much achievable, possible and we are committed”

Atul Lall, Q2 FY23 call, 20 Oct 2022

KEPT on the company's own basis (48.5% in FY25, 44.8% in FY26), though 34% in Q1 FY27 on the same basis, and not on the formula without the mark-to-market gain.

Interest cost: down as a share of sales, because suppliers lend

Interest fell from 0.63% of sales in FY17 to 0.28% in FY26. The causes are the scale of revenue against slow-growing borrowings (₹994 Cr in FY26, up from ₹43 Cr but on 20 times the sales) and the float described in Section 05: supplier credit pays for the working capital, so the cost of funding sits in the purchase price rather than the interest line. Interest also includes about ₹4 Cr a quarter of lease cost (Q3 FY23 call) and acquisition debt (Q2 FY25 call). Both are sourced to management; ICRA's coverage ratios (8.7× to 14.3×) are consistent with the low burden.

This pipeline's related-party audit covers eight annual reports (FY2018–FY2025), re-downloaded from BSE and read page by page (196 pages with related-party keywords). Five questions the formal record leaves open

1. The incentive receivable and an “overflow” claim in dispute

Note 11 to the FY26 accounts, as put to management by Pulkit Patni (Goldman Sachs) on 12 May 2026, shows about ₹1,100 Cr of incentive receivable against about ₹730 Cr payable to customers. The CFO explained the mismatch:

“Now the overflow money is still pending, which we are in discussions with the government.”

Saurabh Gupta, CFO, Q4 FY26 call, 12 May 2026

The scheme budget is shared among ten applicants, so a company that exceeds its ceiling can receive the unused share of those that underperform. Dixon has been paid up to its ceiling and is pursuing the overflow; the government has not yet settled it. “So the auditors felt right that there should be a note to it”, the CFO added. So part of the receivable (and of the booked PLI income) rests on a claim in discussion. The amount of overflow within the ₹360 Cr FY26 income is not disclosed. [single-source: one call, plus the note as quoted by an analyst.] The receivable is also long-dated: mobile claims had been received only up to September 2025 as of January 2026 (Q3 FY26 call).

2. Transsion is owner, customer and counterparty at once

Transsion owns 49.9% of Ismartu, which is Dixon's second-largest customer by ICRA's naming. It earns committed-PAT payouts from Dixon for three years: asked on 20 May 2025, management said “Ismartu has delivered on the committed PAT in this fiscal as per our agreement” and that there would be “further payouts for the next couple of years”. The annual report records Material-RPT shareholder approvals tied to the same arrangement. The structure is disclosed and arm's-length on the record, but it concentrates a customer, a minority owner and a payee in one counterparty, and the payout amount has not been quantified in any source read. The 49.9% minority also takes a share of Ismartu's profit that Dixon consolidates (minority interest was ₹64 Cr in Q4 FY25 alone).

3. The promoter stake: 28.55% on the register, 42% in the answer

Asked in October 2025 whether more selling by promoters should be expected, the MD answered:

“the combined shareholding of promoter and the family and myself is more than 42%. Second, we don't see any more dilutions happening, and that is it.”

Atul Lall, Vice Chairman & MD, Q2 FY26 call, 17 Oct 2025 (answering Rahul Agarwal, Ikigai)

The public shareholding pattern (Screener, September 2026) gives the promoter group 28.55%, down from 32.27% before the June 2025 block sale by the Vachani family. The difference of about 13 points is not explained in any source (it presumably counts holdings outside the formal promoter group such as the MD's own and family trusts). The question was about sales; the answer addressed dilution. Nothing in the 18 calls records a second question. Motilal Oswal Mutual Fund took 14.45 lakh shares (2.39%) in the June 2025 block. [Shareholding figures from the prior version of this report, not refreshed this cycle.]

4. What is shared on calls and what is not

Segment disclosure was reduced in January 2026, with separate numbers offered privately (Section 09), and the customer split, Vivo price and incentive-sharing terms are withheld as confidential. Withholding is legal; the effect is that the three facts that would test concentration risk are unavailable to outside investors.

5. The mark-to-market gain and the tax on it

The fair-value gain carries a deferred tax liability (“the auditors have kind of created a deferred tax liability”, Q2 FY25 call), so reported profit is after a tax that has not been paid and will be paid only on sale. If the stake is sold, the cash tax is real; if the price falls, the gain reverses through the P&L. The pipeline values the stake net of about 14% tax (₹2,600 Cr of ₹3,020 Cr) for that reason.

Read-through

None of the five is a red flag on present evidence. Together they say the accounting is orthodox and the economic substance (who earns what from whom) is only partly visible. The one that bears directly on profit is the incentive overflow; the one that bears on valuation is the Transsion payouts; the one that bears on ownership is the 13-point gap. All three can be closed by a short management disclosure.

What would make these results too harsh
