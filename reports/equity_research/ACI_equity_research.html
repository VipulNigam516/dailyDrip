The one-paragraph version
A low-cost brine franchise whose margin has halved on price, now funding a Rs 2,500 Cr semiconductor bet while its own promoter pledge, lease and tax position are unresolved: quality C, value stretched

Archean Chemical Industries makes industrial salt (70% of FY26 standalone revenue of Rs 1,042 Cr), liquid bromine (30%) and a trace of sulphate of potash from brine on leased land in the Rann of Kutch. It listed in November 2022, so the post-listing record is four years (FY23–FY26) plus one quarter: one peak and one downturn, which is a short cycle test. In that window consolidated operating margin before other income fell from 44.0% to 22.1% and profit after tax from Rs 383 Cr to Rs 105 Cr. The cause is price and volume of bromine (realisation Rs 379/kg to Rs 231/kg; 18.7 kt to 13.3 kt sold), which account for the whole Rs 400 Cr revenue loss, on a cost base in which freight is 41% of revenue. All six listed comparators also lost margin over the same window, but Archean lost the most (−19.0 points against −0.3 to −12.2) and gave up more in its worst year than the peer median (−9.1 against −3.5): we find no margin evidence of a moat, only of a low starting cost.

The core business passes all three cash gates (standalone FY18–FY26 cash conversion 1.79 times; Rs 810 Cr left after capex and interest). The group does not: in FY24–FY26 operating cash flow was Rs 695 Cr against Rs 854 Cr of capex, and the balance sheet went from Rs 336 Cr of net cash to Rs 344 Cr of net debt in two years. CRISIL cut the rating to A-/Stable on 21 October 2026 and expects debt to reach Rs 1,100–1,200 Cr by March 2027 and net debt to EBITDA 3.5–3.8 times. About Rs 980 Cr, roughly half of net worth, sits in ventures that earn nothing yet. We tested 50 dated statements from 15 calls: 8 kept, 8 slipped, 28 broken, 6 unresolved. The pattern is clear: management delivers what it controls (plants, a court order, a stock sale) and misses everything that needs a customer, the brine, a regulator or a government: promoter pledge “at about 3%, so I anticipate we should not be crossing more than 5%” in February 2025 against 24.5% by June 2026; “net debt-free” until November 2025.

Valuation leads with the reverse DCF: at ₹467.65 (enterprise value Rs 6,188 Cr) the operating business cannot justify the price at a 12% cost of capital at any margin up to 40%; the price implies a 7.9% return if the base case is delivered, or a 30% margin and 21% revenue growth for ten years. Scenario DCF per share: bear Rs 62, base Rs 166, bull Rs 282; exit-multiple cross-check on Rs 385 Cr of mid-cycle EBITDA at 8–16 times: Rs 271–520; ventures carried at cost add Rs 55. Our central value is about Rs 280, within a range of Rs 225–365. The value grade moves from stretched to fair at or below about Rs 365. This is not a buy or sell call.

01The business: three products from one leased brine field, 70% of revenue is salt, and the profit has come from bromine prices that are now 39% lower

What it sells, who buys it, what management says the moat is, and where the profit comes from

Archean Chemical Industries (Chennai head office; plant at Hajipir in the Great Rann of Kutch, Gujarat; incorporated 2009, commercial production from June 2015) extracts bromine, industrial salt and sulphate of potash (SOP) from sea brine in one integrated complex. Price ₹467.65 on 27 October 2026, market capitalisation Rs 5,783 Cr, 12.37 crore shares. Standalone FY26 revenue from operations was Rs 1,042 Cr: salt Rs 729 Cr (70%), bromine Rs 308 Cr (30%), SOP Rs 3.5 Cr. CRISIL's consolidated split for FY26 is 67% salt, 25% bromine and 7% bromine derivatives. About 70–75% of revenue is exported (CRISIL); all of the salt is, mostly to chlor-alkali and soda-ash makers in Japan, South Korea and China, and the largest salt customer, Sojitz Corporation of Japan, takes about 2.2 million tonnes a year, roughly 25% of company revenue and 50–55% of salt revenue (CRISIL, October 2026). Bromine goes to about 43 customers; the top ten customers are about 70% of revenue.

Two products, two economics. Salt is a volume commodity sold on 12–24 month fixed price and volume contracts, 92% free-on-board (Q2 FY25 call). Bromine is a price-taking specialty chemical: in FY23 it was half of revenue and, on our arithmetic, most of the profit; in FY26 it was 30% of revenue. The company does not report segment margins (“We are not doing segmental level EBITDA because there are a lot of costs which are shared”, Q3 FY23 call), so any statement of what each product earns is an inference.

What the moat is, in management’s words, and what it tests as

“I think there is no one who really sets the price.”

Executive Director, Q4 FY23 call (May 2023), on bromine pricing

“I would say it's not an established benchmark or index like the more commonly traded chemicals or hard goods.”

Same answer, same call

That is the central fact of the business: there is no benchmark price, so Archean sells at what its customers will pay, and the only defence is cost. On cost the evidence is real but old. CRISIL credits a low-cost “steaming out” process on Rann brine, a four-to-five-year gestation for brine fields and scarce suitable brine as entry barriers (every rationale from June 2023 to October 2026), and claims a 60–70% share of Indian bromine exports (company-sourced). Management also says the supply side is thin:

“I think the reason why we feel that bromine is well placed is because there are only a handful of manufacturers who do produce bromine.”

Managing Director, Q4 FY25 call (7 May 2025)

Three claims in this section should be read with a flag. First, the company's claim to be India's largest exporter and manufacturer sits beside the new Managing Director saying “During the first 9 months, India was a net importer of bromine” (Q3 FY26 call); both can be true, but the second is a statement about the domestic market, not a share. Second, the company-commissioned Frost & Sullivan report in the 2022 prospectus (RHP) puts Archean at 14 kt of about 32.5 kt of Indian merchant bromine in FY21, which is 43%, not 60–70%; the CRISIL figure refers to exports only. Third, the USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 credit India with only 7,000 t of bromine a year, against 13–19 kt reported by Archean alone; the USGS line is unusable for share work and we do not use it (section 16).

The plant, and the competition

Nameplate bromine capacity is stated as 42,500 tonnes a year (CRISIL, from January 2023; the RHP table shows 42.5 kt) but actual output has never exceeded 20,200 t (FY22) and was 13,263 t in FY26. On 6 February 2026 the Managing Director described the real near-term ceiling as 25,000 t and the Deputy GM Finance added “the capacity expansion will actually primarily happen between 25,000 and 42,000” (same call). Salt capacity is quoted as 4 Mt (FY26 MD&A), over 5 Mt (calls) and 7 Mtpa (CRISIL). We therefore do not compute utilisation from any single source. On competition: management names only global majors (ICL, Albemarle, Lanxess) and never an Indian rival; Indian merchant producers per the prospectus are Satyesh Brine Chem, Solaris ChemTech and Agrocel (all unlisted), and GHCL, a listed soda-ash maker, commenced 2,800 tpa of bromine on 12 October 2026 (Indian Chemical News, single-source). Asked about new domestic capacity in November 2024, management replied “The competition is bound to happen in any industry.”

Where the profit comes from

Salt volume and bromine price. CRISIL: realisations rose 2.0 times for bromine and 1.8 times for salt between FY18 and FY23, lifting revenue 3.3 times; bromine has since given back 39% from the FY23 level; the cost base did not fall with it. That asymmetry is the whole story of sections 3 and 4.

The cycle test is short

Archean listed in November 2022. There are no earnings calls before February 2023 (the first one calls itself “our first ever earnings call” and mis-dates the listing to November 2021), no audited FY22 accounts in readable form, and no ten-year record of the current capital structure. The post-IPO record is one boom year (FY23), three falling years and one quarter (Q1 FY27). Everything we say about resilience, management credibility and mid-cycle margin rests on that single cycle, and FY18–FY22 is the history of a highly levered borrower (debentures at 17%), not of today’s company.

Part one, FY2018–FY2020: a stressed borrower

The plant was commissioned in June 2015, ran late, and was restructured in March 2017 (Rs 673 Cr under the S4A scheme); debt service was delayed until November 2018 (CRISIL, June 2023). In November 2018 India Resurgence Fund (Bain Capital Credit and Piramal) subscribed Rs 840 Cr of debentures at an effective 17% to repay the banks (ICRA, March 2022 and 2023). Margins of 12–24%, net worth near zero, interest of Rs 100–120 Cr a year on sales of Rs 440–610 Cr: pre-tax loss in FY18 and FY20. ICRA’s debenture rating moved B+ (October 2019), BB- (January 2021), BB (March 2022), then BBB+ and withdrawal at redemption (March 2023).

Part two, FY2021–FY2022: the expansion pays

Bromine capacity rose from 10.5 kt to 28.5 kt (CRISIL June 2023). Operating margin went 24% to 35% to 41% as bromine moved above 45% of revenue and Chinese supply-chain disruption lifted prices; ICRA recorded bromine realisation of Rs 265/kg in FY21 and Rs 298/kg in the half to September 2021 (its report prints Rs/MT, a unit slip). FY22 sales were Rs 1,130 Cr and PAT Rs 189 Cr. Total outside liabilities to net worth were still 4.8 times and interest cover 2.9 times (ICRA).

Part three, FY2023: the peak and the IPO

The IPO in November 2022 raised Rs 805 Cr of fresh capital (plus an offer for sale by the financial investors and the promoter entity); the debentures were redeemed on 19 November 2022. FY23 revenue was Rs 1,441 Cr, operating margin 44.0%, PAT Rs 383 Cr, return on equity 45% (Q4 FY23 call). The Executive Director was explicit that the prices were temporary: “during my investor road shows last year, we had said that those prices were not sustainable, the $7-8 prices”.

Part four, FY2024–FY2026: three years of decline on a flat asset base

Revenue Rs 1,330 Cr, Rs 1,041 Cr, Rs 1,081 Cr (consolidated). The explanations given at each stage were different: destocking after the spike (FY24), Cyclone Asna, truck and berth shortages (FY25; Rs 40.2 Cr of salt stock lost), lower brine yield from erratic monsoon, then a 25% bromine volume fall and Kutch road works (FY26). Net worth grew only from Rs 1,706 Cr to Rs 1,998 Cr standalone while the group spent Rs 854 Cr of capex. Half-year FY27: only Q1 is out. Standalone revenue Rs 332 Cr (+14%), operating margin 21.4% before other income (25.3% on the company’s basis), consolidated PAT Rs 30.4 Cr and EPS Rs 2.48. The Q2 FY27 results and call are due in the second week of November 2026 and are not in this report.

Is there pricing power?

Not in the data. Bromine is priced at what the market bears and management says so; salt moved down 5–10% (“There is, I think, maybe a drop of 5%, 10%.”) while 3–9 month bromine contracts (CRISIL) or annual ones (calls) lag the spot price in both directions. Sensitivity on our own arithmetic: Rs 10/kg on 20 kt of bromine is Rs 20 Cr of operating profit (about 1.4 points of margin on Rs 1,425 Cr); a 5% change in salt price on 4.5 Mt is about Rs 39 Cr; a Rs 100/t change in salt freight is about Rs 45 Cr. The Q1 FY27 bromine price of Rs 319/kg is spike-assisted: the Managing Director expects realisations to “largely remain firm near the INR300 per kg level” but also reported that landed China prices had already fallen 30–40%. We use Rs 275/kg as mid-cycle in section 14.

The derivatives economics have not been shown to work

The Acume case was built on a three-times asset turn on Rs 250 Cr, or Rs 750 Cr of revenue at full run, with a 20–25% margin over market bromine cost (Q3 FY23, Q4 FY23, Q3 FY24 calls). FY26 revenue per kilogram was Rs 81 Cr over 5,300 t, about Rs 153/kg, below elemental bromine at Rs 231/kg, because clear brine fluids carry only 0.2–0.3 t of bromine per tonne. At that price 28,000 t of capacity would earn about Rs 430 Cr, a turn of 1.7 times, and the 18,000 t Phase 1 about Rs 275 Cr, 1.1 times; the promise needs about Rs 268/kg. The new Managing Director also said that higher bromine prices squeeze the segment, because contracts do not pass the cost through, and that it is “12 to 18 months behind”. At Mar 2026 Acume had a negative net worth of about Rs 46 Cr, Rs 131 Cr of loans from the parent and a Rs 134 Cr parent guarantee (AOC-1, note 35).

Market growth

The FY26 annual report cites IMARC for a global bromine market of US$3.8 bn growing about 4% a year to 2034 and an Indian market of US$119 mn in 2024 growing 4.1%; the Managing Director called it a low-single-digit to mid-single-digit growth sector on 3 August 2026. These are company-cited third-party estimates. If demand grows 4% and Archean’s output ambition is +15% a year, the difference has to come from price, share, or new uses (zinc-bromide storage is the only one with a name). On the supply side, ICL’s Dead Sea concession expires on 31 March 2030 and is to be re-tendered (Reuters, November 2025; medium); China’s benchmark bromine price rose 76% in the month to mid-April 2026 and was back to RMB 34,000 a tonne on 1 August (SunSirs, medium). GHCL’s new 2,800 tpa is about 21% of Archean’s FY26 output.

Ventures: not yet growth

SiCSem (Rs 2,500 Cr, 70% owned) has spent Rs 425 Cr on land and advances and has not started construction; production is guided 24–27 months from the fiscal support agreement of 11 May 2026 (mid-2028 at the earliest) and CRISIL expects no cash flow before FY29. The Offgrid 10 MWh pilot was inaugurated in August 2026 and Archean says it will be “several months before we can actually say” it is scalable. None of these contributes to FY27 or FY28 profit on any disclosed plan; they contribute capital employed and debt.

Which we weight, and why

We weight the DCF at 60% and the exit multiple at 40%. A DCF charges explicitly for the things this business has shown it cannot avoid: freight at 41% of revenue, working capital at 22% of incremental sales and routine capex at 8–9%; a multiple silently assumes them away.
