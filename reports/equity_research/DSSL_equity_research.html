The one-paragraph version
A real growth record and a record order book, but the profit is thinning into depreciation and interest, cash lags profit, and the one quarter of evidence since the stock peaked was flat

What the three calls show. Dynacons held its first earnings call on 16 February 2026 and has held two since (2 June, 14 August). Of 14 dated or quantified statements management made, 3 were kept, 4 slipped, 2 broke and 5 cannot yet be tested. The pattern is not a hit rate. Statements about the near-term margin (a “temporary blip”, back to “the current levels”) were kept. Statements about hardware costs (“currently do not see material pressures”, 16 Feb), about returns on the new asset base (“does not drop significantly”, 16 Feb, then “may moderate”, 14 Aug) and about growth measured year on year were reversed within three to six months. Three calls over six months cannot support a management scorecard in the usual sense; this one is a log of what was said, not a credibility score (Section 09).

What the numbers show. Q1 FY27 EBITDA rose 26% to ₹40.2 Cr while revenue fell 4.6%. Pre-tax profit rose 0.6%, from ₹26.3 Cr to ₹26.5 Cr, because depreciation (+₹6.6 Cr) and interest (+₹2.3 Cr) took 106% of the EBITDA gain. Since Q4 FY24 the EBITDA margin has risen from 6.9% to 12.8% and the pre-tax margin from 6.6% to 8.4%: more than two-thirds of the improvement is not in profit. An analyst put the cause on the 14 August call: lease rentals were “INR30 crores” last year against “INR11 crore” the year before and sit outside EBITDA. Across ten years cash flow from operations was 0.53 times profit, receivable days went from 68 to 154, and borrowings including leases went from ₹36 Cr (FY24) to ₹237 Cr (FY26) while ROCE on year-end capital fell from 41.9% to 24.9%.

What the price needs. At ₹1,029.65 the market enterprise value is ₹1,436 Cr. On the prior report’s own convention (reproduced to within ₹1 on five of its eight printed values; the printed bear and base do not reproduce) the bear, base and bull are ₹265, ₹381 and ₹901. On inputs updated for Q1 FY27 they are ₹251, ₹419 and ₹816. A reverse DCF needs a flat 16% EBITDA margin for seven years, against 10.25% in FY26 and 12.8% in the best quarter. An exit-multiple frame is kinder: the base case supports ₹751 at 8× and ₹910 at 10× EBITDA, and the price implies 11.5×. Those two frames disagree by a factor of two and the report does not blend them (Section 14).

Two-axis verdict. Quality: moderate and slipping on the measures that matter (returns on capital, cash, disclosure), with a clean related-party record and good counterparties. Value: not cheap on cash flow, cheap on multiples. Below about ₹560 (the base case at a 13% discount rate) the DCF case clears; above about ₹900 the price needs the bull path. Triggers with thresholds are in Section 15. This is analysis, not advice.

01The business: an IT infrastructure integrator that buys hardware, bills on milestones, and is now building an annuity book

Sources: three earnings calls (16 Feb, 2 Jun, 14 Aug 2026), CARE (Nov 2024), FY25 annual report, filings via news summaries

Dynacons Systems & Solutions (DSSL) was incorporated in 1995 and is run by its three founders: Shirish Anjaria (chairman and managing director), Parag Dalal and Dharmesh Anjaria (executive directors; Mr Anjaria is also CFO). It designs, installs and runs IT infrastructure for banks, public-sector bodies and large enterprises, and organises revenue into four lines: data centre and cloud, network and security, workplace solutions, and managed services. It does not make hardware. It buys servers, laptops and networking gear from OEMs (Dell, HP, Lenovo, Cisco) and from distributors (Redington, Rashi Peripherals, Ingram Micro), integrates them and bills the customer. Segment reporting reflects this: in Q1 FY27 System Integration was ₹310.6 Cr of ₹313.7 Cr revenue and workforce services ₹3.1 Cr (company filing, via a news summary). Cost of revenue was about 88% of FY26 sales (yfinance quarterly series, medium confidence), so gross profit is roughly ₹170 Cr on ₹1,424 Cr and EBITDA of ₹146 Cr is most of it. There were 1,013 permanent employees at FY25, about ₹1.25 Cr of revenue each.

“we are a pure-play system integration company”

Management, Q1 FY27 call, 14 August 2026, on why it is not comparable with Netweb, which designs and makes servers

How a contract turns into revenue, in management’s words

A typical order has a go-live phase (hardware supplied, installed, implemented) and then an operations and maintenance (O&M) phase. On the Q3 call management said the go-live phase runs “six months to 12 months” and that revenue up to go-live is booked immediately; the O&M phase “gets billed over a period of time, like it could be a quarterly billing over the next five years”. On the split: “around 60% of the project value is usually billed till go-live and the balance is usually O&M”, varying project to project. Customers pay on milestones while the company bills up front, which is the root of the receivable problem in Section 05.

The pivot: Device-as-a-Service and Core-Banking-as-a-Service

The new element is as-a-service contracts. In Core Banking as a Service, DSSL went live with 38 cooperative banks for NABARD in FY26 and said on 2 June that Haryana (20 banks) and Telangana banks were being added. In Device as a Service (DaaS) it supplies and manages end-user devices for a customer over five years (J&K Bank, about ₹75 Cr; LIC workplace, about ₹138 Cr). The assets sit on DSSL’s balance sheet: some bought, most leased. Asked directly on 2 June whether the model had changed, management said:

“A majority of the portion has been leased out as such, but as per the accounting standards we have you know taken them into our books as right to use assets there and recognized the corresponding lease liabilities.”

Management, Q4 FY26 call, 2 June 2026

The result is visible in the accounts: net block (property, plant, equipment and right-of-use assets) went from ₹9.2 Cr at March 2025 to ₹158.0 Cr at March 2026, and depreciation from ₹1.7 Cr to ₹14.5 Cr. Management said the annuity and managed-services share was “around 21% of our overall product mix” in February and has not restated it. The bet is that recurring contracts earn more per unit of revenue and are stickier. The cost is that the company now owns, or owes for, the assets that earn that revenue, which turns an asset-light integrator into a lender of equipment to banks (Section 11).

What the moat is, and how far to believe it

Management’s stated edge is pre-qualification: CMMI Level 5, ISO certifications, a track record on SD-WAN, private cloud and core banking, and a pan-India service footprint (CARE counts 11 branch offices and presence in over 300 locations). This is what lets it bid against larger companies:

“We are competing with larger, much larger Tier-1 and global SIs there.”

Management, Q1 FY27 call, 14 August 2026, on the move from a ₹350 Cr largest order to the ₹750 Cr RBI order

That is a self-comparative claim, recorded as a claim: no market share figure exists (management: “there is no market share numbers published there”), and rating agency CARE describes the opposite pressure: “high competitive intensity from other players in the industry which limits its pricing flexibility”. Management also avoids the segment where scale buys little margin:

“most of these have direct OEM engagements there, and the kind of margin profile that is available there is significantly lower there”

Management, Q1 FY27 call, on hyperscaler data-centre build-outs

02The checklist: growth and returns clear, cash does not

Computed with src.modules.equity_research on the Screener-basis series; the Malik framework page for this industry is not in the corpus (Section 16)

Growth. Revenue grew more than 10% in eight of the nine years from FY18 (FY20, at 5.8%, is the miss; the earlier report misplaced it in FY19. Cause not found: the FY20 annual report is not in the corpus). It grew 12.4% in FY26, the slowest year since, and fell 4.6% year on year in Q1 FY27, the first decline in the ten-year record and in the ten quarters in the database. Three-year CAGR to FY26 is 21.0%, five-year 26.7%. PAT went from ₹1.2 Cr (FY17) to ₹84.7 Cr (FY26), a 60% compound rate off a base so small that the base effect management itself invokes was not yet in play.

Returns. On the module’s year-end-capital basis ROCE exceeded 15% in eight of the nine years from FY18, peaked at 41.9% in FY24 and was 24.9% in FY26. The earlier report used average capital (Screener’s own 29.8% for FY26). Both fall; the choice of basis changes the level, not the direction. The incremental view is blunter: EBIT rose from ₹80.9 Cr (FY24) to ₹137.1 Cr (FY26), ₹56.2 Cr, on ₹358 Cr of extra capital employed, a pre-tax return of 15.7% (about 11.7% after 25.6% tax) on the money added in two years, against 40% on the base it was added to.

Gate 1 fails and Gate 3 is the one that moved

Over ten years the company converted 53 paise of each rupee of profit to operating cash. The best year was FY25 (0.91×), the worst FY26 (0.54×). Gate 3, which charges the company for its own debt, was negative over the decade and negative in FY26 on its own (−₹20.6 Cr). It is also incomplete: lease principal is neither capex nor operating cash flow, and an analyst reading the cash-flow statement said lease repayments were ₹30 Cr in FY26 and ₹11 Cr in FY25. On that figure FY26 cash after capex, interest and lease principal was about −₹51 Cr. Management did not contest the figure, so it is treated here as probable but unconfirmed until the FY26 cash-flow statement is read.

Asset turnover against margin

Net fixed asset turnover fell from 178 times (FY24) to 8.9 times (FY26). Rising turnover with falling margin would be Malik’s subcontracting tell; this is the reverse, a collapse in turnover with rising EBITDA margin, and it comes from capitalising devices and right-of-use assets, not from outsourcing. It is a change of business model, and the margin reading in Section 04 has to be made with it in mind.

Gross and net working capital disagree again

Management’s net working capital figure is 17 days (up from 14); CARE’s gross current asset days were 204 in FY24 and Acuité’s were 155 in FY25. The two measures are not in conflict arithmetically, because payables fund most of the receivables, but they describe different risks; Section 05 takes it up.

Three patterns. Revenue is lumpy by design: Q4 FY26 was ₹402.5 Cr (+22% y/y) and Q1 FY27 ₹313.7 Cr (−4.6%), a 22% sequential fall. EBITDA margin climbed in a near-straight line from 6.9% to 11.9% over eight quarters, broke once (Q4 FY26, 9.0%), and then reached its high of 12.8%. And the pre-tax line did not follow: Q4 FY26 pre-tax profit (₹25.4 Cr) was below Q2 FY26 (₹30.8 Cr) on 14% more revenue.

Asked on 2 June why PAT had not grown with a 22% revenue rise, management said the quarter’s margin dip was “short-term cost pressures and these are normal quarterly variations rather than any structural change in the business or accounting”; the analyst replied that gross margins were stable and the difference was finance cost and depreciation. Management’s answer to that was only that the PAT growth was “definitely there on account of those lease margins”. The quarterly series supports the analyst: depreciation plus interest rose from ₹6.4 Cr in Q1 FY26 to ₹15.3 Cr in Q1 FY27.

One year the record cannot show is a downturn. Revenue grew in every year including the FY21 pandemic year (+32.6%; the FY21 annual report says the company “swiftly returned to a sharp growth trajectory over the next nine months” after the lockdown disruption), margins never fell on an annual basis, and the first revenue decline came in a quarter. A ten-year record with no down-cycle cannot say how receivables, borrowings and margin behave when volumes shrink, which is when the 154-day collection period matters most.

04Margins: EBITDA is up 590 basis points in nine quarters; pre-tax margin is up 185

Margin bridge from margin_bridge; quarterly series from xbrl_quarterly; drivers from the Q3, Q4 and Q1 FY27 calls

The bridge: where the EBITDA gain went

Between FY24 and FY26 EBITDA margin rose 262 basis points, from 7.62% to 10.25%. Pre-tax margin rose 96 (7.03% to 8.00%): depreciation took 87, interest 76 and other income 3. Almost two-thirds of the gain went to the cost of the assets and borrowings behind the new business before it reached the pre-tax line. The same shape repeats inside a single year.

Q1 FY27: EBITDA up 26%, profit flat

EBITDA after lease repayments

On the 14 August call an analyst put the cash lease repayments from the cash-flow statement at “INR30 crores” last year (FY26) and “INR11 crore” the year before (FY25). Management did not dispute the figures and said: “fully classifying lease assets and removing them completely from this would not be a right measure to correlate the revenue”, and “We are not giving any specific differentiation between these two.” Taking the analyst’s figures as stated: FY26 EBITDA of ₹145.9 Cr less ₹30 Cr is ₹115.9 Cr, or 8.1% of sales; FY25 EBITDA of ₹105.4 Cr less ₹11 Cr is ₹94.4 Cr, or 7.4%. The margin improvement is 70 basis points, not 190. CARE’s Nov 2024 rating defines margin as “profit before interest, lease rentals, depreciation and taxation”, so even the FY24 figure of 7.62% was before lease rentals; the earlier years are not distorted by leases only because they were small.

What management says drives the margin, and what it does not say

Management’s explanation was the same on all three calls: “the improvement in EBITDA margins is a structural improvement which is driven by a richer solution mix and a operating level” (Q4 call). On the first call, asked for the margin profile by segment, it said data centre and cloud, network and security and managed services all carry a higher margin than workplace solutions, but declined to give any segment number. Asked on 2 June about the economics of the as-a-service deals, it offered only “these deals are definitely more margin accretive there is what I can say”. The analyst’s reply was the correct summary of the disclosure position:

“as an investor then it would be very difficult for us to judge this new deals that we are doing either you're not able to share the payback or ROCE or IRR neither you're willing to share any kind of gross margin understanding”

Ankush Agrawal, Surge Capital, Q4 FY26 call, 2 June 2026, to which management had no further answer

An analyst on the same call put the sequential gross margin at 18% falling to about 14% between Q3 and Q4; the figure is his, with an undefined basis, and is not in the database.

The Q4 FY26 dip: cause and corroboration

EBITDA margin fell from 11.9% to 9.0% in Q4 FY26 on 18% more revenue. Management attributed it to hardware cost escalation tied to AI-infrastructure demand: “the key risk definitely would be availability. First would be the supply chain”. Two checks. External: memory and GPU supply tightness is a documented 2026 condition (a March 2026 report cited by the research corpus put OEM memory deliveries at 50–66% of ordered volume; medium confidence, relayed through a secondary source). Peer: Orient Technologies, the listed integrator management called closest, saw EBITDA margin fall from 9.9% (FY24) to 2.7% (FY26), and Allied Digital from 12.2% to 7.5%. But the supply explanation is incomplete as an account of DSSL’s own result: on 2 June management also said a bid that exceeds the customer’s budget may be won at a lower margin:

“we may decide to win over the customer. So sometimes, because of these reasons also the short-term margin pressures can come in.”

Management, Q4 FY26 call, 2 June 2026

That is a pricing choice, not only a cost shock, and it cuts against the Feb 2026 statement that the company had “no hit on us” from component prices. The Q1 FY27 recovery to 12.8% is consistent with either reading; the unusual part is that it came with a 22% sequential revenue fall.

Is it the company or the industry?

Module attribute on FY24 to FY26 EBITDA margin changes: DSSL +2.6 points against peers of −7.3 (Orient), −4.7 (Allied Digital), −2.0 (Netweb), −0.6 (iValue), +0.2 (Rashi, Coforge), +2.7 (Inspirisys) and +5.8 (Silver Touch). Four of eight moved the same way, median −0.2: the verdict is company-specific, not an industry-wide margin rise. That is favourable on its face, but with the lease effect described above, part of DSSL’s gain may be accounting that peers with fewer leases do not show.

“But if the early payments don't result in giving cost efficiency then it would not benefit if I'm able to enjoy a credit period because most of the OEMs are supporting us in the projects”

That is a clear statement of the model: the company is paid late by banks and the public sector, pays its suppliers late, and keeps the spread. It works while the suppliers extend credit. Both rating agencies name the exposure: CARE’s tripwire was “Elongation in operating cycle driven by increase in collection days on a sustained basis” and Acuité (medium confidence, from its rating page) lists deterioration in the working-capital cycle as a negative trigger. Receivable days have risen from 126 to 154 since the Acuité review base year.

Can management’s 17 days be reproduced?

Partly. The earlier report could not reproduce it and said receivable days minus payable days on sales gave 40 days for FY26. Measuring payables on cost of revenue, which is the correct base for a purchases-driven balance, FY26 payables of ₹446 Cr are 130 days of the ₹1,255 Cr cost of revenue (yfinance quarterly sum, medium confidence; the analyst on the call computed 134). Receivable days 154 less payable days 130 is 24 days, close enough that average rather than year-end balances, or inclusion of customer advances, could bridge the gap to 17. The earlier report’s 40-day figure overstated the discrepancy; the 17 itself remains unverified until the FY26 annual report shows the definition. The direction (up from 14) agrees with the series.

The lease receivable question nobody followed up

On 16 February an analyst offered his reading of why receivables and payables both jumped in FY25:

“from FY'25 balance sheet has started showing large lease liabilities, receivables and payables. The payable part is understandable that you are buying certain devices on lease. But the receivable part would be broadly the revenue that is committed, annual revenue that is committed in this opex deals, right?”

Ankush Agrawal, Surge Capital, Q3 FY26 call, 16 February 2026; management answered “Yes.”

If so, part of the ₹602 Cr of trade receivables is committed future billing on as-a-service contracts rather than invoices overdue from customers, and part of the payables is the matching lease and supplier obligation. That would make 154 days partly a presentation of the contract book, not purely a collection problem. It also means receivable days are no longer comparable with FY21 to FY24. The split is in a note the stripped annual report does not contain (Section 16); nobody asked for it on either later call.

Cash: profit is not converting

Incremental working capital absorbed 25.6 paise of each extra rupee of sales over three years and 21.8 paise over five. At a 10.25% EBITDA margin, each rupee of new sales yields about 6 paise of after-tax operating profit (EBITDA less depreciation, taxed at 26%); the working capital it needs is about four times that. The valuation in Section 14 is built on this arithmetic. CARE also noted “sizeable bank guarantees to clients which further constrain the capital structure” and Acuité’s rated facilities include ₹237 Cr of bank-guarantee lines among ₹377 Cr. Contingent liabilities of ₹146.6 Cr at March 2025 were reported by a data aggregator (single medium-confidence source); the note and the allowance for expected credit losses are not in the corpus.

What the comparison does and does not say

The one close peer had a worse year. Orient Technologies is the only listed integrator with the same business: revenue ₹870 Cr (+4%), EBITDA ₹23 Cr against ₹74 Cr, PAT ₹4.6 Cr against ₹50.4 Cr. Netweb is the investor’s lazy comparison and management rejects it. A retail participant on 14 August noted Netweb’s 172% revenue growth and 14–15% operating margin and suggested vertical integration. Management: “Netweb is essentially designing and manufacturing their own servers”, an OEM that also bids direct, while DSSL is a pure-play integrator. Rashi and VDA. Management’s remark that a large distributor bought a systems integrator because of the margin profile means DSSL’s own suppliers are moving into its space. Asked whether rising component prices and working-capital needs thin out smaller bidders, management agreed that “the order sizes have increased there” and said it now competes with Tier-1 and global integrators. A consolidation in DSSL’s favour is plausible and untested.

07Growth: a record order book, a falling quarter, and no guidance

Management gives no revenue or margin guidance by policy; the only quantified forward numbers are the order book, the pipeline and an average execution time

Management declined every request for a number on every call. On the first: “As per policy, we are not giving revenue guidance there.” On the last, asked for a five-year vision: “Sorry, we're not providing any guidance in terms of any numbers there, whether it is current year or five years there.” followed by an invitation to extrapolate: “maybe you can look at extrapolating that over the next five years.” What it did say, repeatedly and in the same words, is that it expects the ten-year momentum to continue (“we expect to continue the growth momentum that you have seen over the last 10 years, we expect that to continue”, 14 Aug). That is a claim, not a guide, and the ledger in Section 09 marks it Unresolved.

What the order book does and does not promise

  • Coverage. ₹3,104 Cr is 2.2 times FY26 revenue, against 0.85 times at CARE’s August 2024 date. Management says it executes over “18 months to 24 months” on average, “from some immediate orders to be executed and some to be executed over a period of five years” (16 Feb). Even at the 18-month end, existing orders alone would support about ₹2,070 Cr of revenue a year, more than FY26’s ₹1,424 Cr, but the five-year O&M tails, including the RBI order’s, spread revenue thinly and a quarter of the book is one order.
  • Tie-out. The book was about ₹3,000 Cr on 30 May; named wins since then (NPCI, Central Bank, J&K Bank ERP) total about ₹418 Cr, and Q2 revenue to date takes some away. ₹3,104 Cr on 14 August is consistent with that if those three orders were booked after 30 May. The RBI order is reported inclusive of GST (₹750.82 Cr), about ₹636 Cr before 18% GST by the author’s arithmetic; the corpus does not say whether the order book is stated gross or net.
  • Pipeline. Management says bid conversion is “historically it is 30%”; applied to ₹6,650 Cr that is ₹2,000 Cr of future orders. Part of the pipeline growth is price: management said on 14 August that “when budgets are shooting up by let's say 30%, 50% there” customers prioritise. Bids priced on inflated component costs enlarge the rupee pipeline without adding units, and the 3,083 to 5,100 jump (Dec to May) coincides with the worst of the hardware squeeze. This is the author’s inference, not management’s.
  • Seasonality. None: “there is no seasonality or any particular quarter which is heavy” (16 Feb), although the Q4 FY26 revenue of ₹402.5 Cr was 18% above Q3.

The demand tailwind is real; its conversion is not measured

The external case is large and documented in this corpus at summary grade (aggregators, not primary documents): data-centre co-location capacity of about 1.5 GW in 2025, projected at 6.5 GW by 2030; the IndiaAI Mission’s ₹10,372 Cr budget with over 38,000 GPUs deployed by mid-2026; a Union Budget 2026-27 tax holiday for foreign cloud providers. DSSL’s own orders tie to it (the Central Bank order specified Blackwell and H200 GPU servers because the customer’s tender did: “whatever is mentioned in the RFP is what we mention out”). A caution in the same corpus: only about ₹400 Cr of the IndiaAI budget had been disbursed by April 2026 (one source, summary grade). Government headline numbers convert slowly, and DSSL’s largest customers are exactly that kind of buyer. Management itself said:

“While government spending can be cyclical, but we usually mitigate this, because we have a very diversified customer base”

Management, Q3 FY26 call, 16 February 2026

That is not quantified. The corpus has no split of revenue between public sector and BFSI, and management declined to give one: “the customer breakup would be similar to what ultimately, similar to what we are executing currently there where BFSI will be leading the space”.

The same supply squeeze that lifts bids defers deliveries

Management’s explanation for the Q1 FY27 revenue fall is lead times: “extended delivery lead-times on certain orders due to OEM and supply chain lead-time issues which have deferred execution and revenue recognition during the quarter”. Its framing of demand is more mixed than the headline: customers are moving ahead on mission-critical purchases, but for non-critical applications “they are dividing it into priorities there, so immediate priorities are taken up, others are pushed forward there”. Delay in customer purchase is deferred demand, not only deferred delivery. How much of the Q1 shortfall is each is unknowable from the corpus. The external view (summary grade) is that the packaging and memory gap narrows from roughly 20% to 10% by end-2026, which would help Q3 and Q4 FY27 more than Q2.

Base effect and the risks management names

Revenue growth has gone from a 27% five-year compound rate to 21% over three years to 12% in FY26 and −4.6% in the latest quarter. The CFO on 14 August did not contest it: “obviously with a higher base, the base effect will always catch up with all companies once the base increases over a period of time”. Asked on 2 June for the key risk, he named supply availability and added “with the amount of AI adoption there, we see that as a strong tailwind”, without quantifying either. A dollar-linked escalation clause exists in “some of the contracts” but not in all. On AI disruption of services revenue, a retail participant asked about Anthropic’s tools; management answered “These tools will help us in execution and help us improve our productivity”, adding that DSSL does not sell software as a service.

Expansion beyond India is sequenced: India first, then APAC (Southeast Asia), then the Middle East and Europe, addressed for now “through our partners”. The Cygeniq on AI-driven cybersecurity is the visible step. Nothing is quantified. Inorganic growth: “we continue to evaluate these opportunities as and when they come across”.

Pre-tax return on incremental capital employed over FY24 to FY26 was 15.7% (EBIT +₹56.2 Cr on capital employed +₹358 Cr). Against a 12.4% cost of borrowing (9.2% after tax) the spread is about 3 points before tax and 2.5 after, thin against the 15% return required on the equity-funded part. These are the numbers management declined to give for the as-a-service book alone; the total-company figure is the only one available, and it includes a base business earning far more. If devices are leased in and leased out, the lease out can be an operating lease (asset stays on the balance sheet) or a finance lease (the asset is replaced by a lease receivable). The earlier question about receivables (Section 05) implies some contract assets sit in receivables. The notes that settle it are not in the corpus.

The price at which the analysis changes: below about ₹560, the base case at a 13% discount rate, the cash-flow frame is satisfied without the bull path; above about ₹900, the price requires the bull path or an 11× exit.
