The one-paragraph version
Strong cash returns on almost no capital, but dollar revenue has been range-bound for six quarters, the FY26 margin loss is in cost lines nobody has explained, and the stock trades at the base case

What the two calls show. Management spoke to investors on 1 December 2023 and 9 February 2024 and has not done so since (Section 09). Of 16 dated or quantified statements, 3 were kept, 7 slipped, 3 broke and 3 cannot yet be tested. The kept ones were cheap and inside management’s control (a dividend every year, revenue per employee, the direction of the buyout). The ones that slipped or broke were about growth, hiring and call frequency: “closer to 10 to 11 million” of dollar revenue “for the next year” was reached one to two years late, “14 to 15 million” has not been reached, head count fell to 216 against a stated 450–500, and the promised “conference call at least half yearly” did not happen. Two calls cannot support a management scorecard; this one is a log, not a credibility score.

What the numbers show. Revenue of ₹95.6 Cr (+25.5%) looks like acceleration. In dollars it grew 20.1% in FY26 and 0.8% in Q1 FY27; quarterly dollar revenue has sat between US$2.4M and US$2.9M since Q4 FY25, while the rupee fell 10.6% against the dollar year on year in Q1 FY27 (Section 07). Operating margin fell 425 basis points in FY26, to 29.5%; 371 of them are in cost of service, consultancy and outsourcing lines and 147 are bad debts, while the India entity’s head count fell 15% (Section 04). The latest quarter is a recovery in margin (33.6%) and a distortion in profit: ₹8.97 Cr of other income, 54% of pre-tax profit, with no stated cause. Cash conversion is the real strength: operating cash flow of ₹30.5 Cr, capex of ₹0.2 Cr, and net cash of ₹56.8 Cr (Section 05).

What the price needs. At ₹46.22 the enterprise value is ₹418 Cr. The prior report’s valuation convention was recovered exactly (₹37.9, ₹28.9, ₹44.4 at ₹44.20). On the same convention with today’s price and net cash the bear, base and bull are ₹30.0, ₹39.0 and ₹45.5, so the stock sits at the bull case. A reverse DCF needs a flat 34.6% operating margin for ten years (FY26: 29.5%; FY24: 35.5%) or 18.2% revenue growth every year for ten years at today’s margin (Section 14). The exit-multiple frame is kinder: the base case supports ₹41.6 at 8× and ₹46.5 at 10× EBITDA, and the price implies 9.9×. The two frames bracket the price (DCF base 16% below it, a 10× exit equal to it), and neither is a margin of safety.

Two-axis verdict. Quality: strong on returns and cash, weak on disclosure. One entity books the revenue, the people who run it are the promoter’s son, daughter and son-in-law, the filings give no US/other split, no dollar revenue and no customer names, five directors left in nine months, and the earlier report misread this company on several points (below). Value: fair, not cheap. The base case is 16% below the price and equals it at a 13% discount rate. The analysis changes below about ₹39 (base case at 14.5%), or if dollar revenue grows again. Triggers with thresholds are in Section 15. This is analysis, not advice.

Thin evidence: read this before the rest

The listed history is short. The database holds ten fiscal years (FY17–FY26), but FY17–FY19 have revenue of ₹2–3 Cr and a missing operating-profit line, so the usable record is seven years (FY20–FY26). The company listed on the NSE SME platform on 19 October 2020 and moved to the mainboard on 9 June 2023: three years as a mainboard stock, no full cycle, and no year in which revenue fell. A cycle argument is not possible and none is made. Section 03 says what the seven years can and cannot bear.

The management record is two interactions. A 1 December 2023 management interaction and the Q3 FY24 earnings call of 9 February 2024. Screener’s archive lists three earlier calls (December 2022, May 2023, August 2023) that are not in the corpus and could not be retrieved; none exists after February 2024. Section 09 is scoped to what two calls can bear.

The filing set is better than the earlier report knew, and still incomplete. The FY2021–FY2025 annual reports in the corpus contain only the notice, the Directors’ report, the MD&A and the governance report: no financial statements and no notes (the earlier report said FY23–FY25 “converted cleanly”; they carry no tables at all). The FY2026 annual report (filed 31 August 2026) was retrieved from the NSE archive for this rewrite and does carry full statements and notes, which is where most of the new findings come from. FY24 and FY25 notes (ageing, cost lines, related parties) therefore exist only as the FY26 report’s comparatives.

What is new against the 14 September 2026 report

(1) The dividend was not frozen. The earlier report’s central capital-allocation claim, ₹0.50 a share for four straight years, compared ₹0.50 on a ₹10 share with ₹0.50 on the ₹1 share after the 10-for-1 split. The FY26 final is ten times the per-share rate: ₹5.14 Cr, 74% of standalone profit (Section 11). (2) Dollar revenue has stopped growing; the rupee averaged 84.5 to the dollar in FY25 and 94.7 in Q1 FY27 (Section 07). (3) The margin cause was found in the FY26 notes: cost of service +53%, consultancy +219%, bad debts doubled, staff down 15%. The earlier report called it unexplained and floated industry-wide AI pricing pressure; peers’ margins rose 1–9 points over the same two years while Sigma’s fell 6, so the module classifies it company-specific. (4) The ₹80–95 Cr “FY24 guidance” was mis-scored. An analyst proposed it for the next twelve months from December 2023, management said “Correct. Yes. Yes.”, and the same call said growth would stay “flattish” to March; management’s own number was US$10–11M (Section 09). (5) Net cash is ₹56.8 Cr, not ₹45.3 Cr: ₹16.6 Cr of bank deposits were omitted. (6) The buyout was priced from the subsidiary’s own accounts: about US$5.0M of cash left the group (Section 11). (7) The earlier report’s statement that the pipeline “runs to a few hundred thousand dollars” misread a monthly bookings figure ($250–300,000 a month, $3.6M a year). (8) Three gaps closed: Rish Info Logistics has revenue (₹0.55 Cr); the Sigma Accounting exit date is December 2024, not 2025; the SEBI order of 25 September 2025 and its ₹2 lakh penalty are confirmed in the company’s own filing. (9) Board change: four independents and one non-executive left between June 2025 and March 2026, the audit committee was rebuilt, and four of eight directors are now the promoter family. (10) Quarterly margin has recovered from 24.1% in Q1 FY26 to 33.6% in Q1 FY27 (31.7% in each quarter between); the earlier report described three straight years of decline.

01The business: an Ahmedabad cost centre that bills a US sales company owned by the same family

Sources: FY26 annual report (MD&A, Form AOC-1, notes 19, 27, 29), 1 Dec 2023 and 9 Feb 2024 transcripts

Sigma Solve Ltd was incorporated in Ahmedabad on 29 April 2010 by Prakash Parikh, raised ₹4.95 Cr in an NSE SME IPO on 19 October 2020, and moved to the NSE and BSE mainboards on 9 June 2023. It sells custom enterprise software, web and e-commerce development, analytics, CRM, QA automation and digital marketing to US customers. It is two companies. The Ahmedabad listed entity employs 216 permanent staff and does not sell to anyone outside the group. The FY26 report says so in its own words:

“Our Company do not have any identified customers being almost 100% of sales are booked by Sigma Solve Inc., and subsequently, our company ‘Sigma’ is charging on monthly basis to Sigma Solve Inc. for providing the execution facilities on per man hour basis in conjunction with efficiency level achieved on monthly basis.”

FY2026 annual report, MD&A, 18 August 2026 (identical wording in FY25 and FY24)

Sigma Solve Inc., in Florida, holds the customer contracts, does the selling and leads delivery. In FY26 it booked ₹95.07 Cr of turnover and ₹22.06 Cr of pre-tax profit (Form AOC-1); the listed entity billed it ₹38.52 Cr for hours and earned ₹9.42 Cr before tax. Consolidated, the US company is 70% of pre-tax profit (Section 03). Its US footprint, per the annual report, is “Florida (USA), Atlanta (Home Office), Australia (Home Office)”. The ownership is the point the earlier report built on and it holds: the US entity was a 51.04% subsidiary from 2019, 59.81% from FY22, and 100% from FY24 after it bought back the other 40.2% (Section 11).

The model in management’s words

“the main differentiator for us always has been that the promoters, we reside here in the US. It’s a true US presence and not a satellite office.”

Prerak Parikh, co-founder and CEO of Sigma Solve Inc., management interaction, 1 December 2023

The pitch is a hybrid: US-resident founders who sell and manage, an Ahmedabad team that builds, billing rates of “$20 to $40” against incumbents at “$100 to $150” (an analyst’s figures, accepted by management with “that still continues to be a driving factor”, and not verifiable from any filing). The services are described as enterprise software built on Microsoft and open-source stacks, AWS and process automation. On 9 February 2024 the verticals were named as logistics (about 7–8% of revenue), construction technology, process automation and a new Microsoft Dynamics practice; a dental-insurance platform and a customer-data platform were mentioned on the December call. The December call also claimed Inc. 5000 rankings for four years running, a Microsoft Azure AI partnership as a goal, and a “learning management system” and an insurance-adjudication model as AI examples. None of these appears in the FY25 or FY26 annual reports. They are management claims, not corroborated ones.

What management says it is not:

“We don’t want to be a body shop business. That has been very clear from day 1 for us.”

Prerak Parikh, Q3 FY24 earnings call, 9 February 2024

The same call supplies the sceptical reading. Asked about generative AI, the same speaker said “if we look at ourselves as a company that provides software developers, then that’s a problem. Because that is replaceable, or at least portion of the effort is replaceable through AI”. The intercompany contract is billed per man-hour, the India entity’s only revenue is hours, and the group’s revenue line in the FY26 notes is a single item, “Service Fees”, with no product or subscription revenue at all. Asked about the SaaS products, management answered “It’s all perpetual licenses” and said “we just don’t have anything that we sell on a per user per month basis”. On the evidence in the filings this is an hours-based services business with a good sales channel, not a platform.

Who the customers are, and what is not disclosed

The company discloses less about its customers than any of the other small IT exporters in this pipeline. What exists: 100% of revenue is from outside India (note 29: India nil, “Others” ₹95.62 Cr); two customers account for 10% or more of revenue, together ₹22.56 Cr or 23.6% in FY26 (24.7% in FY25); client retention “over 90%”; and, from the calls, a top five of 35–40% and a top ten of 65–70%. What is not: the US share (the call mentions Canadian customers and Europe prospecting; the report says only “export”), any customer name, any industry split after the February 2024 slide, and any dollar figure. In February 2024 the biggest customer was put at “about 9 to 10%” and the second at “about 8%”; the FY26 note has both at 10% or more, so concentration at the top has risen against what the call implied (Section 09, claims table).

Currency is the other undisclosed driver. All revenue is in dollars and most costs are in rupees; the group holds no forward contracts, and the FY25 governance report still says the company “does not have any exposure of foreign exchange”. FY26 carried a net foreign-exchange gain of ₹0.94 Cr in other income and a ₹4.11 Cr translation gain in other comprehensive income. Section 07 shows how much of reported growth that is.

Why the structure matters for every number that follows

The India entity earns a cost-plus margin (24.5% pre-tax in FY26, up from 20.4% in FY24) on hours billed to the family-run US company. The US company carries the customer risk, the bad debts, the third-party delivery costs and the sales spend, and its pre-tax margin fell from about 30% to 23% over the same two years. A buyer owns 100% of both, so the transfer price does not move the consolidated total, but the consolidated numbers hide where the margin loss happened and who manages it.

What the checklist does not catch

The first five rows describe a company that, mechanically, looks like a clean compounder, and in cash terms it is. They would also have been true of the version of this company in the earlier report, which spent most of its pages on a margin it called unexplained and a dividend it called frozen. Both of those conclusions were wrong or stale (Sections 04 and 11). What the record shows, year by year

FY23 was the peak, and the next two years were a pause. Operating margin hit 40.5% and receivable days 35. Asked on 1 December 2023 why growth had gone “flattish” in the quarter, Prerak Parikh blamed the customers, not the company:

“I think the predominant reason is the slowdown in the economy, especially, let’s say, even around logistics. There were a lot of, let’s say like Morse, for instance, Morse laid off 10,000 employees.” … “So economy is the only reason right now for that flattish growth.”

Management (Prerak Parikh, answering Deepak Poddar; the conversion drops the speaker label), interaction of 1 December 2023. The transcript spells the logistics firm “Morse”, probably a transcription of Maersk; the 10,000 figure is the speaker’s

That reading is half-corroborated: Q3 and Q4 FY24 revenue were identical at about ₹16.2 Cr, and FY24 growth was 16.5%. It is also single-source (management, on one call) and was not tested on a later call, because there was none. The December answer “nothing really has changed on our end” is what the later cost notes put in doubt (Section 04).

The FY24 profit story had a presentation effect inside it. On 9 February 2024 management said the profit after tax attributable to owners “grew by 59% to rupees 44 million year over year as a result of our successful group simplification”. Group simplification means removing the minority share: FY24 consolidated profit was ₹19.33 Cr, and ₹3.38 Cr of it (17%) belonged to the former minority (FY25 report). The next year’s total profit was ₹19.09 Cr, 1.2% lower, while profit to owners rose 18% (₹16.14 Cr to ₹19.09 Cr). The pre-minority series in the table (FY25 and FY26) therefore looks flat for FY24–FY25 while EPS rose.

Management’s EBITDA includes other income. The 9 February call gave nine-month EBITDA of “Rs. 203 million” and a margin “maintained at 42%”. The database operating profit for the same nine months is about ₹17.1 Cr (35%); the difference, ₹3.2 Cr, is nine-month other income. The FY25 and FY26 reports repeat the construction: “EBITDA (before other income)” of 34.46% and 32.40% equals profit before depreciation, interest and tax after other income. The like-for-like operating margins are 33.9% and 29.5%. Anyone comparing the company’s headline margin with Screener’s, or with the analyst’s 35–40% on the December call, is comparing two different things.

FY26 was the strongest year in rupees and an ordinary one in dollars. Revenue, profit and cash flow all rose; the dollar series says the rise ended in Q4 FY25 (Section 07). Trailing four-quarter PAT is ₹31.3 Cr on pre-tax profit of ₹41.3 Cr, of which ₹11.4 Cr (28%) is other income and ₹8.97 Cr arrived in a single quarter.

Reading the cost lines

Third-party delivery costs took 371 basis points. Cost of service (+₹4.99 Cr), consultancy (+₹1.18 Cr) and outsourcing (+₹0.48 Cr) together rose ₹6.65 Cr, 34% of the ₹19.4 Cr increase in revenue. Over the same year the India entity’s permanent head count fell from 254 to 216 and group staff cost grew 24.8%, in line with revenue. The incremental delivery capacity was bought, not hired. That is Malik’s subcontracting pattern (asset turnover up, margin down; Section 02), and the module flags it for the same reason. The December 2023 call anticipated the first half of it:

“for us to even get to these kinds of top lines and continue to grow, we also have to get into higher end technologies. And when you get into those, the cost goes up, but at the same time, what you can demand a premium for those services as well.”

Prerak Parikh, management interaction, 1 December 2023, on whether the 35–40% margin was sustainable

The cost went up; the premium did not show in the margin. The same answer said that wage inflation had “levelled out”, so “our margins are going to be a little better”. Staff cost per employee is not available (US staff are not counted in the 216), but staff cost as a share of revenue was 40.5% in FY25 and 40.3% in FY26: wages were not the problem. The pressure came from the lines management did not mention.

Bad debts took 147 basis points. The FY26 charge is ₹4.09 Cr (4.3% of revenue), up from ₹2.14 Cr (2.8%). In both years the charge equals the year-end balance of credit-impaired receivables, fully provided (note 9), which means either the allowance is rebuilt from nothing every year or the P&L line is mislabelled; the filings do not say which, and no customer, geography or cause is named. On 1 December 2023 management was asked directly whether the weak funding environment was causing receivable problems:

“Not as of now. We’ve not had anybody to date that says we are folding up or we are filing bankruptcy. We’ve not had a single customer as of yet.”

Management (Prerak Parikh), management interaction, 1 December 2023, to Lalit Rai; same answer: “I’d rather not work with startups right now”

The statement was about bankruptcies, and nothing in the filings contradicts it literally. Two years later the allowance is ₹4.09 Cr. The explanation is single-source (management, December 2023) and was not tested on the next call, in which nobody asked about receivables.

The loss is in the US entity. Its pre-tax margin fell from about 30% (FY24) to 23.2% (FY26) while the India entity’s rose from 20.4% to 24.5% (table in Section 01). The cost of service, consultancy, brokerage and bad-debt lines all sit where the customers are. Brokerage expense (₹1.39 Cr) and consultancy are consistent with the US sales build described on 9 February 2024 (“a head of sales”, four sales staff, two openings, lead-generation agencies and a strategic advisor), but no filing links them; it is a candidate, not a finding.

Is it the company or the industry?

That does not exonerate or condemn management; it removes an excuse. Management itself offered AI as a reason to stay in solutions rather than staffing (“If anything, it has a positive impact because you are delivering your solutions to a customer who is not that tech savvy”, 9 February 2024), and nobody has since said that AI pricing pressure cost the company margin. The earlier report’s pricing-pressure story was the author’s hypothesis, not a source.

The resilience test

The sector’s stress year for discretionary technology spending was FY24 (the 1 December 2023 call itself blames “the slowdown in the economy”). In that year Sigma Solve’s operating margin fell 5.1 points, the largest single-year drop in the set: InfoBeans lost 4.0, Trigyn 1.6, and Silver Touch, Saksoft, Mindteck and Kellton gained between 0.6 and 20 points (module resilience, peers on Yahoo data). A moat in a cyclical industry is the ability to give up less than competitors when conditions turn. Management called the margin “sustainable” because of a premium for higher-end work; the observable record, in the sector’s stress year, is that it gave up more than all but one peer. The single-year result, the peers’ different margin definitions and a listing history of six years make this a signal, not a verdict.

The currency tailwind that did not arrive

The pipeline’s industry brief quotes a sell-side rule of thumb that a 1% rupee depreciation lifts a rupee-cost, dollar-revenue exporter’s margin by about 30 basis points. The rupee averaged 84.5 in FY25 and 88.3 in FY26 (+4.5%) and 94.7 in Q1 FY27 (+10.6% on Q1 FY26). Sigma Solve’s margin fell through the first of those. Its cost base is not all rupees (the US entity’s cost of service, brokerage and consultancy are presumably dollar costs; the filings do not say), so the rule overstates the benefit, but the absence of any visible effect is one more sign that the margin loss is in the cost lines of Section 04 and not in macro.
