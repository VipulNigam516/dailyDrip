Verdict

Quality: a clean, debt-free, cash-rich franchise whose growth engine — net new paying suppliers — has not worked for thirteen quarters, and whose management has said so on the record every quarter. Value: fair, not obviously cheap — the price sits at the 6%-growth bear case if you treat the customer float as equity, and at the 13%-growth base case if you do not.

This is a full rewrite, not an update. The previous version of this report was built mainly on news, ValuePickr and the call headlines; this one reads all 18 calls (Q4 FY22 → Q1 FY27, April 2022 – July 2026): twelve in full, six from prepared remarks plus targeted passages. It builds a 31-line promise ledger scored against later calls, and it overturns four things the earlier version stated. The first net quarterly decline in paying suppliers was Q3 FY25 (−3,500), not Q1 FY27. The “33%” that was scored as a kept margin target was a collections-growth aspiration from April 2024. The words “no B2B threat” do not appear in any transcript; what the CEO said on 30 April 2026 was hedged. FY2025 dividends were ₹50 a share, not ₹20 — and the ₹3,860 Cr of net cash the old valuation added back was a pre-dividend March figure set against a post-dividend price.

The business is as clean as the old report said, and more precisely so. Operating cash flow has been 82–92% of “cash EBITDA” (EBITDA plus the build in deferred revenue) every year; Gold and Platinum customers, half the base and over 75% of revenue, lose ~1% a month; the related-party audit scores 1 of 3 across eight years. But the engine behind all of that has stalled and management’s own words trace the stall: Silver-monthly churn of 7% a month has sat in a 6–8% band on every call since October 2023 (it was 5% in April 2022); standalone collections growth has been 8–10% in six of the last seven quarters against a stated “20%… definitely” (Oct 2022); unique enquiries have fallen from 31M to 26M in four quarters while paid advertising rose. Margin (34–35%) is a harvest margin: management says it falls to “33% to 35%” once it “push[es] the pedal on gross adds.”

On value, the answer depends on a modelling choice, not on a view about growth. Reverse DCF at the current price needs only 5.1% revenue growth at a 32% EBITDA margin if the prepaid float is counted as permanent funding — but 12.4% if no credit is taken for float growth and deferred revenue (₹2,014 Cr) is treated as debt-like, which is how management itself describes it. Scenario values run ₹993 (severe) to ₹5,047 (bull) on the generous lens and ₹648 to ₹2,825 on the strict one; the probability-weighted range is ₹1,300–2,100 around a ₹1,592 price. Quality grade B+; value grade C+ (fair). Specific triggers, with thresholds and dates, are in Section 14.

01The business: a prepaid subscription float with a treasury attached

Two tiers of customer, one float, one second business — and the moat as management describes it, in its own words

IndiaMART runs India’s largest online B2B lead-generation platform. Buyers post enquiries for free; suppliers pay a subscription — Silver (monthly or annual), Gold, Platinum and the TrustSEAL verified tier — for the right to be found and to answer them. The annual report states the accounting plainly: “Subscription fees are collected upfront, irrespective of tenure. Revenue is recognised over the subscription period… The unearned portion is recorded as Deferred Revenue” (FY2026 annual report, Management Discussion). At 30 June 2026 there were 2.18 lakh paying suppliers, ~26 million unique business enquiries a quarter, ~40 million buyers active in twelve months, and consolidated deferred revenue of ₹2,014 Cr — 1.28× a full year’s revenue.

The company is explicit that it is not a marketplace in the transacting sense. Asked on 30 April 2026 whether it tracks enquiry-to-purchase closure, the CEO answered: “we are mostly advertising and referral platform. We are not a transaction and fulfilment platform. So we are intermediary rather than being a marketplace.” That framing matters twice: it is why there is no take-rate and no GMV to report, and it is the legal footing of the safe-harbour defence in the litigation cluster (Section 11).

Two businesses on one P&L: a pyramid and a long tail

The revenue base is a steep pyramid, and management has disclosed enough to size it. Gold and Platinum are ~50% of paying customers and “more than 75% of the revenue” (opening remarks, every call since 2023). The top 10% of customers — ~22,000 — are ~50% of revenue (49% in January 2025); the top 1%, about 2,200 customers, pay an average ₹11 lakh a year, with the largest above ₹50 lakh (Q3 FY26, 20 Jan 2026). Overall revenue per paying supplier was ~₹32,000 in FY17, ~₹42,000 pre-COVID, ₹53,000 in FY24 and “60–65,000” by October 2025. At the other end, Silver-monthly customers were 27% of customers and 18% of revenue in October 2022. The CEO’s own cash-flow framing of the same split: Gold and Platinum “will typically drive anywhere between 50 to 75% of the revenue and maybe more than 100% of the cash flow” (Q2 FY24, 27 Oct 2023) — Silver is the funnel and the leadership cost, not the profit pool.

“Gold and platinum accounts for about 50% of our total customer base and more than 75% of our revenue now… as the number of customers have not grown, the percentage of gold and platinum revenue contribution is actually inching up quarter-on-quarter.”

Dinesh Chandra Agarwal, CEO — Q4 FY25 earnings call, 29 April 2025 (the mix shifts up because the denominator has stopped growing, not because the tiers have re-priced)

Other mix points management has given in passing, all from the calls: new sales came a third each from field channel partners, own tele-sales and outsourced sales in April 2022; 50% from channel partners by October 2022 (80% field-force pre-COVID); ~40% in April 2024 after cutting Tier-4 telesales centres. Metro cities were 54% of paying suppliers (Apr 2023, 56% before). Export-oriented collections are ₹60–70 Cr (Apr 2026), up from ₹20–25 Cr when the business was purely domestic. Free suppliers receive “less than 20%” of enquiries (Jan 2025), and single-lead purchases at ₹299 and up bring ~₹4 Cr a year of collections. Headcount was 6,222 at March 2026 (4,088 in September 2022), and the annual report records that median employee remuneration fell 2.17% in FY26.

The second business and the thirteen stakes

BUSY Infotech — accounting software bought 100% for ₹500 Cr in April 2022 — is the only acquisition large enough to show in the consolidated figures: Q1 FY27 revenue ₹36 Cr (8.7% of the group), billing ₹59 Cr, deferred revenue ₹146 Cr (+44%), 4.54 lakh licences sold in total, FY26 operating cash flow ₹49 Cr (Q4 FY26 call). Livekeeping (66%), Vyapar (29%), Bizom (31–32%), SuperProcure, Fleetx (25.8% after the July 2026 follow-on) and others form the “ecosystem”; management puts it at “almost ₹750-odd crores across 13 different investments” with six above ₹100 Cr turnover (Prateek Chandra, CSO, Q4 FY26). Section 10 prices all of this.

The moat, as management itself describes it

Asked in October 2025 whether AI scraping dilutes the platform’s advantage, the CEO set out three moats and conceded the first one is weak:

“There are 2-3-4 kinds of data. Some data is just the name address, some of the data is phone number and e-mail and some of the data is the catalogue… phone and e-mail we don’t provide anywhere on the web. So hard to scrap… The second part of the network effect or the barrier to entry is the insight that we have on which supplier likes what… The third part is the paying customer service that we have built… So these are the three moats. Out of that, the only first one… was always scrapable anyway.”

Dinesh Chandra Agarwal, CEO — Q2 FY26 earnings call, 17 October 2025

Two of the three moats — supplier-preference insight and a ~2-lakh paying base responding to ~10 million enquiries a month — are exactly the assets that erode if paying suppliers fall, which is why the net-add record in Section 07 matters more than the profit figures. The third claim to read sceptically is scale: the CEO says IndiaMART had “60% market share at the time of the IPO. Today, we might be having much higher” (Q2 FY24) — the only evidence offered is that the two nearest private rivals’ annual returns and traffic are tracked. A company-asserted share, uncorroborated here.

What each year was, in management’s own framing

FY22 — the recovery year. “We also witnessed our highest ever Net customer addition… 13,000 paying subscribers… The coming year will be the year of investment for us as we expect to add 8,000 to 9,000 net paying customers every quarter” (CEO, Q4 FY22, 29 Apr 2022). Collections grew 31% off a COVID-depressed base, ~20,000 customers lost across two waves had partly returned, and two-thirds of new customers came in at Silver-monthly. That is the seed of the later churn problem: the cohort that was added fastest is the cohort that leaves fastest, a link the CEO made himself on 28 April 2023 (“the early churn is always high”).

FY23 — investment and BUSY. Headcount rose from ~3,700 to 4,583; EBITDA fell from 41% to 27% as management chose growth over margin (“we expect our margins to remain subdued as we continue to make investments for the growth ahead”, CFO, Q4 FY22). The year delivered what it promised: ~33,000 net adds, collections +30%. The cost was a second-order one — a customer base whose first-year cohort was unusually large.

FY24 — the price restoration and the first miss. On 15 May 2023 the Silver entry price was restored to pre-COVID levels (₹2,500 → ₹3,000 a month). Q1 FY24 net adds were 5,000 against a guidance of 7–8k; by Q2 they were 2,000 and the guidance was withdrawn (“we will come back with the guidance on the net customer addition”, Q2 FY24). A ₹500 Cr buyback in August 2023 cost ₹620 Cr all-in. Collections +21%, still above the 20% line.

FY25 — the stall gets a name. Standalone collections growth fell to 5% in Q2 (Oct 2024); the CEO’s answer was candid: “even we have been a little surprised negatively on only 5%, 6% collection growth… until and unless we fix the churn position structurally, I think we must not anticipate more than 10%, 15% of the collection growth”. Net suppliers fell 3,500 in Q3. The margin rose to ~39% (standalone) — for the reason management gives every quarter: “savings arising from lower customer acquisition and operating leverage.” Dividends were ₹50 a share.

FY26 — harvest, a price rise and an AI story. Collections +14% consolidated (+10% standalone, the difference being BUSY’s reclassified partner payouts), revenue +13%, EBITDA 34%. The Silver price was raised in September 2025 (monthly plan ₹3,000 → ₹4,000, Q3 FY26 call) and net adds turned negative for the next three quarters. Other income swung from ₹92 Cr to ₹10 Cr to ₹135 Cr to −₹34 Cr. Dividend ₹60.

The last figure is the record seen from the market’s side. The same business that traded at 84× trailing earnings in March 2021 (year-end price on the bonus-adjusted series) and 54× in March 2023 now trades at 20×; profit per share has risen, price per share has not. That de-rating tracks the loss of the growth narrative, not a change in the cash quality documented in Section 05, and it matters for valuation because the “quality compounder” multiple the stock once carried is no longer in the price.

04Margins: real, but earned by not spending

Where the margin came from, what management says holds it up, and what it costs to restart growth

Read in isolation this is a better result than most companies on this pipeline produce: the PBT margin gain from FY24 to FY26 came from the operating line (+598bp), not from falling interest or depreciation, and other income actually subtracted 444bp. The question is how the operating line was earned. Management’s explanation has been the same since July 2024, quarter after quarter, in nearly the same words — “savings arising from lower customer acquisition and operating leverage” (CFO Jitin Diwan, Q3 FY25, Q4 FY25 and Q1 FY27).

“Margins continue to be elevated on account of savings arising from lower customer acquisition and operating leverage. Margins are anticipated to gradually normalise in the coming quarters, as we focus on measures to increase growth.”

Jitin Diwan, CFO — Q4 FY25 earnings call, 29 April 2025. The same sentence, minus the second half, opens the margin paragraph of the Q1 FY27 call, 21 July 2026

The mechanism: a harvest margin, in management’s own arithmetic

The margin has three moving parts and management has given a number for each. Acquisition cost: sales and marketing fell from 20% of revenue in FY23 (“because we suddenly hired so many people”) to 18% in FY24 and 13% in Q3 FY25, “at the lower side of the range” of 12–18% (CEO, Q3 FY25); the CEO says outright “we are consciously not investing and going aggressive to increase the supplier base”. Manpower: “Manpower cost plus outsourced sales cost put together remains same as last year… we have had almost zero expense growth” in FY25 (Q4 FY25), after +900 employees in FY23. Gross margin: management targeted 80% “sometime next year” in October 2022, reported 72% pre-COVID and “73%” in April 2024 (“I think we are doing fine and we’ll continue to probably improve by 1% from here on”). The 80% target was therefore never close; operating leverage in the sense of better unit economics is small, and cost restraint is large.

The CFO’s sustainable-margin guidance, repeated four times, is explicit that today’s level is conditional on not growing: “till the time we are confident on solving the churn and doubling down on the gross adds, it will be continue to look like 38%, 40% only. And once we push the pedal on gross adds, it will come down to a sustainable margin of about 33% to 35%” (Q4 FY25). The consolidated margin was 34% in FY26 and 35% in Q1 FY27 — at the top of the “sustainable” band while growth is not being pushed. The CEO added a fall-back that bears directly on this: “If we are not able to fix this two, three, four quarters, I think we can always go back to the ARPU led growth or customer acquisition-led growth at a lower profitability” (Q3 FY25, 21 Jan 2025). Eighteen months later the churn is unfixed and the option has not been exercised.

Two reconciliations that change what “margin” means

The first is the associates’ loss above: management’s EBITDA excludes it, Screener’s operating profit includes it. The second is the treasury. Management says the portfolio yields “in the range of the yield of like 6.5–7.5% of actual treasury income” over a half-year or longer (CFO, Q2 FY26). The realised figures, after stripping the identified one-off revaluation gains and dividing by average cash-and-treasury, are lower and far more volatile: roughly 4.2% (FY23), 7.7% (FY24), 8.2% (FY25), 4.0% (FY26), averaging ~5.9%. Two of four years sit below the stated band because of bond mark-to-market.

The consequence for the margin reading is that reported net profit is a mix of three things with three different quality levels: an operating margin held up by restraint (Gold/Platinum monetisation and cost freeze), a treasury income that is cyclical, and investee marks that are discretionary in timing. In FY26, ₹82 Cr of pre-tax profit (13% of PBT) was one investee revaluation. Another way to say it: of FY26 PBT of ₹648 Cr, ~₹122 Cr was treasury at a 4.0% yield and ~₹82 Cr was a one-off mark; the recurring operating piece is the ₹446 Cr of EBIT after associates (₹530 Cr EBITDA − ₹28 Cr depreciation − ₹55 Cr associate loss).

Is it the company or the category?

Info Edge, the closest listed analogue, lifted its operating margin from 28% (FY24) to 36% (FY26) while growing sales ~14% a year, per the peer file; its improvement is operating leverage on growth. IndiaMART’s +6 points over the same window came with growth falling from 21% to 13% and net customer adds going to zero. The same direction, different engine — a point against reading IndiaMART’s margin as simply “category leverage”. Justdial’s file stops at FY24 (21%), so no like-for-like attribution is possible there; with two peers this is a plausibility check, not an attribute() result.

05Working capital: the deferred-revenue float

How collections become revenue, why cash conversion looks flattering, and how to read it properly

For most companies on this pipeline, working capital is where the quality question lives. Here it is the business model. Customers pay before they are served, so the balance sheet carries almost no receivables (1.3 days) and a very large contract liability. Two readings follow, and they disagree: the liability is a free funding source that makes growth cash-generative, and it is also a promise to serve that sits against the cash it funded. The right test is not “is cash flow above profit” — it is always above profit in a business whose deferred revenue is growing — but whether billings turn into cash at a stable rate and whether the deferred balance rolls into revenue on schedule.

The mechanics, from management and the accounts

Management explains the pipeline in three layers. Revenue is mostly last period’s collections: “80% of the revenues is coming in from the opening balance of deferred revenues of that particular quarter” (CFO Prateek Chandra, Q4 FY24, 30 Apr 2024). The recognition profile is stable: asked in April 2023 whether the shift toward monthly customers had changed how collections convert to revenue, the CEO answered “No change actually if you really see. I mean 64%, 63% but no change”, and again in April 2025 “what gets recognised in 12 months and what gets recognised after 12 months remains consistent at 63% - 64%”. And the growth chain is sequenced: “If you take our business, customer growth, followed by collection growth, followed by deferred revenue growth, and followed by revenue growth… it’s more like a 100 day moving average” (CEO, Q4 FY24). The annual report’s own text gives the two ends: standalone collections ₹16,743 Mn and standalone deferred revenue ₹18,318 Mn (+14.5%) for FY26.

What would change this section’s reading

If the customer base keeps shrinking, the float shrinks with it, and the identity above runs the other way: collections below revenue, deferred revenue falling, CFO converting to roughly PAT and then below. Standalone collections (+8%) are already growing more slowly than consolidated revenue (+11%) and only just below revenue ex-BUSY (~9%, this report’s arithmetic from the Q1 FY27 and Q1 FY26 calls): the gap that builds the float stops widening. Because collections still exceed revenue by ~18% of revenue, the float keeps growing in rupees for years, so this is a watch item, not a finding — but it is the mechanism by which the quality of cash flow would visibly deteriorate first, ahead of margins.

Named competitors with no financials — and what the evidence says

TradeIndia (Infocom Network, 1996) and ExportersIndia (Weblink.in, 1997) run the same lead-generation model. This is a sector-wide signature of paid-listing lead-gen to small businesses, not evidence IndiaMART is better or worse. Udaan is a transacting marketplace with logistics and credit (raised ~$2.17 bn; down-round to ~$1.7–1.8 bn in Dec 2023; IPO targeted 2026) and is named by management only once, in a Q1 FY23 answer comparing average order values against reported Amazon ($13–14) and Udaan ($60–75) figures (“our average order value is almost 10x of that”). So, none of the competition” (Q3 FY25). These are single-source assertions, recorded as such. The absence of a named, disclosed churn destination is also consistent with the other reading — that Silver churn is customers leaving the category (or relying on free channels such as Google, WhatsApp and Instagram listings) rather than moving to a rival. The comparison that matters is with Google, and it is increasingly a comparison with LLMs

Demand side: enquiries, bought and organic

The enquiry story has three layers, each in management’s own words. The quality push: the number of suppliers each buyer is introduced to was cut from ~6.5 to ~3.8 (Q4 FY25), buyers must now answer qualifying questions, and 80–90% of requests carry quantity and specification (Q2 FY26) — all of which reduce raw volume by design. The paid push: performance marketing started in Q4 FY25 at ~₹1 Cr and is running ₹5.5–8 Cr a quarter, aimed at the “top 10,000 odd categories”; in October 2025 the CEO said half of the 12% enquiry growth was paid. The friction push: in the months before April 2026 buyers began to face OTP and other verification, and on that call “we are starting to do more buyer verification also, and that is leading to probably 1% of drop in the conversion from traffic”. Registered buyers rose 9% and active buyers fell 3% in FY26 (Abhisek Banerjee, Q4 FY26) — the first divergence on record, which the CEO attributes to agentic scraping inflating registrations. Of ~40 million active buyers, only ~10 million are GST-verified businesses (Q1 FY27).

Price: the one growth lever still being pulled

The Silver price history is now: ₹3,000 a month before COVID, cut to ₹2,500, restored to ₹3,000 on 15 May 2023 (Q1 FY24), then raised in September 2025 to ₹4,000 plus tax monthly and ₹35,000 annual (₹32,000 after a ₹3,000 discount) from ₹28,500 (Q3 FY26). The September 2025 step is +33% on the monthly plan (+12% on the annual plan after the discount), 28 months after the +20% restoration, against an October 2025 statement of intent that “simple 10% kind of a price rise every second year should be applicable”. Existing subscribers renew at old prices until they churn or reach their next renewal, so the full effect will not show for 6–9 months (the CEO and Swapnil Potdukhe, JM Financial, agree this, Jan 2026). The first read, Q4 FY26, is unflattering and management quantifies it: “I think if there was no war, we could have been probably 2,000 customers positive… 1,000 – 1,500 could be because of the war, 1,000 – 1,500 could have been because of the price.” The January 2026 upsell data show the second-order effect: silver-to-gold upgrades have fallen from 15–20% a year to 5–10% because “the renewal itself has dropped by 10–15%”. Fewer renewals mean fewer upgrades, which is how a Silver problem becomes a Gold/Platinum pipeline problem.

BUSY: the clean growth line, with an accounting note

BUSY’s numbers are strong but must be read around a presentation change. In Q3 FY25 BUSY changed the payout structure for channel partners, which grossed up reported billing and revenue (“an impact of approximately Rs. 7 crores and Rs. 10 crores respectively”, Q4 FY25; ~₹15 Cr in Q1 FY26). Normalised, FY26 billing grew 43% to ~₹170 Cr reported, revenue +44% to ₹119 Cr, with ₹49 Cr of operating cash flow; in Q1 FY27 reported billing grew only 10% (~30% normalised for a one-off ₹10 Cr of prior-year win-backs) while revenue grew 47% and deferred revenue 44%. Management runs BUSY for growth, not profit: “My continuous recommendation to Busy is to have zero EBITDA and invest all the money that they can generate into growth” (CEO, Q4 FY24). At 8.7% of group revenue it cannot offset a shrinking core, but it is flattering consolidated growth: IndiaMART’s ex-BUSY revenue growth in Q1 FY27 is ~9%, against the 11% headline.

Hindsight on the two buybacks

The June 2022 (₹100 Cr at ₹6,250, i.e. ₹3,125 bonus-adjusted) and August 2023 (₹500 Cr at ₹4,000) tenders bought ~15.7 lakh shares for ₹600 Cr, an average ~₹3,820. At ₹1,592 those shares are worth ~₹250 Cr: a mark-to-market shortfall of ~₹350 Cr, or ~₹495 Cr counting the ₹145 Cr of tax and expenses — about 5% of the current market cap. The tender prices were ~64× (2022) and ~86× (2023) trailing EPS on the bonus-adjusted share count, well above the 47–54× the market was paying at the nearest year-ends (Section 03), while collections growth was already beginning to roll over. Hindsight, to be fair to the decision: the August 2023 tender was announced two months after a 20% price restoration, with Q1 FY24 net adds at 5,000 — the weakness was visible. On the governance angle raised by retail investors (promoters took ~42% of one buyback’s proceeds against a ~49% holding), the mechanics are standard proportionate tender; the exact acceptance table was not retrieved and the figure remains forum-sourced (Section 11).

How much surplus is left is a separate question. By the CEO’s own framework (Section 05) about ₹500–600 Cr is genuinely free after the June dividend and Fleetx cheque, so a repeat of a ₹60 dividend funded from existing cash would be a draw on the safety margin; funded from FY27 CFO of ~₹700+ Cr it is comfortable. The one-paragraph version

IndiaMART is a debt-free, founder-run, prepaid classifieds platform that turns billings into cash at a steady 82–92% and keeps its best customers at ~1% monthly churn, but has not added net paying suppliers for 13 quarters, and says so. Its margin is a harvest margin; its collections growth is 8%; its enquiries are falling despite paid advertising; and its price of ₹1,592 equals the six-per-cent-growth bear case if you count customers’ prepayments as equity, and the thirteen-per-cent recovery case if you do not. The management record is the most useful single finding: promises about its own costs, payouts and disclosure are kept, promises about what small-business customers will do are not, and the horizon on the unfixed one has slid from “two quarters” to “a year or so”. That is a fair-value stock for someone willing to underwrite a stable Gold/Platinum core and a possible, unforecast restart in growth; it is not one for someone who needs the Silver funnel repaired before committing.
