The one-paragraph version
A good, cash-rich business with a flattered 2025, a growth floor that was cut, and a price that has run 13% in a month

What the calls and the annual report show. Of 29 dated or quantified statements management made on seven calls (April 2020, then March 2025 to May 2026), 9 were kept, 6 slipped, 5 broke and 9 are still unresolved. The things inside management's control held: days sales outstanding, cash conversion, the platform milestones, the answer on the sponsor's refinancing. The things that depended on client decisions did not: the “double-digit” baseline for 2025 closed at 7.6% in dollars, the four “mega consolidation deals” slipped by two to four quarters, and the 7.6% “floor” for 2026 was cut to 6–7% in August. The $3 billion target for calendar 2029 has not been repeated since July 2025.

What the numbers show. The 17.1% reported EBITDA margin for CY25, which the company hit in its guided range, is the database's 14.3% plus a ₹382 Cr non-cash reversal of the Softcrylic earn-out (2.8 points of sales). The company's own adjusted figure is also 17.1%, because it adds back about ₹377 Cr of exceptional costs. The two coincide; neither is the margin of the business. On the database basis EBITDA rose 4.5% against revenue up 12.2%, and basic EPS of ₹22.51 (+16.2%) is about ₹16–19 without the gain. Cash is real: operating cash flow was 1.28 times profit over ten years, DSO is 67 days, and there is no bank debt. But dividends rose from 45% of profit to 51% to about 72% of this year's estimate, while the controlling shareholder, CA Magnum (Carlyle), took a $1.26 billion HSBC-led loan in November 2025 secured by an upstream encumbrance.

What the price needs. At ₹561.70 the prior report's own method gives ₹355 (bear), ₹425 (base, 24% below the price) and ₹496 (bull). The prior report's figures at ₹497 are reproduced to within ₹2. The price needs a flat EBITDA margin of 20.4% on a growth path that fades from 12%, against 16% guided (EBIT 13–14%), or 13.4% a year of revenue growth for ten years at the guided margin, against 6–7% guided. An exit-multiple view is kinder: the price returns 13.7× EBITDA in year seven, close to where peers trade today, so the two frames disagree and this report weights the DCF.

Two-axis verdict. Quality: high on cash, returns on capital (ROCE 26%) and related-party cleanliness; held down by flattered headline earnings, a ledger that is wrong on the parts that depend on customers, goodwill at 63% of net worth, and a sponsor holding 74.3%. Value: expensive on this method; the analysis changes below about ₹430 (base covered) and needs the bull case above about ₹500. The 9 October jump on 30 million shares (5% of the company) has no cause I could find. Triggers are in Section 15. This is analysis, not advice.

Thin and discontinuous history: read this before the rest

The database holds ten continuous calendar years (CY16–CY25), but Hexaware was delisted in November 2020 and relisted on 19 February 2025. For CY21–CY24 there is no annual report and no call in the store; the database figures for those years are unverified against any primary document (only the CY25 annual report's five-year adjusted EBITDA series touches them). The annual reports in the store are CY15, CY17, CY18, CY19 and CY25; the file labelled 2020 is a duplicate of the CY19 report, so there is no CY16 and no CY20 report either. The annual-report conversions have lost their tables (five of the six files contain no table rows at all, the sixth one), so related-party values, receivables ageing and contingent liabilities cannot be read, only the prose around them. The call store holds eight files but seven distinct calls: one is an April 2020 COVID update, one is the corrected re-issue of the 29 April 2025 call, and the 30 July 2026 call that cut guidance is not in the store at all. Management can therefore be judged across six listed-era quarters, not through a cycle.

What changed against the 14 September 2026 report

That report read six of the eight files and carried the 280 bp gap between the database margin and the company's as an open item. This one reads all eight and resolves it, and the answer reverses a conclusion: the 17.1% that the old report called “a margin success” in its own words was flattered by the gain. Also new: the other-income line it called “likely IPO-related treasury income” is the same gain; the corrected April 2025 transcript names the $1bn bond as Carlyle's; the pledge is described in the annual report as an encumbrance on CA Magnum's shares held by CA Silkie, one level above the Hexaware shares themselves; Carlyle-funded MOIC payments of ₹42.9 Cr ran through the company's books; top-10 client concentration rose in CY25 (35.8% to 36.4%) where the old report said it was moderating; the 9/9 coffee-can pass holds only in rupees; and the price is ₹561.70, not ₹497. 01The business: a $1.5 billion IT-services firm with a sponsor on top and a CEO about to leave

Navi Mumbai · about 34,000 people (33,844 including subcontractors at December 2025) · CY25 revenue $1,537.4m = ₹13,430 Cr · calendar-year reporting

Hexaware sells application, infrastructure and business-process services to large, mostly American, companies. The Americas were 75.3% of CY25 revenue (74.0% in CY24). Management reports seven verticals since Q1 CY26: Financial Services, Banking, Healthcare & Insurance, Manufacturing & Consumer, Travel & Transportation (renamed GTT), Professional Services, and Technology, Products & Platforms (TPP), the last two created by splitting the old High-Tech & Professional Services vertical. Inside those it runs a services business (IT outsourcing, application development, cloud and data work) and a smaller business-process (BPS) arm whose headcount has been shrinking. Its own platforms carry the product pitch: Tensai (IT operations), RapidX (reverse-engineering and modernising legacy code) and Amaze (cloud migration).

How the money is earned

  • People, not products. Revenue is people billed to clients. Contractors cost about 17% of revenue (CFO, 7 Mar 2025) and are paid only for days worked, which is why the number of working days moves quarterly margins. Third-party licences resold to clients add $11–18m a quarter at low margin: when they were high in Q3 CY25 they cost 70 bp of EBITDA margin.
  • The offshore lever. Offshore revenue was about 49% in Q3 CY25, up 520 bp on the year, half of the sequential gain coming from the SMC Squared acquisition, which is entirely offshore. Management calls moving work offshore the main margin lever; two clients moved work offshore in Q1 CY25, which cost revenue and added margin.
  • Rebadging. Deals in which the vendor takes over a client's staff carry low margins on day one. On 5 Feb 2026 the CEO said three of the new deals “have re-badging components, which will depress our margins”; press coverage of the August call puts one such deal at 50 bp.
  • Clients. Management says “61, 62% of our revenues comes from clients whose revenues are above $5 billion” (29 Apr 2025). Top-10 clients were 36.4% of CY25 revenue; no single client exceeds 10%. Two clients now exceed $100m a year and 34 exceed $10m (May 2026).

The moat, in management's words

Asked on 29 April 2025 why Hexaware wins consolidation deals, the CEO answered:

“Why we win the ones that we've won is I'll say it's really kind of three things. One, whatever we executed, we execute very well. Two, we are able to bring more intensity and focus to the clients. And three, customers love our platforms.”

R. Srikrishna, CEO, Q1 CY25 call, 29 April 2025

That is execution, intensity and platforms, each unmeasured on the call. The supporting claims are self-reported and carry a scepticism flag: attrition “the lowest or among the lowest in the industry” (10.8% in Q4 CY24, about 11% since), a net promoter score of 67 “27 points higher than industry median”, and first place in the Whitelane UK and Ireland ranking for 2025. The sell-through ratio with the large clients “is very high” (7 Mar 2025). The only competitor named on any call is ANSR, as the specialist that sets up global capability centres (GCCs) alongside SMC. The consolidation deals are the real test of the moat, and in the ledger (Section 09) they came late.

Ownership and why the history matters

Hexaware was listed until November 2020, taken private by Baring at ₹475 a share, sold to Carlyle's vehicle CA Magnum in 2021 for a reported $3bn, and relisted on 19 February 2025 through an offer for sale of 12.37 crore shares at ₹708, all by CA Magnum (₹8,750 Cr; the company received nothing). The annual report confirms the offer-for-sale size and that the ₹32.9 Cr of IPO costs the company paid up front were recovered from CA Magnum by December 2025. CA Magnum held 74.3% at June 2026, public shareholders 6.2%, institutions about 19%. Yahoo's data puts insiders at 75.2%. A free float that thin is why a single 30-million-share day (9 October 2026, 5% of the company) moves the price 5%.

CEO leaves on 28 October 2026

The CEO since 2014, R. Srikrishna, steps down on 28 October 2026. His successor, Vivek Jetley (ex-EXL), takes over two weeks from this report. The six listed-era calls scored in Section 09 were all given by Srikrishna and the CFO, Vikash Jain; the guidance record belongs to a CEO who will not deliver or defend the 2026 numbers. The first call under the new CEO is the Q3 CY26 call, around November.

Context: the industry grew about 5–6%

The repo's industry brief, drawn from NASSCOM (secondary, medium confidence), has Indian IT exports growing 5.6% in the year to March 2026. Hexaware's 7.6% in dollars for CY25 beat that. The miss is against its own framing, not against the sector, and the 7.6% sits below the high-growth mid-tier names (Persistent +29% and Coforge +50% with acquisitions, latest-quarter growth in rupees; Section 06).

The other tests

  • Return on capital. ROCE has run 24.5–32.3% for ten years and was 26.2% in CY25. Return on equity (profit over book equity) has been 19–25%. This is not a thin-base figure: net block rose from ₹479 Cr to ₹5,130 Cr, mostly right-of-use assets and acquired customer relationships. But goodwill is ₹3,577 Cr against net worth of ₹5,708 Cr (63%), so the equity the return is earned on is substantially acquisition cost, and a write-down of the Softcrylic type reduces it.
  • Revenue growth above 10% every year. It holds in rupees (the lowest year is 11.5%) and fails in dollars in CY25 (7.6%). The database is in rupees, and the rupee weakened about 4% against the dollar in CY25 (87.4 against 83.8 per dollar, from the annual report's rupee and dollar revenue); the prior report's “9 / 9” pass is a rupee pass. Dollar growth was 13.5% in CY24.
  • Leverage. The database shows borrowings of ₹681 Cr and the module's total outside liabilities to net worth of 0.73×, up from 0.33×. Management says the balance sheet is “completely debt-free” (5 Feb 2026 call: “The balance sheet is completely debt-free”). The two can both be true; the box below explains how.
  • Ownership. CA Magnum 74.3%, public 6.2%. This is a sponsor, not a founder; the annual report lists six non-independent non-executive directors (the sponsor's nominees by structure, though the report does not label them), four independents and the CEO.
  • Related parties. The annual report says there are “no materially significant related party transactions” with promoters or directors. Section 12 covers the Carlyle-funded MOIC payments and recovered IPO costs that the clean statement sits beside.
  • Concentration. Top-10 clients were 56% of revenue in CY16 and 36.4% in CY25, a real decade-long fall. In the last year it rose, from 35.8% to 36.4%.
Debt-free is true of bank debt, not of obligations

The ₹681 Cr borrowings line is most likely lease liabilities (right-of-use assets are ₹612 Cr; the note that would settle it was stripped in conversion). Beyond that, the CY25 balance sheet carries other financial liabilities of ₹1,701 Cr, up ₹473 Cr in the year, which include deferred and contingent consideration for acquisitions (up ₹221 Cr), hedge mark-to-market liabilities (up ₹108 Cr) and employee liabilities. The CyberSolve deal alone carries $31.5m of performance-linked payments. None of this is in the net-cash number the valuation uses. Above the company, CA Magnum has $1.26bn of bank debt secured on shares (Section 10). The company has no bank debt; the structure it sits in does.

What the record says

The record is of a business that grew steadily and held a mid-teens margin through two changes of owner. Rupee revenue was ₹3,535 Cr in CY16 and ₹13,430 Cr in CY25. ROCE stayed between 24.5% and 32.3%. Dividends paid totalled ₹3,566 Cr against profit of ₹7,941 Cr. Free cash flow was ample and the business has no financial debt of its own.

Three things the table does not say on its face. First, the company's annual report claims “For the third consecutive year, profits grew faster than revenue” (CEO's letter, CY25). On the company's own adjusted EBITDA that is true for CY23 (+14.9% against revenue +12.8%) and CY24 (+23.2% against +15.4%) and false for CY25 (+10.5% against +12.2%). It is true for CY25 only on reported EBITDA (+20.7%) and EPS (+16.2%), both of which contain the ₹382 Cr gain (Section 04). Second, the adjusted EBITDA series grew 13.9% a year over CY21–CY25 (the report's own figure) against rupee revenue growth of 17.0% a year: margin dilution has been continuous. Third, dividends took 75% of CY22 profit and 53% of CY23, in the first two years of Carlyle's ownership, which is the period for which the sponsor had acquisition debt to service (this is a pattern, not a sourced cause).

The private years cannot be checked

CY21–CY24 are in the database but in no document in the store. CY25's annual report restates only adjusted EBITDA for them. The sponsor's offer document (the red-herring prospectus) would carry restated figures for CY22–CY24; it is not in the store. Any conclusion that rests on CY21–CY24 alone, including the CY22 margin step and receivable-day spike, is a database observation rather than a documented fact.

The guided fall: EBIT 13–14% and what it is a fall from

From Q1 CY26 management stopped reporting EBITDA and reports EBIT excluding other income and hedging and translation effects. Management's DSO, reconstructed

Management reports days sales outstanding as billed receivables plus unbilled revenue plus contract assets. That is my reconstruction, not a disclosure; the definition is not stated in the report. The database receivables line (₹2,056 Cr) is billed plus unbilled only, which is why its 56 days sits below management's 67. Over CY25 contract assets rose ₹210 Cr (76%) while unbilled revenue fell ₹84 Cr, so the mix moved toward contract assets, which are not yet billable.

Management's direct answers on credit are specific. On 25 Jul 2025 the CFO said of a European client: “One of the European clients acted in bad faith to settle their dues, and the client is also undergoing some financial strain”, with a $9m provision, “a provision and not a write-off”. On 5 Feb 2026, of a further $4m: “Now, last couple of years, we have seen an increase in credit risk on account collections”, and the charge was “generic in nature and not associated with any specific client”. The CEO added on 5 Feb 2026 that “two software clients, substantial ones, went bankrupt during the year”, and on 7 Mar 2025 that at least two manufacturing clients had been through Chapter 11. Top-10 customer dues were 30.4% of receivables at December 2025 (29.6% a year earlier) against 36.4% of revenue, so large clients pay faster than average. The April 2020 call, the only pre-delisting call, had the same question: analysts asked whether clients wanted credit relaxation and the CEO said a few might ask to pay “a few days later” but “very, very, very few customers” would.

Gross and net disagree, in the opposite direction to the usual

The module's working-capital measure fell from +13.9% of sales in CY16 to −2.3% in CY25, which would normally signal a business funded by suppliers. It is not that. The database's other-liabilities line includes acquisition earn-outs, hedge liabilities and new employee provisions, none of which is operating funding. Counting only receivables, unbilled revenue and contract assets less trade payables, working capital was about +11% of sales in CY25 (INR 15.4bn on INR 134.3bn). The three-year incremental working-capital ratio in the module is −23%, which would suggest the business releases cash as it grows; that is the same artefact. For the valuation I held incremental working capital at zero, as the prior report did, and show 10% and 20% as sensitivities (Section 14).

07Growth: a floor that moved, a $3 billion target that went quiet

What was guided, what was delivered, and what the pipeline can and cannot say

CY25: from a baseline to a lesson

On the first call after relisting (7 March 2025) the CEO declined to give formal guidance for the year (“we've decided not to do guidance”) and then gave an anchor: “Double-digit, I think, is like a solid baseline you can assume”. Hexaware closed CY25 at 7.6% in dollars (7.1% in constant currency; 6.6% pro forma for acquisitions) against 13.5% in CY24. The sequence of calls records how the expectation fell:

  • 29 Apr 2025: a “surprisingly normal quarter” with 12.4% year-on-year growth, two government-sponsored-enterprise (GSE) clients, one delayed and one cut by about 1% of revenue, and a promise of accelerating Q3 and a growing Q4.
  • 25 Jul 2025: “Our quarter on revenue performance this quarter was a little bit softer than what we had anticipated, predominantly driven by delayed decision-making from customers”. The CEO said expectations for the year were “lower than what they were a quarter ago”, and that the slowdown was “not AI-driven. It is not structural”.
  • 7 Nov 2025: “Last quarter, we said our business model is shifted right by 1–2 quarters”; Q4 would be flat. A US government shutdown stopped visa transfers, which limited on-site hiring for growing accounts.
  • 5 Feb 2026: Q4 revenue was “a tad lower than we expected”: a GSE cut worth about 70 bp annualised, a client that furloughed, and lower pass-through revenue. The CEO opened the 2026 outlook with the lines the prior report quoted: “Now, given how CY25 panned out, clearly, we have some lessons learnt on how to communicate with you”.

The business-model reasons the CEO gave for the miss, on 5 Feb 2026, were Softcrylic (“the performance was not good”), two bankrupt software clients and a weak manufacturing and consumer sector. The macro reason he gave on 25 Jul 2025 (“I think it's macros”) is a different explanation, and both were given within seven months. Both are management's account, uncorroborated by any independent source in the store; the vertical numbers support the macro explanation for Manufacturing & Consumer, which was down 11.5% year on year in Q2 CY25.

CY26: better than 7.6%, a floor, then 6–7%

On 5 Feb 2026 the CEO said: “our revenue growth to be better than what we reported in CY25, which is 7.6%”, added that it was not a new baseline, and kept the long-term claim: “Our core thesis of growth in base growth in low-teens and acceleration levers to get us to mid to high teens, remains completely intact”. On 7 May 2026 he went further: “At a minimum, we are reaffirming what we said last time, the floor of growth of 7.6%”. DAM Capital's analyst replied that this needed about 4.5% growth a quarter for three quarters. In August 2026, per press coverage of the call (investing.com summary, five outlets agree on direction; not read from a primary transcript), the guide was cut to 6–7%, with management saying (paraphrased from the press summary) that the path to 7.6% was narrow and that the new range needs a 2.7% compound quarterly rate. The reasons given: three or four deals expected to ramp in Q2 now ramp in mid to late Q3, a Travel & Transportation vertical that shrank 18% year on year, and a rebadging deal worth about 50 bp. I treat the August figures as medium confidence.

The $3 billion ambition

On 29 April 2025 the CEO tied the growth accelerators to “a 3 billion ambition on revenue for us”, arriving “in calendar year 2029”. On 25 July 2025 he said it “remains unchanged” after cutting the year's expectations. It was not mentioned again on the 7 November 2025, 5 February 2026 or 7 May 2026 calls, where the language changed to “low-teens” base growth and “mid to high teens” with levers. The base path in the valuation (Section 14) reaches about $2.2bn in CY29. The target is UNRESOLVED in the ledger, but the evidence is that it is no longer being repeated.

Where growth is coming from

  • Deal wins and pipeline. Management does not report total contract value; on 29 Apr 2025 the CEO said “the reason we're not reporting in TCV” is the gap between booking and revenue, and gives instead the revenue a deal adds over 12 months (for the two GSE deals, $20–30m and $25–35m a year). The pipeline stood at above $3bn in November 2025, which the CEO described as “It's got to this size in part because it got bloated”, and above $4bn for the first time on 5 Feb 2026. In February he said the company had won “pretty much everything we expected to win” in Q4, headlined by a consolidation deal at a big technology company that “doesn't come with a predefined book of work”.
  • Legacy modernisation (RapidX). Roughly 40 clients were in the pipeline in April 2025, mostly as proofs of concept; two paid orders beyond proof of concept by July and three scale deals by November. The CEO expects two or three to complete in CY26. This is the clearest example of a promise that was kept.
  • Global capability centres. SMC Squared (acquired July 2025; $22m revenue the year before; about $45m paid) and CyberSolve (identity security, bought in November 2025 for $35m upfront plus $31.5m performance-linked; $26m revenue, so 2.5× sales at full price). The Q1 CY26 call said SMC's earn-out was largely earned.
  • AI services. A new service roughly every month, per the CEO on 7 May 2026, and “a dozen areas of new revenue opportunities”; the company holds an AI day in Chennai after the Q2 results. The CEO says AI in software engineering is “a dampener on our revenue”, quantified in Section 04. Net of that, the claim is “positive growth for us”; no revenue from the new services has been disclosed.
  • Verticals for CY26 (as of 7 May 2026). Healthcare & Insurance leads, Banking is strong, Manufacturing & Consumer recovers and may lead, Travel lags because of fuel prices hitting airlines. The CEO's CY26 vertical call changed within three months: Travel moved from company average to the laggard.

The two large government-sponsored clients

These two clients decided the year. In April 2025 one cut spend sharply (about 1% of company revenue, a “peanut butter” cut); the other's consolidation deal was delayed. By 5 Feb 2026 another cut worth 70 bp had landed; by 7 May 2026 “We've had ~40-45 % down over the last four quarters”. Hexaware was then down-selected as one of three vendors and expects “stability, not necessarily growth immediately”; the CEO said growth is next year, not this one. The cause of every cut is the client's cost reduction, not Hexaware's delivery (per the CEO), and that is unverifiable. What can be said is that management called the downside “in the books” in April 2025 and was wrong twice.

Against what management said

The calls and the rating agree on the structure and disagree on emphasis. With dividends of ₹17 a share indicated for CY26 that is about ₹770 Cr a year (Section 11). Fitch has withdrawn its rating, so the external monitor on the group's leverage is gone, and no one asked about it on the three calls after the loan was signed (Section 08). The rating tripwires Fitch published are not in the store, so none can be quoted here.

News reports of 17 November 2025 describe CA Magnum pledging its entire 74.55% stake (45.39 crore shares) for the $1.26bn HSBC-led facility. The annual report, a primary source written later, words it differently:

Hexaware Technologies Ltd, CY25 annual report, shareholder information section

So the Hexaware shares are not directly pledged; the shares of the holding company that owns them are. A lender enforcing on that security would take control of the holding company and hence of the 74.3%. The economics for a minority holder are close to a pledge, the legal route is different, and the Hexaware share price is not the margin trigger in the way a direct pledge would make it. The loan terms (rate, loan-to-value, margin-call triggers) are not disclosed in anything in the store.
