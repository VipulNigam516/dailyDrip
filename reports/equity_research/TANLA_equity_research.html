The one-paragraph version
A cash-rich, debt-free franchise whose profit has been flat for four years and whose price already assumes that continues: quality B−, value fair, no margin of safety

Tanla sells India’s business text messages and pays about three quarters of every rupee of revenue out to suppliers, mostly telcos. Revenue is Rs 4,418 Cr (FY26), EBITDA margin 16.4%, profit Rs 509 Cr, net cash about Rs 1,029 Cr, ROCE 25%. We read all 26 earnings calls and built a ledger of 48 dated statements checked against later calls: 9 kept, 12 slipped, 18 broken, 9 unresolved. Management is reliable on money it controls (buybacks, dividends, capex, cash conversion) and unreliable on platform milestones, counterparties and claims of no competition.

Profit after tax was Rs 539 Cr in FY22 and is Rs 509 Cr in FY26. The margin fall since FY25 is deliberate spending (gross margin rose 50bp while indirect costs rose Rs 91 Cr), the fall in FY23 was price, and the platform layer that was to lift the business is 9% of revenue and fell 1.4% in FY25. Receivables over six months doubled in FY25 with no stated cause. Governance has no related-party problem but three CFOs in five years and three audit chairs in six, a board-composition fine and a Rs 46.9 Cr tax order that no analyst has asked about.

Valuation leads with the reverse DCF: the price needs a flat 15.9% EBITDA margin on our base growth path, or 7.1% growth a year at today’s margin. Scenario DCF: bear Rs 450, base Rs 613, bull Rs 821 (probability-weighted about Rs 606); exit-multiple cross-check Rs 404, Rs 599 and Rs 941. The analysis changes below about Rs 450 and above about Rs 800. This is not a buy or sell call.

01The business: a toll-keeper on India’s business messages with a small, high-margin platform layer that has not scaled

What it sells, who buys it, how concentrated it is, and where the profit actually comes from

Tanla Platforms Ltd (Hyderabad; formerly Tanla Solutions) is the larger of India’s two listed sellers of application-to-person (A2P) messaging: the one-time passwords, bank alerts, delivery notices and promotional texts that banks, e-commerce firms, insurers, airlines and government bodies send to phones. It buys the right to deliver those messages from telecom operators and resells it with a margin. In FY2026 (year to March 2026) it reported consolidated revenue of Rs 4,418 Cr, EBITDA of Rs 726 Cr (16.4%) and profit after tax of Rs 509 Cr, with borrowings of Rs 53 Cr (lease liabilities) against Rs 1,144 Cr of cash and liquid investments. At Rs 582.55 on 27 October 2026 the market capitalisation is Rs 7,727 Cr, so the company is priced at 15.2 times FY26 earnings and about 9.2 times FY26 EBITDA after netting about Rs 1,029 Cr of free cash.

Two businesses with opposite economics

The FY2026 annual report splits the company into Enterprise Communications (Karix and ValueFirst: SMS, WhatsApp, RCS, voice) and Digital Platforms (Trubloq, the spam-filtering ledger that telcos pay for, plus the Wisely products). Enterprise is 91% of revenue (Rs 4,023 Cr) but earns only a 19.5% gross margin because most of each rupee is the operator’s termination charge passed straight through. Platforms are 9% of revenue (Rs 395 Cr) at a 98.5% gross margin and supply a third of gross profit (33.1%). Management described the enterprise model plainly in 2018:

“We collect the full amount and we keep our revenue share and pass on the remaining amount to Telco.”

— Management, Q4 FY2018 earnings call, 18 May 2018

The platform line exists because of a regulation. TRAI’s 2018 telemarketing rules (TCCCPR 2018, in force February 2019) require every commercial message to pass a header, template and consent check on a shared ledger. Tanla’s Trubloq runs that ledger for several operators and, per management, is paid by them rather than by senders. On the Q2 FY2022 call management said Trubloq’s market share had risen to 63%, and on the next call named the two other ledger vendors: “I think IBM has deployed with Airtel and the Tech Mahindra is deployed with Jio” with “something like” 35% between them. Both are management’s figures and we found no independent check.

Who the customers are and how concentrated they are

CARE Ratings’ April 2026 rationale says 19 customers contributed 41% of FY2025 revenue and 46% in the first nine months of FY2026. Management gave a wider but consistent picture on the Q4 FY2026 call: about three quarters of enterprise business comes from the top 250 customers and the rest from “another fifty, sixty thousand enterprises”. CARE adds that half of revenue comes from clients of more than five years’ standing. Those are sticky customers, but they are also customers with purchasing departments: CARE writes that Tanla’s “pricing power is structurally constrained by telecom operators” (section 10), and the same note records that over-the-top (OTT) channels, mostly WhatsApp, rose from 15% of revenue in FY24 to 23% in FY25 and 31% in the nine months of FY26.

The customer base also changed shape in ways the revenue line hides. The 2025 online-gaming ban hit a vertical that mattered: “Dream11 is one of our largest customers and the rest is history”, the CEO said on the Q2 FY2026 call, and a colleague said a gaming business went “overnight”. A government tender (NIC) was lost in FY26; management said “the impact is not material” (Q3 FY2026 call). Vodafone Idea, a major platform customer, was exited in FY24 (section 9). The FY24 annual report notes that top-20 customer concentration fell from 58% to 42% when ValueFirst became inter-company; that is arithmetic of consolidation, not diversification.

How the company got here, in four steps

  1. Tanla Solutions to FY2019. A small messaging and mobile-payments firm with revenue of Rs 579–1,004 Cr, EBITDA margins of 8–10%, and a negative Rs 30 Cr operating cash flow in FY19. Its calls were held, then stopped: we hold calls for 2017 and 2018 and then nothing until June 2021.
  2. FY2020 reset. Karix (bought under a share purchase agreement in FY2018–19 from a non-resident seller) was consolidated; the company wrote off its legacy technology (Rs 305.6 Cr per the FY2020 annual report) and impaired Rs 48.7 Cr of goodwill, taking a net loss of Rs 211 Cr. See section 3 for why the write-off figure appears three ways.
  3. FY2021–22 boom. Higher gross margin (the FY2021 annual report’s explanation), the DLT platform and an international (ILD) price rise from July 2021 lifted EBITDA margin from 9.5% to 21.9% and profit to Rs 539 Cr.
  4. FY2023 onwards. Margin fell back, ValueFirst was bought from Twilio in June 2023 for Rs 374 Cr (cash-flow statement, FY2024 annual report), and revenue has since grown 6% a year from FY24. Gamooga was merged into Karix in November 2025.

The founder, Uday Kumar Reddy, holds 23.45% (3,11,01,481 shares at 31 March 2026, up from 21.72% a year earlier); the promoter group holds 46.17% in total (FY2026 annual report). Foreign institutions hold about 8% and domestic institutions under 1% on the public Screener page read on 27 October 2026. Mutual-fund absence is a recurring question on retail forums (section 13), and the answer is not in any filing we read.

What this section changes

Do not read Tanla as a software company because it has a 98% gross-margin platform line. Two thirds of gross profit is a pass-through messaging business with 19.5% gross margin, 41% of revenue from 19 customers, and operators who set its input prices. The platform line is real, but it is Rs 395 Cr, and it fell 1.4% in FY25 and grew 8.5% in FY26.

Part one, FY2017–FY2019: a company that did not generate cash

Revenue grew from Rs 579 Cr to Rs 1,004 Cr while EBITDA margin sat between 8% and 10% and operating cash flow fell from Rs 72 Cr to a negative Rs 30 Cr. On the Q4 FY2018 call management said it would “retain probably upwards of 85% of our EBITDA going forward”; the next year, cash flow was negative (section 9). Depreciation rose from Rs 20 Cr to Rs 57 Cr to Rs 73 Cr after intangible assets were capitalised in Q1 FY18 on useful lives of “5 to 10 years”.

Part two, FY2020: the write-off, in three versions

FY2020 is the year that explains the later figures, and the filings give it three ways. The FY2020 annual report’s MD&A says management “assessed the net book value of such assets as on March 31, 2019 at INR 305.6 Cr and provided accelerated depreciation”. The notes to the same year’s accounts give Rs 239.4 Cr standalone (23,940.17 lakh) and Rs 256.1 Cr consolidated (25,612.32 lakh) of “additional provision of accelerated depreciation” for FY2020. The FY2021 annual report says “Accelerated Depreciation of ₹ 290.8 crore was provided on legacy assets in FY20”. We cannot tell from the filings whether these are different measures of one item (the first is a net book value assessed at 31 March 2019 and provided “as and when the related platforms were moved to cloud”, which could span more than one year); the Rs 14.8 Cr gap between 305.6 and 290.8 is not explained in any filing we read. The stated reason in all three is the same: the 2018 telemarketing regulation, with blockchain-based ledgers, “resulted in the need for re-assessment of useful life of technology assets”. Separately, goodwill of Rs 48.7 Cr was impaired on management’s reading of Covid-19.

Two things the record adds. First, on the Q1 FY2018 call the CFO said the company charged “depreciation for an average period of 5 to 10 years for all the intangible assets”, and on the Q4 FY2018 call he put net assets at “around Rs. 451 crore”; within two years much of that base was written off. The company attributes this to a regulation announced in July 2018, an external cause, but a reader should note the lives were set before the regulation and cut after it. Second, that is where NFAT comes from (section 2): net block fell from Rs 350 Cr to Rs 257 Cr and fixed-asset turnover tripled without any change in how the business operates.

Part three, FY2021–FY2022: the boom

EBITDA margin doubled to 18.6% and then 21.9%; profit went from a Rs 211 Cr loss to Rs 539 Cr; and ROCE reached 46–48%. The FY2021 report explains the margin step by “an increase in gross margin and reduction in both sales & marketing expenses and general & administration expenses”. It also ran a 14% effective tax rate against 20% the next year, which flatters FY21 profit by about Rs 25 Cr relative to a 20% charge (our arithmetic). The FY21 report states the 14% rate without a reconciliation in its MD&A (it mentions only that the FY20 deferred-tax credit of Rs 26.1 Cr “would be reversed in subsequent years”); the cause of the low rate is not found in the document read. Retail forums noticed it at the time and the database confirms it (14.1% in FY21, 20.0% in FY22).

Part four, FY2023–FY2026: the plateau

Revenue has grown from Rs 3,355 Cr to Rs 4,418 Cr, a 9.6% annual rate, but that includes ValueFirst from Q2 FY24 (the Q2 FY24 call says organic growth was 7% when reported growth was 19%). Profit after tax was Rs 539 Cr in FY22 and is Rs 509 Cr in FY26; per share it is about Rs 39.6 against Rs 38.4 after four buybacks (our arithmetic on year-end share counts). EBITDA margin has fallen to 16.4% and depreciation has nearly tripled from Rs 46 Cr to Rs 122 Cr.

The cash story is better than the profit story, but not as good as ten-year totals suggest

Ten-year CFO of Rs 3,413 Cr against profit of Rs 2,786 Cr reads as strong conversion. Over FY22–FY26 the ratio is 0.995 times, and receivable days have risen from 64 to 82 while payable days fell from 77 to 56. Profit of Rs 2,552 Cr and about Rs 392 Cr of depreciation over FY22–FY26 were offset by a build of about Rs 590 Cr in net operating working capital (Rs 50 Cr to Rs 641 Cr), which is why CFO only matched profit. Unbilled revenue is part of that build (Rs 489 Cr, about 11% of revenue, at March 2026); how much of it is slow-paying rather than simply unbilled we cannot separate from the filings read.

Gross margin did not fall; it rose 50 basis points. Indirect costs rose Rs 91 Cr to 10.2% of revenue (8.9% in FY25), and that alone explains the 80-basis-point drop in EBITDA margin. The report says so itself (“Growth in gross profit was partially offset by higher indirect costs, depreciation, and amortisation during the year”), and the Q1 FY2026 call had said it in advance:

“each line item of our expenditure has gone up despite revenue being flattish. I think that was a conscious call by the management team.”

— Abhishek Jain (CFO), Q1 FY2026 earnings call, 25 Jul 2025

Depreciation and amortisation rose 24.5%, which the report ties to “amortisation of platforms that went live during the year, including Wisely.ai”. So the profit line pays for the platform investment through two doors, indirect cost (people, RSUs, go-to-market) and amortisation. Both are single-source (this one report) as to cause, but they are consistent with the CFO’s statement on the call, with the quarterly series (depreciation Rs 22.9 Cr in Q4 FY24, Rs 32.9 Cr in Q1 FY27), and with the Q3 FY25 call, where an analyst pointed to internally developed platforms rising from Rs 85 Cr to Rs 146 Cr in a quarter and the CFO defended capitalising platform-development staff cost.

Why this matters for the margin forecast

If the FY26 margin fall is discretionary, it can be reversed; if the investment has to continue to produce growth, it cannot. Even if indirect costs returned to FY25’s 8.9% of revenue, today’s 26.6% gross margin would give an EBITDA margin of 17.7%, not the 19% in our bull case. Reaching 19% therefore needs gross margin near 28%, which means platform mix well above a third of gross profit. We do not assume that in the base case.

FY2022 to FY2023: the price episode

The big drop was earlier. EBITDA margin fell 4.3 points in FY23, and the Q1 FY2023 call gave the decomposition: forex about 1pp, platform modernisation about 1pp and customer-specific about 2.5pp, “cumulative impact on EBITDA of almost around 4.5%”. The customer-specific item was a top-five customer whose volumes were scaled down after a large telco quoted a lower price. Two statements from the same call sit uneasily together:

“Overall, we don't see any pricing competition as such, okay. Though, little bit of price erosion is always there, but this is very normal for our industry.”

— Deepak Goyal (Executive Director and CBO), Q1 FY2023 earnings call, 26 Jul 2022

… and, in the same answer, that pricing had been “quoted much lower” for one large customer. On the next call, asked whether a large bank had been lost, management said “we have not lost the bank. Yes, we have lost certain volumes through the competition” (Q2 FY2023 call). The margin effect was therefore real, the cause is management’s account (corroborated by the telco-quote being acknowledged in the Q3 FY2023 call: “one of the large telecom provider also made certain efforts to enter into this market”), and the recovery to about 20% in Q4 FY23 is what the FY23 explanation predicted. That one-quarter recovery did not last: the margin was 16.0% by Q4 FY24.

Is there pricing power?

The evidence is mixed and mostly negative. When Tanla raised domestic prices in August 2023, the Q2 FY2024 call recorded that the rise “contributed around ₹35 crores growth sequentially. However, the demand was elastic and we saw a drop in the volumes of around 7%”. Management reset ILD prices twice (about 100% in FY22, 33% in FY23) and said about half of Q1 FY24 growth came from price increases. But on the Q1 FY2026 call the CFO said:

“We have seen a lot of price aggressiveness with our competition. Maybe some of those have settled, but there are still players, one or two, which are a little bit competitive, which has eroded our realisation versus the volume growth.”

— Abhishek Jain (CFO), Q1 FY2026 earnings call, 25 Jul 2025

CARE’s April 2026 note agrees: Tanla’s “pricing power is structurally constrained by telecom operators, as SMS termination rates and route availability are largely set by telcos”, and enterprise gross margin is “minimal at 18-20%”. On the Q4 FY2026 call an investor described “a continuous pricing shrinkage that is happening both from the large banks and also from the Government segment”; a director answered that a reset “is on the cards” but could not say when. Two independent sources, the agency and the call record, say the same.

The quarterly series makes the present state plain. Over the six most recent quarters EBITDA margin has stayed between 15.8% and 17.0%, with Q1 FY27 at 16.4% on a 17.8% rise in revenue. The CFO on the Q4 FY2026 call: “We do have seen a decline, but honestly, we have become range bound. If you look at it, the operating profit at a gross margin level has improved slightly in the last couple of quarters.” We take that at face value as description; as prediction it is a claim to track in the ledger (section 9).

What we could not establish

Gross margin before FY25 is not in a filing we have by year (only call-quoted bands: 29.5% in Q3 FY22, 25–27% in the 12 quarters to Q3 FY25), so the split of the FY22–FY25 margin fall between price, mix and cost cannot be stated to the point. The FY23 split above is management’s own.

What the company says, and what the ageing shows

Management has explained the build in pieces. There is one place where management and the numbers disagree outright. At the June 2021 Investors’ Day the company said “Our net working capital excluding cash was negative as of year-end balance sheet date.” On the Screener basis FY21 net operating working capital was positive Rs 50 Cr (2.1% of sales). The definitions probably differ (the company may have counted unbilled and other current liabilities); we could not reconcile them, and by FY26 the same measure is Rs 641 Cr positive on any definition we can construct.

Cost of growth

Each additional rupee of sales has taken about 28 paise of working capital over the last three and five years (20 paise over two). At base-case added revenue of Rs 440–540 Cr a year that is Rs 110–135 Cr of cash tied up annually at 25%, which is why the valuation uses 25% and tests 15% and 35% (section 14). One structural offset is WhatsApp: the Q4 FY2024 call said Tanla collects “in about 60 odd days from invoicing, and you may pay in 60 odd days”, so OTT growth is working-capital neutral if it holds.

Reading the questions as a signal

The pattern

Management keeps what it controls. Capex stayed under the Rs 5 Cr it promised in 2017 (Rs 1.5 Cr). The 30% dividend payout policy, announced in 2022, has run at 29–32% of PAT in every year since. The three buybacks announced on calls in our window (Rs 65 Cr in 2021, Rs 170 Cr in 2022, Rs 175 Cr in 2025) were completed as stated. It misses on three kinds of statement. Dated platform milestones: Wisely-Vodafone Idea launch (eight weeks late, then lost within 18 months), the case studies (never published), the platform revenue disclosure (not given), a platform growth floor of 20% (the segment shrank 1.4% in FY25), two platforms at Rs 100 Cr gross margin (never shown) and the anti-phishing bank signings (six months to the first and about 30 months to the third, against a promise of all three in one quarter). The rationale is a corrective to the narrative in section 1. What the credit view adds to the thesis

The liquidity read is strong and consistent (cash Rs 859 Cr at December 2025, gearing 0.02x, a downgrade trigger that needs cash to halve). The operating read is the one CARE keeps declining to upgrade. A reader should treat the rating as supportive of balance-sheet safety and silent on equity value.

Buybacks: generous, at a premium, and not always funded from that year's cash

Four buybacks in five years (FY2026 annual report). The prices paid were Rs 920.88 in the open market in September 2021 (Rs 65 Cr), Rs 1,200 by tender in January 2023 (Rs 170 Cr, Rs 212 Cr with tax) and Rs 875 by tender in 2025 (Rs 175 Cr). Against the month-end market closes (yfinance, our reading) of Rs 787 in September 2022 and Rs 644 in June 2025, the tenders were priced at about 52% and 36% above market. A tender at a premium transfers value from holders who do not tender to holders who do; in the 2025 tender the founder evidently did not tender, because his holding rose by 18.6 lakh shares in FY26 (what promoters did in 2022 is not stated in what we read). The FY23 payout of Rs 321 Cr (dividend plus buyback) came in a year when free cash flow was only Rs 91 Cr: the balance sheet paid, not the year's earnings. Today's price of Rs 582.55 is 51% below the 2023 tender price and 33% below the 2025 one.

The tax logic changed in the middle. In January 2025 management said:

“buyback, if I understand correctly, is not anymore same tax efficiency or tax policies. I think the regulation has changed mid of this year.”

— Management, Q3 FY2025 earnings call, 22 Jan 2025

Five months later the board approved a Rs 175 Cr tender buyback at Rs 875. On the Q1 FY2026 call a shareholder challenged the tax arithmetic and management replied “basis our evaluation, it does make sense to a good proportion of our shareholders’ base”. On the next call the same shareholder asked for the calculation and was told “There is no need to get back to you, okay?” We have not independently checked the tax treatment; the point is that the stated reason against buybacks in January was not the stated reason in June.

Acquisitions

Karix (SPA in FY2018–19, price about Rs 340 Cr in cash and stock on forum posts, medium) is the larger success: the June 2023 call credits it with taking EBITDA “from Rs25 crores to around Rs500 crores in the last four years”, a claim we could not reconcile with segment data because EBITDA by segment has only been shown as EBIT since Q1 FY24. ValueFirst (Rs 374 Cr in cash for the India business, announced 9 Jun 2023) was bought at about seven times EBITDA by management's account, with a first-quarter EBITDA margin of 20% against 5–7% before; that is Rs 80 Cr a year if it holds, or 4.7 times the price (our arithmetic on a single quarter). Goodwill is about 11.7% of equity (FY2025 key audit matter) and was the subject of a goodwill impairment of Rs 48.7 Cr in FY20, so the earlier history of acquisition accounting is not clean. Management's own statements on buy versus build are in section 9.

The cash itself earns little

Cash and liquid investments were Rs 1,144 Cr at March 2026 (Rs 1,009 Cr a year earlier), of which Rs 62 Cr is restricted, Rs 53 Cr is in rupee current accounts, Rs 98 Cr in dollar current accounts, Rs 852 Cr in fixed deposits, Rs 54 Cr in long fixed deposits and Rs 25 Cr in liquid funds (FY2026 annual report). Interest income on deposits and liquid funds was Rs 34 Cr, a yield of about 3.2% on the average balance (our arithmetic), or 3.8% if only the Rs 906 Cr of fixed deposits earned it. That is well below what Indian bank deposits pay. The Q4 FY2022 CFO gave one reason: “Lot of our ILD business is done overseas and it's in dollars” with “challenges around moving cash across entities, there are taxation implications” and 30–40% of the then cash in Singapore not earning interest (our notes). That is management's explanation four years old and not repeated since; the FY26 report does not say where the deposits sit or at what rates. At a 6.5–7% rupee deposit rate Rs 906 Cr would earn Rs 59–63 Cr, against the Rs 34 Cr reported. We flag a possible shortfall of up to about Rs 25 Cr a year (dollar deposits earn less, so part of it is explained) and do not claim more.

Verdict on capital allocation

Disciplined on dividends and cash, fair on acquisitions, and arguably generous on buybacks priced at a premium that the balance sheet and not the year's cash funded. The weak point is the cash drag: Rs 1,029 Cr of net cash is 13% of the market capitalisation and earns about 3%, and the company has not said how it intends to use it. On the Q4 FY2025 call it said it would “continue to hunt for the right strategic assets” and “We do not want to rush.” Eighteen months and no acquisition later, that remains an intention.

Tax, litigation and the auditor

Contingent liabilities for claims not acknowledged rose to Rs 63.56 Cr at March 2026 from Rs 16.84 Cr, almost all of it a single order. On 31 March 2026 the income-tax department issued an order under sections 201(1) and 201(1A) demanding Rs 46.90 Cr for alleged failure to deduct withholding tax under section 195 on the 2018–19 purchase of Karix from a non-resident seller who relied on the India-Mauritius treaty. The seller relied on a tax opinion that no withholding was needed, the share purchase agreement carries an indemnity from the seller, and management is filing an appeal; in the report's words, management is of the view that “the demand is not tenable” (we have not seen the order itself). It equals 0.6% of market capitalisation and 9% of FY26 profit. The risk is collection from the indemnifying seller, a non-resident entity; the report does not say anything about its capacity to pay. Other items: guarantees of Rs 189.3 Cr, a secondary guarantee of Rs 200 Cr for a subsidiary, service-tax and cenvat disputes, and several Karix and mGage assessments, several of which the company calls remote. No analyst asked about any of this (section 8).

The statutory auditor is MSKA & Associates; the audit partner changed between FY23 and FY24; key audit matters in FY25 were revenue recognition and goodwill impairment. The internal auditor has been Deloitte since FY20. We saw no auditor qualification in the sections read.

Disclosure and investor relations

Three behaviours stand out. Investor calls stopped after April 2026 (reported on hold in July 2026; the Q1 FY27 results came with a press release and a shareholder report). Management declined to give per-platform revenue on the first call we hold with the platform business and never began. And two shareholders on the Q2 FY2026 call said they could not get replies from investor relations. History that matters, and what the filings do and do not show

Retail forums carried allegations about the Tanla Solutions era for years: promoter warrants in 2016–17 (the CMD said on the Q4 FY2017 call “in hindsight there is no need for us to issue warrants to raise the money to meet the working capital”), a capex-heavy asset base that was later written off, and funds “squandered” on overseas ventures in the 2000s. A governance-risk thread on a forum lists Tanla Solutions as a case; no judgment or regulatory finding is cited in it, and a web search found no SEBI order or show-cause notice against Tanla (the SEBI orders database was not searched directly). We therefore report suspicion about the earlier era as a reason for caution, not a finding.

14Valuation: the price assumes today’s margin and 7% growth, equals our base case, and leaves no margin of safety either way

Reverse DCF first, then scenario DCF, sensitivity, and the exit-multiple cross-check

Price and balance sheet first, because they fix everything else. Tanla closed at Rs 582.55 on 27 October 2026 (yfinance, TANLA.NS; the database cache held Rs 567.80 from the previous evening and Screener showed Rs 579). On 13.2646 crore shares that is a market capitalisation of Rs 7,727 Cr. From cash and liquid investments of Rs 1,144 Cr we deduct restricted cash (Rs 62 Cr) and lease liabilities (Rs 52.7 Cr) to get net cash of Rs 1,029 Cr and an enterprise value of Rs 6,698 Cr. On FY26 the stock is on 15.2 times earnings and 9.2 times EBITDA; on the trailing four quarters to June 2026 (revenue Rs 4,603 Cr, EBITDA Rs 761 Cr, profit Rs 533 Cr) it is on 14.5 times and 8.8 times. FY26 free cash flow of Rs 477 Cr is a 6.2% yield on the market capitalisation, and FY26 dividends plus buybacks of Rs 339 Cr are a 4.4% cash-return yield. The 52-week range is Rs 366–656.

What the price already assumes (reverse DCF, first)

We start with what has to be true. Using the module’s reverse DCF at a 12% discount rate and 5% terminal growth: on the base growth path (10%, 10%, 9%, 8%, 8%), the enterprise value of Rs 6,698 Cr requires a flat EBITDA margin of 15.9%, against 16.4% in FY26 and 16.4% in Q1 FY27. Turned round, on today’s 16.4% margin the price requires revenue growth of 7.1% a year for five years. That is below the 8–12% market growth management cites and below Q1 FY27’s 17.8%, which is the plain reading: the market is pricing today’s economics with no deterioration and no recovery.

Three cautions on that. First, the answer is very sensitive to terminal growth: with 4% rather than 5% the required revenue growth jumps to 13.5% a year, because terminal value is 69–78% of enterprise value in our cases. Second, a margin required at 8% flat growth is 16.3% and at 5% growth 16.9%, so slower growth must be paid for with a better margin, and the 16.4% already earned is the middle of that range. Third, 12% is our assumption for the cost of capital; at 11% the base DCF is Rs 704 and at 13% Rs 545 (table below).

Weighting the three DCFs 30%, 50% and 20% (we give the bear case more weight than the bull because the ledger shows platform milestones slip and the margin has not risen in three years) gives Rs 606 a share, against a price of Rs 582.55. The price is within 4% of the probability-weighted value and 5% below the base case. That is fair value, with the margin of safety close to zero.

The exit-multiple cross-check, and which method we weight

The exit-multiple method values the business in FY31 at 6, 8 and 11 times EBITDA and discounts back. It gives Rs 404, Rs 599 and Rs 941. It agrees with the DCF at the base (within 3%), is lower in the bear (a DCF with 4% perpetual growth is more generous than a 6-times multiple) and higher in the bull. We weight the DCF and use the exit multiple only to test it, for two reasons: the DCF forces explicit assumptions about cash conversion, working capital and capex, which are where Tanla’s record is least clear; and the exit multiple imports whatever the market will pay in 2031, which for a company whose multiple sits between roughly 3 times (Route, unadjusted) and 9 times (Tanla) is not a number we can anchor. Where the two methods diverge in the tails we show both: for the bull we use the lower (Rs 821), and for the bear we treat Rs 404–450 as the range.

The price at which this analysis changes

At about Rs 450 or below the market would be paying for our bear-case DCF (the bear exit-multiple value is Rs 404), and our value assessment would move from fair to attractive. At about Rs 800 or above the market would need our bull case (DCF Rs 821). Between those, the stock is a hold-the-current-economics bet whose return will be roughly its cash return (about 4% a year) plus growth in per-share earnings. This report does not make a buy or sell call; it states these levels so that a reader can see the distance.

What the valuation does not include

A recovery in the Rs 46.9 Cr tax claim, a ValueFirst International consolidation (management says Rs 150–170 Cr of revenue at a 22% gross margin, which would add perhaps 3–4% to revenue if it closes), and any value for the platform business separate from its contribution to profit. We did not run a sum-of-parts because segment EBITDA is not disclosed. Nor does the valuation include any penalty for the cash drag (section 11) or the tail of a governance event (section 12).
