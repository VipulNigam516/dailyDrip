The one-paragraph version
A steady, lightly levered processor of fillers for paints and plastics that has not grown in its biggest market for two years, run by a family whose pay has outrun profit and whose forecasts have mostly missed: quality B-minus, value full at ₹161

20 Microns grinds calcium carbonate, kaolin, talc and other minerals for formulators; paints are 46% of FY2026 revenue of Rs 953.8 Cr, plastics 26%, rubber 10%. Operating margin has stayed between 12.2% and 14.4% for ten years (12.8% in FY2026), the narrowest range of the seven companies we compared, and debt has fallen from 1.2 times equity to 0.3. But the whole 149 basis-point rise in the pre-tax margin since FY2019 is interest (+277 bp), not operations (−156 bp); paint revenue has been flat at about Rs 438 Cr for two years; FY2026 growth was 4.5% against a start-of-year guide of 15–18%; the first half of FY2027 is about +1%; and a single-source reading of Q2 FY27 puts the margin near 11.3%, below the decade low. Net working capital rose from 18% to 27% of sales because suppliers are paid faster, and cash conversion, 1.48 times profit on the headline, is 0.92 times after interest.

We read all seven earnings calls and tested 37 dated statements against what followed: 5 kept, 13 slipped, 11 broken, 8 unresolved. Management delivers short-horizon operations and misses growth, margin, capex dates and its balance-sheet line; the Rs 100 Cr capex plan has been re-dated five times while 18% growth and 18–20% return targets were set on a 4.5% year. Governance is orderly and clean-audited, with large signals in small amounts: the two Managing Directors’ pay up 3.9 times since FY2022 against profit up 1.95 times, a 2.8% minority in the Nano subsidiary bought from the family at 24 times earnings while the listed company trades at 9, and a 13.6% stake in the family vehicle that owns 24.6% of the company. Both rating agencies sit at A.

Valuation leads with the reverse DCF: the price needs a flat 13.2% operating margin for ten years (the ten-year mean; FY2026 was 12.8%), and at a margin of 12.0% or less no growth rate reaches it, because growth costs 30 paise of working capital per rupee. Scenario DCF: bear ₹93, base ₹136, bull ₹243 (weighted ₹145); exit-multiple cross-check on mid-cycle margins ₹110, ₹152, ₹204 (weighted ₹150); central value ₹147. The value grade becomes Fair below about ₹132 and Stretched above about ₹184. This is not a buy or sell call.

01The business: a micronised-minerals processor that sells half its output to paint makers, whose paint revenue has not grown for two years

What it sells, to whom, what the moat is in management’s words and in the rating agencies’, and who the competitors are (management names none)

20 Microns Ltd (Waghodia, Vadodara; incorporated 1987 by the late Chandresh Parikh and run today by his sons Rajesh, Chairman and Managing Director, and Atil, CEO and Managing Director) grinds industrial minerals to controlled particle sizes and sells them to formulators as fillers, extenders and functional additives. Consolidated revenue was Rs 953.8 Cr in FY2026 (year to March 2026), operating profit before other income Rs 122.5 Cr (12.8%) and profit after tax Rs 66.8 Cr (audited consolidated statements, FY2026 annual report). At the 28 October 2026 close of Rs 161.04 the market capitalisation is Rs 568 Cr, 8.9 times trailing earnings (which include a Q2 FY27 profit taken from Screener’s feed, section 7). The company reports one segment, so every end-market figure below is a management statement, not a segment disclosure.

The product range is calcium carbonate (about 50% of the parent company’s revenue), kaolin (about 22%) and talc (about 10%), with quartz, mica, feldspar, dolomite and red oxide in the remainder; the Nano subsidiary adds opacifiers, waxes, matting agents and other specialty additives. On the 26 October 2023 call the Managing Director gave the split: “Our 20 Microns product revenue mix typically is in the range of 50% for calcium carbonate, about 22% of kaolin, about 10% of talc”. Nano’s FY2026 turnover was Rs 114.9 Cr and its profit after tax Rs 7.4 Cr (Form AOC-1, FY2026 annual report), about 12% of group revenue and 11% of group profit. The group runs eight manufacturing locations and thirteen warehouse and distribution sites in India (FY2026 annual report), has 413 permanent employees, and says utilisation is high: “we are operating at around 85% of our capacities” (450,000 tonnes a year) on the October 2023 call and “the company level utilization as of now stands at ninety percent” in November 2024. Capacity is stretched by contract work: “the regular run of the mill products, which are there, we do it in toll and contract manufacturing”.

Who buys it, and how concentrated it is

The end-market split for FY2026 is paints 46%, polymers 26%, rubber 10%, ceramics 5%, paper 4% and others 9% (FY2026 annual report, management discussion); exports are 14% of revenue, Rs 133 Cr (Q4 FY26 call and deck). The named customers are Asian Paints, Berger, Kansai Nerolac, AkzoNobel, Grasim, Pidilite, Finolex, Kajaria, L&T, ONGC and JK Tyre (every call opening). The FY2026 annual report discloses that two customers each account for more than 10% of sales, with Rs 231.0 Cr of net sales recorded against them (the note words it in the singular, so the figure is probably the two combined): that is 24.2% of revenue, up from Rs 201.8 Cr (22.1%) in FY2025, and it equals 71% of the Rs 41 Cr by which FY2026 revenue grew. CRISIL (13 October 2025) adds that “the top five customers contributing to almost half of its turnover”. A retail note of the September 2020 AGM relayed on ValuePickr put two customers at 38% of revenue (medium: forum post, not a primary record).

There are no customer contracts of the usual kind. The Managing Director on 26 October 2023: “there is no specific contracts that we usually do with most of our end customers”; on 26 May 2026: “we are not bound by any particular contracts for short term or long term”. Offtakes are annual understandings that “keeps changing with the changing demands”. The supplier therefore has volume exposure and price exposure on a monthly cycle, to customers with far larger balance sheets.

What the moat is, as management and the agencies describe it

Management’s claim is a shift from commodity to specialty. The CEO’s letter in the FY2026 annual report says “We are no longer a commodity-driven organisation”, and on the 21 May 2024 call he put value-added products at “about 20% comes from our value-added products and about 80% comes from our industrial minerals”. The rating agencies describe a different mix. ICRA, in all three rationales from April 2024 to October 2025: “nearly 50% of the products under 20ML are commoditised in nature”. Both pictures can be true, since half of the products by count may be well under half by value, but only one of them has a number behind it (section 4 tests it with ten years of margins and finds a stable margin, not a premium one). The agencies give three real strengths: an established position (“dominant player in the segment”), a DSIR-approved R&D centre with more than 40 people, and plants close to customers.

Own mines are a smaller part of the story than the annual report suggests. On the 26 May 2026 call: “approximately about 30% of our total raw material requirement comes from the mines and 70% comes from external sources”, and of the Indian mines “some of the mines are under environmental clearance and some of the mines are operational”. On 11 November 2025 he said that apart from one mine the others were still at the stage of “we have already started to do core drilling to establish the quality of the materials which is available in the mines at a certain depth”. Imports were 40% of raw material in October 2023 and 36–37% in May 2025 (calls); the FY2026 annual report says domestic procurement is 69%. The mining-reserve and plant-count figures that the annual reports and calls give are not consistent with each other (section 9).

Competitors: none named, one real, and the claims cannot be checked

Asked on three calls who the competitors are, management has named none. October 2023: “No, there are no listed players in the competition. We have few international players.” May 2025: “we do not have many or any listed players listed on the Indian Stock Exchange”, with domestic rivals “privately owned family entities”. May 2026: market share “it ranges anywhere from 10% to 30% depending on what kind of things are talking about” and “We will send you a list of, you know, the competitors because not many of them are in the listed space.” No list is on the record (section 9). A 9 March 2018 Nirmal Bang note named English Indian Clays, Ashapura Minechem, Omya India, Wolkem India and Imerys India; only Ashapura is still listed, and it is a bentonite, kaolin and bauxite business seven times larger by market value. Management claims about market share, about the share of customers’ needs it supplies (the 2018 broker note says AkzoNobel sourced about 90% of its micronised minerals from 20 Microns, a broker figure never confirmed) and about being a ‘pioneer’ are flagged here as claims. Section 6 builds the peer set on this basis: one imperfect competitor, one weak comparator and four customers used as demand read-throughs.

What the business is, in one paragraph

A capital- and working-capital-intensive grinding business (net block turnover 3.1 times, trade working capital 27% of sales) with real customer relationships and an unusually steady 12–14% margin, selling about half its volume to paint makers who are themselves in a price war, with no contracts and a small group of large buyers. It is not yet the specialty-materials company its chief executive describes; about 20% of revenue is value-added by his own count.

Management’s framing of the record

On the 26 May 2026 call the CFO said: “Over the last five years, the revenue has nearly been doubled, the PAT has almost tripled”. That is true from FY2021 (sales Rs 483.5 Cr, profit Rs 23.0 Cr: 1.97 times and 2.9 times) and the FY2021 base is the pandemic trough. From FY2022, which the same speaker used a few sentences later (“the revenue increased from 613 crores in FY22 to 954 crores in FY26”), sales are 1.56 times and profit 1.93 times: a sales CAGR of 11.7% and a profit CAGR of 17.9%. The same presentation described operating cash flow as having “increased sharply to 103.6 CR” without mentioning that the FY2025 figure was Rs 31.8 Cr, which is the figure it was measured against. The honest summary is: steady growth around 10–12% a year, cash flow that depends on inventory timing, and a margin that has not moved.

The call-by-call colour on the margin: in the quarter to September 2023, when the margin touched 15%, the company attributed it to “reduced power and fuel expenses and improved negotiations for freight charges”, that is, to input prices rather than to mix; in November 2024 the margin slipped because “the higher freight costs for the incoming raw material” “do not get translated in terms of the price increase that we received from our customers at times”; and in the Q1 FY26 results deck (1 August 2025) it wrote that “Elevated freight costs on imported raw materials exerted significant margin pressures, as these increases could not be fully passed on to customers.” The margin is held in a band by cost swings netting out, which is what the rest of this report tests.

What the record does not contain

The three years before this series (FY2014–FY2016) were a restructuring: EBITDA margin 9.2%, 8.4% and 12.8% and net losses in FY2014–15 (Nirmal Bang, 9 March 2018), an ICRA ‘D’ default-grade event in 2014 (relayed by a forum poster, medium) and a corporate debt restructuring that ended in September 2020 (ICRA). Ten years of ‘steady margin’ is a post-restructuring ten years. There are no transcripts between June 2019 and October 2023, so management’s account of the pandemic, the restructuring exit and the 2022 freight shock exists only in annual reports and rating rationales.

04Margins: a 12–14% band held for ten years by passing costs through with a lag, no sign of pricing power, and a Q2 FY27 reading below the band

The bridge, the peer test, what management says drives the margin, and why the specialty mix has not lifted it

The pre-tax margin rose from 8.0% to 9.5% between FY2019 and FY2026. The operating margin contributed −156 basis points of that. Interest contributed +277. The bridge for FY2022–FY2026, the window management uses, gives the same answer: operating −11, interest +150. The profit-growth story told on the calls (“the PAT has almost tripled”) is therefore a story about debt, and debt is now too small to repeat it: finance cost is Rs 17.1 Cr (including Rs 4.1 Cr of bill-discounting charges) on Rs 156 Cr of debt including leases, and the interest line cannot fall by another 277 basis points.

The module’s blunt rule gives opposite answers on the two windows, which is itself information. Over FY2019–2026 four of six comparators moved the same way, but the two biggest moves (Ashapura +11.4 points, GMDC −12.0) are mining-cycle swings, and the three paint makers moved −0.7, 0.0 and −1.8. Over FY2022–2026 the paint makers recovered 0.2 to 2.3 points from a raw-material-squeezed FY2022 while 20 Microns, which never suffered the squeeze, stayed flat. Our reading: no company-specific erosion of margin, no participation in the customers’ margin recovery, and a level that is 2.6 to 6.0 points below three of the four customers. The test of whether this is a moat follows.

Resilience. In the FY2022–23 input-cost shock the worst one-year margin drop was −0.7 points for 20 Microns against a peer median of −4.3 (Asian Paints −5.9, Berger −2.2, Kansai −6.8, Finolex −15.4); in FY2025–26 −0.7 against −2.6. In FY2020 it did worse than the paint makers, whose worst year across FY2020–21 was still a gain of 0.8 to 1.4 points, while 20 Microns lost 1.9 (cause not found, section 2). Across all six stress years its worst one-year drop was −1.9 points against a peer median of −9.8 (−5.9 to −6.8 for the three paint makers). That is the observable form of a moat in a cyclical industry, with one caveat that changes its meaning: a margin that does not move when costs swing is also what a cost-plus supplier looks like, and the level (12–13%) is below the customers’. Resilient, then, but not a premium.

What management says drives the margin

Four drivers recur on the calls. Energy and freight. The 15% quarter of September 2023 was power, fuel and freight (quoted in section 3). In the 2026 energy shock, the Q1 FY27 deck (6 August 2026) says “Raw material consumption as a percentage of revenue improved both year-on-year and sequentially, helping offset the sharp increase in furnace oil, gas and distribution costs during the quarter.” Pass-through lag. On 26 May 2026: “in many cases we do pass it on to our customers, but it doesn’t happen immediately”, after listing “fuel hikes to gas hikes to foreign exchange hikes” and “So we have a bundled effect.” Sourcing. The Q2 FY26 margin of 13.8% was put down to “continued cost initiatives, operating discipline and more efficient sourcing”. Mix. The Managing Director on 26 October 2023: “the margins usually get driven by the product mix in our scenario”; value-added products were to lift the margin. They have not, as the next two paragraphs show.

The specialty mix is not visible in the margin. Nano is the value-added vehicle. Its FY2026 profit before tax was Rs 9.7 Cr on turnover of Rs 114.9 Cr, 8.5%, against 9.6% for the group (Form AOC-1 and consolidated statements, FY2026 annual report); profit after tax margin 6.4% against 7.0%. On the 5 November 2024 call Nano’s margins were put at “12 to 13 %”, the same as the group’s, and the two newest partial-replacement products (Lithomer, Zinkomer) at “under the 12 to 13 % range”. In October 2023 the Managing Director explained why Nano was not yet earning more: “we are currently operating with older machinery, the yield which is there is not as per the required ratio”. In H1 FY26 he reported lower raw-material cost in Nano, “we have significantly decreased our raw material cost in Nano”. The June 2019 call (OCR-damaged scan, medium confidence, not quoted) said specialty chemicals carry the best margins and calcium carbonate the lowest; seven years later the specialty subsidiary earns about the group margin on a revenue base that is 12% of the group.

Pricing power: what the paint makers are doing

On the 11 November 2025 call a questioner asked whether rising competition in paints lets 20 Microns improve margins by taking share. The answer is the clearest statement of the bargaining position in the corpus:

— Management reply to an analyst question, Q2 FY26 earnings call, 11 November 2025 (the transcript does not label the speaker; Atil Parikh, Managing Director, answered the other questions in this part of the call)

The answer continues: “we work on a very selective approach when it comes to paint industries in terms of our product offerings to sustain our EBITDA margins currently”. Receivables, in analysts’ words and the company’s

The one analyst who pressed the point was a retail investor on the 5 November 2024 call: “the paid receivables have increased a lot like from 125 to 160 in just half a year which is a bit concerning because it has been increasing gradually”. The finance controller’s answer: “if you look at holding it it’s not much changes if you compare holding days with march 24 year then it is around 53 days and there is not much change”. His basis is the balance over annualised latest-quarter sales, and the balance sheets bear him out on it: 54 days at March 2024 (Rs 125.5 Cr), 62 at September 2024, 58 at March 2025 (Rs 143.2 Cr) and 62 at March 2026. On full-year sales the same balances read 59, 57 and 68 days. For FY2026 receivables reached Rs 176.7 Cr (+23% on sales +4.5%). Three facts qualify the alarm: March-quarter sales were +14.8%, the Managing Director’s explanation being customers building stock because of “the war situation” (26 May 2026); 75% of the balance was not yet due; and only 1% was over six months (FY2026 annual report, note 11). One fact aggravates it: the Q1 FY27 revenue of Rs 244.7 Cr was −1.0% year on year, so the March stock-up may have been pulled forward.

Bill discounting. Finance cost includes Rs 4.1 Cr of hundi (bill) discounting charges in FY2026 and Rs 5.3 Cr in FY2025 (note 37). The Managing Director on 26 October 2023: “we have discounting that we only do with many of the big customers, that is also a significant component as part of that whole debt cycle”; asked why, “That is because we have to service our major customers or the bigger customers who have a significant amount of share”. The annual report does not state the amount of bills discounted or whether they are derecognised: at a charge of Rs 4.1 Cr and a 9–12% rate, the outstanding stock would be roughly Rs 35–46 Cr, 20–26% of receivables (our arithmetic; the rate is an assumption). Whether reported receivables would be higher if discounted bills were counted is not found in retrieved filings. This is one reason the receivable-days comparison with peers in section 6 should be read with care.

Inventory

Consolidated inventory rose 42% in FY2025 (Rs 112.8 Cr to Rs 159.8 Cr) and fell 11% in FY2026 (to Rs 142.2 Cr). The finance controller on 5 November 2024: “we have kept material looking to future demand and we have reserved that material so that operational cash flow is being used in that inventory”. Management on 27 May 2025: “when you are dependent on imports, you have to build up on inventory because there is you know dependency on the freight factor” and “just in time does not work for these kind of minerals”. Both statements describe a deliberate buffer; the second was given after an inventory rise of 42% as the reason for it. ICRA’s 15 October 2025 rationale confirms the effect on working capital (“as the inventory days rose because of increased inventory stock at its warehouses across India”). The CFO’s 26 May 2026 slide, “Inventory turnover has improved steadily over the years” (5.8 to 8.3 times), uses the ratio reported in the annual report (8.27 in FY2026 and 8.09 in FY2025); revenue over the average of the year-end inventories in the audited balance sheets gives 6.3 and 6.7 for the same years. The ratio’s denominator is not defined beyond ‘average inventory’, and the difference is not explained in retrieved filings. Our days are based on year-end balances.

What FY2026’s Rs 103.6 Cr of operating cash flow was

The consolidated cash-flow statement shows operating profit before working-capital changes of Rs 126.6 Cr, a net working-capital change of +Rs 0.2 Cr (receivables −Rs 33.3 Cr; inventory +Rs 17.5 Cr; other assets +Rs 17.6 Cr; payables −Rs 7.5 Cr; other liabilities and provisions +Rs 5.8 Cr) and tax paid of Rs 23.3 Cr. So FY2026’s strong cash flow was ordinary earnings with no working-capital windfall; the 1.55 times profit it represents is mostly the addback of Rs 17 Cr of interest and Rs 21 Cr of depreciation. After interest the conversion is 1.29 times in FY2026, 0.22 times in FY2025, 0.68 in FY2024 and 1.09 in FY2023. The more honest cash-conversion record is that growth has taken, on average, close to everything the business earns.

Liquidity: two agencies, two pictures

ICRA reports fund-based limits utilised at 98% at 31 March 2025 and 95% at 30 June 2025; CRISIL reports an average of 70% over the twelve months to June 2025. A point-in-time and a twelve-month average can both be right, and both mean the cash-credit lines are close to full at the working-capital peak. Consolidated cash credit was Rs 84.7 Cr at March 2026 against Rs 106.8 Cr a year earlier (FY2026 annual report, note 25), which is where the FY2026 cash flow went.

07Growth: 10–12% a year for a decade, 15–18% promised three years running, 4.5% delivered in FY2026, and a plan for 18% a year whose first half-year grew 1%

Where growth came from, how it was guided, what the new plan requires, and how management answers questions about external risks

Sales grew 10.3% a year over FY2019–26 and 11.7% over FY2022–26. Management’s habit is to guide 15–18%. On the 26 October 2023 call, with first-half growth at 4.5%, it said of the 15–18% range for the full year “Yes, we will be able to do that. And yes, we shall.”, and of the following years “at least for the next two year 15% to 18% growth levels is the ideal goal for us”. The second half of FY2024 would have needed +27% to reach +15%; it delivered +18% and the year closed at +10.8%.

FY2026. The growth guide was 15–18% on 27 May 2025 (“keeping a 15 to 18% target of growth is something that we are really looking at and hopefully we should be on track with that considering the external conditions also”), 13–15% on 1 August 2025 (“We remain confident in achieving our 13-15% revenue growth guidance for FY26.”) and on 11 November 2025, after a quarter of −3.9%, an analyst asked whether the company was still on track for 13%. The answer: “the target would continue to remain the same that we had anticipated at the beginning of the year”, and then “we should hopefully achieve the targeted growth rate of 13% and about”. The start-of-year target was 15–18%, not 13%. The year closed at 4.5%.

Where the FY2026 growth came from

Revenue rose Rs 41 Cr. Paints, 46% of revenue, contributed nothing (section 1). Polymers added about Rs 20 Cr (+8.7%) and rubber about Rs 13 Cr (+16%), from the shares the company states (the same rates appear in a forum poster’s reading of the Q4 FY26 deck, medium). Exports were 14% of revenue (Q4 FY26 call), about Rs 133 Cr against about Rs 118 Cr at 13% in FY2025 (our arithmetic from the stated shares). Two customers account for Rs 231 Cr of sales, Rs 29 Cr more than in FY2025 and 71% of the increase. New products are small: “about 4 to 5% is what the contribution usually comes in from the new products”, against the “That will go up to 10%.” of October 2023. The Managing Director gave the cause of the strong March quarter in these words: “with the war situation, many people try to you know build up on the capacity so that based upon the raw materials that they were already holding and that kind of led to an upscale demand for the entire quarter”. That is a customer inventory build, and the first quarter after it, Q1 FY27, fell 1.0%.

The new plan, and what it would take

On 26 May 2026, the day after FY2026 results, the CFO set out the plan: “around 18% of revenue CAGR growth, 200 approx. BPS margin expansion, ROCE improvement between 18 to 20%” over the next three years, “provided that the prevailing economic condition remains stable”, and added that “the Company shall modify or defer its phase plans accordingly”. The Q1 FY27 deck repeats 18%, 200–250 bp and ROCE of 18–20% on a 24-month, Rs 100 Cr capex programme (40% Malaysia, 30% India, 15% the Sievert JV, 15% R&D and ESG), with a 20%-plus share of high-value products by FY2030.

The arithmetic: 18% a year from Rs 953.8 Cr is Rs 1,567 Cr in FY2029, +Rs 613 Cr, with a margin of 15–15.4% (12.9% plus 2.0–2.5 points). At the incremental working-capital intensity of 0.30 observed over four and five years that growth ties up about Rs 184 Cr of working capital before the Rs 100 Cr of capex. The best three-year CAGR in the series outside the post-pandemic rebound (FY2021–24 was 17.2% from the pandemic trough) is 14.2% (FY2022–25); a 15% margin has never been reached in ten years (peak 14.4%). The plan was announced in February 2026 (filing as relayed by a forum poster and the corpus news file: medium) when nine-month growth was 1.1% (Q3 FY26 deck), repeated when FY2026 closed at 4.5%, and the first half of FY2027 is tracking +1.0%. To pass Rs 1,000 Cr in FY2027, which the Managing Director hoped for on 26 May (“cross the thousand crore benchmark and milestone hopefully in this financial year”), the second half would need about +9% on a Rs 476 Cr base. The annual report’s chief executive’s letter, published on 9 July 2026, is less sure: “While we may fall marginally short this year, the trajectory is firmly in place.” (Whether ‘this year’ means FY2026 or FY2027 is not stated; Rs 953.8 Cr is 4.6% short of Rs 1,000 Cr.)

Why the plan does not carry the same weight as a forecast

Every element is conditional on the geopolitical situation staying stable, the capex plan has been re-dated five times (section 9), no capital commitments were outstanding on 31 March 2026 (Rs 2.9 lakh, against Rs 140.3 lakh a year earlier), and the guidance for FY2027 itself was not given. The plan is best read as a statement of intent and ROCE target, not as an estimate.

Framing of external risks

Management answers named external risks with qualitative statements and rarely with numbers. On US tariffs (11 November 2025): “there is no direct impact on the US tariffs because our exposure to the US markets is very limited”, with an indirect effect via supply chains that is “not in a very significant way”. On China (21 May 2024) the claim was that customers “have moved to Indian products” because of trust, logistics and price, and that an anti-dumping duty on Chinese goods “could maybe benefit 20 Microns to some extent” (no quantification; a TiO2 duty was imposed in May 2025, quashed in September 2025 and reportedly reimposed in October 2026, medium). On the 2026 energy shock (26 May 2026) the answer was “our teams are efficient enough managing those tough situations” and, on cost recovery, “it takes it’s, you know, a few days to few weeks before those things get implemented”. None of the three answers gives a rupee figure for the exposure.

The pattern

Kept: operational events within the company’s control and within a year: the Malaysian quarries starting, the first Sievert plant, an export band, the one year in which demand cooperated (FY2025 growth). Slipped: anything with a capex number or a date attached, which has been re-based almost every time (capex scope and timing, the Malaysian plant, the Sievert announcement, the EV product). Broken: revenue guides in the two years the paint cycle was weak, every margin ambition above 13.5%, the 2019 promoter-holding promise, the Nano capex pause and the working-capital statement of May 2024. The reliable portion is the narrower one: what the company does with its own plants and balance sheet over months. The unreliable portion is exactly the part an investor is asked to pay for, which is a growth path and a margin path.

How management frames bad news

Four habits are visible. (1) Guidance is cut by restating the base. FY2026’s 15–18% became 13–15% and then “13% and about” with the later figure called the original target (section 7); FY2027’s guide was not given, with the formula that the company has “not withdrawn” it. (2) The base year is chosen to flatter. Revenue “nearly been doubled” is measured from the pandemic year. (3) The headline metric is the favourable one. The inventory turnover ratio and the operating cash flow of Rs 103.6 Cr were highlighted while the preceding year’s Rs 31.8 Cr and the consolidated balance-sheet turnover were not. (4) Weak quarters are called discipline. The Q1 FY27 deck, with revenue at −1.0%, says the company “remained focused on maintaining operational stability rather than pursuing low-margin growth”, and on 26 May 2026 the CFO said the company “avoided aggressive low margin business” in a quarter of +14.8% growth. None of this is concealment; each figure is in the filings. It is selection.

Which method we weight, and why

We weight the cash-flow value and the multiple-based value equally. The cash-flow value is the more conservative because it charges the business for what growth actually costs here: working capital that has risen from 18% to 27% of sales and capex that the company says is 4% of revenue against depreciation of 2.2%. A P/E frame assumes both away. The multiple method is less bearish because the stock has traded at 4.0 to 6.8 times EBITDA and at 5.3 times now, in the middle of that range. Two-thirds of the DCF value is terminal, so the discount rate and terminal growth matter as much as the margin: terminal growth of 4% instead of 5% takes the base case from ₹136 to ₹124.
