The one-paragraph version
A good lead-acid franchise with a falling margin, a late and cash-hungry cell build, and a price that needs the margin to recover: quality B−, value full, no margin of safety

Amara Raja makes car, two-wheeler, industrial and telecom batteries (94% of Rs 13,814 Cr FY26 revenue), is the second-largest in India after Exide, and has borrowed almost nothing. Operating margin was 14.2% in FY24, 10.9% in FY26 and 9.6% in Q1 FY27; the fall is operating, not financial: warranty cost is 1.4 of the 3.3 points, the new-energy segment lost Rs 135 Cr in FY26 against a profit of Rs 28 Cr two years earlier, and Exide, the one like-for-like peer, held its margin. Reported FY26 profit of Rs 896 Cr includes a Rs 255 Cr fire-insurance gain; without it profit is about Rs 706 Cr.

We read all 19 earnings calls and scored 42 dated or quantified statements against what followed: 5 kept, 18 slipped, 10 broken, 9 unresolved. None of the 16 statements about plant dates, capex or project size was kept, and the first-cell start date has been re-dated eight times in eleven dated statements. Governance process is strong (clean opinions, a minority vote on the Mangal demerger), but Rs 1,436 Cr a year is bought from promoter-group suppliers on the consolidated basis and the three promoter-directors take 14% of profit. The pollution-board closure orders were revoked in July 2026, which removes the largest tail risk by the company’s own account.

Valuation leads with the reverse DCF: the price needs a flat 14.0% consolidated margin for ten years, or a 11.6–12.2% lead-acid margin with lithium valued at the Rs 1,500–2,500 Cr put in. Scenario DCF: bear Rs 314, base Rs 602, bull Rs 988 (weighted Rs 626); exit multiple on a 12% margin Rs 803; central value Rs 685, range Rs 517–803. The view changes below about Rs 620 (fair) and Rs 510 (cheap) and above about Rs 860 (stretched). This is not a buy or sell call.

01The business: India’s second-largest lead-acid battery maker, 95% of sales still lead-acid, now funding a lithium cell build with the cash it makes

What it sells, to whom, what management says the moat is, and where the profit comes from

Amara Raja Energy & Mobility (formerly Amara Raja Batteries; BSE 500008, NSE symbol ARE&M, ISIN INE885A01032; Tirupati and Chittoor in Andhra Pradesh, a lithium campus at Divitipally in Telangana) made consolidated revenue of Rs 13,814 Cr in FY26. The segment note splits it into lead-acid batteries and allied products at Rs 13,005 Cr (94.1%) and new energy (lithium packs, chargers, storage systems) at Rs 809 Cr (5.9%). The audited segment note is the source of this split. Price Rs 713.10 on 27 October 2026, 18.30 crore shares, market capitalisation Rs 13,052 Cr; borrowings plus leases Rs 406 Cr against Rs 371 Cr of cash and current investments, so net debt is Rs 36 Cr.

CRISIL describes the company as “the largest player in this segment after Exide Industries Ltd” in all six of its rationales since 2022, with 100,000-plus points of sale, 1,000-plus PowerZone stores and 23 branches. Management’s own share estimates, none independently verified, are about 33–36% of the four-wheeler aftermarket, 35–40% of the two-wheeler aftermarket, 35% of four-wheeler and 25% of two-wheeler OEM supply, 42–45% of industrial UPS and about 55–60% of telecom (lead and lithium combined) (calls of 5 Aug 2024, 10 Feb 2025 and 12 Feb 2026). Automotive is about 70% of lead-acid revenue and industrial 30%; exports are about 12%; telecom lead-acid is now “less than 5%” of revenue (Q3 FY26 call), down from 24% in FY17 (Business Standard, medium confidence).

Two facts shape everything that follows. First, the company does not disclose segment margins within lead-acid: “We don't discuss the margin splits at the subsegment level in these calls” (30 May 2024). Second, demand is not the problem. Total domestic vehicle sales were 28.3 million in FY26 (SIAM, +10.4%) and OEM battery volumes grew 24% (four-wheeler) and more than 35% (two-wheeler) in Q1 FY27. The problem is what the company keeps of each sale: the consolidated operating margin before other income has gone from 14.2% in FY24 to 10.9% in FY26 and 9.6% in Q1 FY27, while capital employed has been growing into a cell plant that sells nothing yet.

What the moat is, in management’s words, and how it tests

“considering our facilities being located at the same location, there is a huge amount of scale advantage we derive in terms of whether it is employee cost or whether it is through the other manufacturing expenses”

Y. Delli Babu, CFO, Q4 FY25 call (30 May 2025), asked why Amara’s gross margin was better than its competitor’s

The moat claimed is aftermarket mix, single-site scale, and a distribution network of franchisees and retailers. The same single-site concentration is CRISIL’s first-named weakness: both plants are in Andhra Pradesh and “the concentration of operations in a single state exposes the company to risks relating to natural calamities” (CRISIL 3 Nov 2023); a January 2023 fire at the Chittoor tubular plant (loss recognised Rs 438.6 Cr, Rs 700 Cr of annual revenue) and pollution-board closure orders (section 12) are the two times that exposure has been tested. A moat is an observable fact, not a story: the company gives up less than its rivals when conditions turn. Section 6 tests that and finds the opposite in the one comparison that matters.

The plant and the competition

Capacity at August 2024 was about 21 million four-wheeler and 32 million two-wheeler batteries a year, running at 85–90% utilisation, plus industrial capacity of about 2 billion ampere-hours, where the old telecom (LVRLA) line runs at 65–70% and has a net block of about Rs 50 Cr (Q3 FY25 call, 10 Feb 2025). Management says throughput projects added 5–6% of capacity without capex. Lead is about 65–70% of the raw-material basket, plastics about 10%, and lead plus alloys about 70% of material cost (Q4 FY26 call). The company consumes about 3 lakh tonnes of lead a year, 70–75% from recycled sources (30 May 2024). Its own refinery at Cheyyar began in December 2024 at 50,000 t; battery breaking is still in trial production.

On the calls competitors are almost never named. In 19 calls the name Exide is spoken five times in all (analysts refer to “a competitor” six more times in the Q4 FY25 call alone), and the one product question about Exide went unanswered: asked whether Amara was the only maker of AGM batteries, the CFO said “I really don't know for sure whether Exide is making it or not” (10 Feb 2025). Livguard, Luminous, Okaya and Microtek are never named. The only direct listed rival is Exide; the others in section 6 are context comparators.

Finding

The cash-quality test is passed and the capital-allocation test is not. Operating cash flow of 1.35 times profit says the earnings are real; free cash flow after interest and dividends of about nil over eight years, and −Rs 547 Cr in the last two, says the company is now spending more than the lead-acid business earns. The new-energy programme is being paid for from the balance sheet, not from surplus.

Part one, FY17–FY21: a 25–29% return on capital and margin of 15–16%

Under the former team (CEO Vijayanand, CFO Raghavendra), standalone sales grew from Rs 5,317 Cr to Rs 7,150 Cr in four years, 7.7% a year, with company-reported EBITDA margins of 16.5%, 15.4%, 14.6% and 16.4% and a standalone ROCE of 29%, 25%, 23%, 23%. FY18 was the lead shock (LME USD 2,375, +18.6%): the Nov 2017 call implies Q1 FY18 at about 12.8% (EBITDA Rs 192 Cr on Rs 1,497 Cr of sales) after the July 2017 GST destocking, with price rises made “at least to 6 to 7 times” in twelve months and margin back at 16.7% in Q2; standalone operating cash flow fell to Rs 322 Cr, 68% of profit, as inventory absorbed the price rise. The company’s response to a lead spike was therefore price, repeatedly, and it worked: FY18 profit was Rs 471 Cr against Rs 478 Cr. FY20 and FY21 were helped by the opposite: a vehicle-volume collapse (SIAM −18% and −14%) that shifted mix to the higher-margin replacement market. That is a different stress from the present one.

Part two, FY22–FY24: the 2021 strategy, the fire, the merger, the restored margin

In June 2021 the founder-chairman retired, the Gourineni nephews became executive directors, and the company announced an “Energy & Mobility” strategy: lead-acid “value maximization” at a 15–17% growth target and a lithium cell business of 8–12 GWh. FY22 margin fell to 11.9% (−376 bp on the database series; operating share of the fall 88%). FY23 brought the Chittoor tubular-plant fire on 30 January 2023, the Mangal plastics scheme (restating FY23 and FY24 and adding Rs 428 Cr of goodwill) and the sale of 14% by Clarios in July 2023. By FY24 consolidated margin was back to 14.2% on a lead price near Rs 2 lakh a tonne, sales had reached Rs 11,708 Cr and profit Rs 934 Cr. That year is the high-water mark of the record.

Part three, FY25–FY26: record volumes, falling margin, and a reported profit that is not the earning power

Sales grew 9.7% and 7.5% while consolidated margin fell to 12.6% and 10.9%. Reported profit after tax was Rs 945 Cr and Rs 896 Cr (−5%). That headline hides two one-offs. Strip the exceptional items out and pre-tax profit was Rs 1,162 Cr in FY25 and Rs 952 Cr in FY26, down 18%; profit after tax is about Rs 862 Cr and Rs 706 Cr. The FY26 reported figure includes Rs 181.2 Cr from the final fire-insurance settlement and Rs 121.8 Cr of business-interruption cover, less Rs 47.6 Cr of labour-code gratuity (consolidated note 29). In the same year the producing business’s depreciation rose 15.5% and the new-energy segment lost Rs 135 Cr (section 4). The market’s trailing multiple is 14.6 times reported profit and 18.5 times the profit left when the exceptional items are removed.

Where the 330 basis points went, whether it is the company or the industry, and whether the company has pricing power

Did the business get worse, or the balance sheet?

Between FY24 and FY26 the consolidated pre-tax margin as reported fell from 10.7% to 8.7% (−194 bp). The bridge from margin_bridge shows why it looks milder than it is: EBITDA margin −330 bp, depreciation −26 bp, interest −3 bp, and other income +165 bp, which is the insurance settlement. Remove the exceptional items (Rs 111 Cr in FY25, Rs 255 Cr in FY26) and the pre-tax margin is 6.9%, a fall of 379 bp, of which operating margin is 87%. Nothing on the balance sheet helped: interest is trivial, depreciation rose, and underlying other income fell 20 bp. Over seven years (FY19–FY26, same basis) the pre-tax margin is down 386 bp, EBITDA −316 bp and depreciation −55 bp. This is an operating decline in a business that has not been levered or de-levered.

Management’s explanation, and a test of it

“we have seen 16-17% EBITDA margins also when the lead was around 1,50,000 or even less”

Y. Delli Babu, CFO, Q2 FY24 call (1 Nov 2023), answering why 15–16% had not returned

The company’s position since 2021 is that margin percentage is diluted by the higher rupee lead price (Rs 1.5 lakh a tonne three years ago, Rs 2.1–2.2 lakh now, Feb and May 2026), that rupees earned per battery are unchanged, and that 14–16% belongs to a lead price of Rs 1.50–1.80 lakh. The first test is dollar lead: it averaged USD 2,120 in FY24 and 1,952 in FY26 (World Bank), so the whole lead rise in rupees is the rupee itself (84 in Nov 2024, 87.5–88 in Feb 2025 per the calls). The second test is arithmetic. If only the denominator had grown, rupee EBITDA would be flat or higher while the percentage fell. It is lower: Rs 1,662 Cr in FY24, Rs 1,505 Cr in FY26, down 9% on sales up 18%. The denominator argument explains the FY22–FY23 gap; it does not explain FY24 to FY26. What is left is warranty, people, the lithium build and, as the company concedes, pass-through that lags.

Is there pricing power?

“you are inviting competition from elsewhere”

Y. Delli Babu, Q3 FY26 call (12 Feb 2026), replying to an IIFL analyst who put the two producers’ blended margin at about 15% before Covid and about 11% now and asked if the industry could get back to it

The CFO’s full answer is that margins of 16–17% on this lead base would invite competitors, which “the industry was able to successfully avoid so far”; he states a company aim of 13–14% and declines to speak for the industry. OEM contracts pass lead through automatically but only lead, with a month’s lag; aftermarket prices move after a “sustained” change and, as the CFO said in 2024, “we're not quick to react as prices fall”. The record since April 2025 is a series of increases (2%, 5–6%, 3%, 2–3%) that did not restore margin, and Q1 FY27 shows 9.6%. CRISIL says the same in every rationale: “During periods of subdued end-market demand, the increase in lead prices cannot be fully transferred to end customers, especially in the aftermarket segment” (30 Mar 2026). There is no evidence of pricing power beyond lead pass-through.

Resilience in the stress years

Stress years are a property of the industry: FY22 (lead +22% in dollars) and the FY25–FY26 cost shock (industry brief, section 6). resilience() measures the largest one-year fall in operating margin. Amara gave up 3.8 points in FY22 against Exide’s 2.2; in FY25–FY26 it gave up 1.7 points in its worst year against Exide’s 0.4. Peer median fall across the four peers and the three stress years is 0.7 points, so the test returns outperformed = false. On this evidence the franchise is strong in share and weaker than Exide’s in margin defence. FY18 cannot be tested on the consolidated series (it starts in FY19); standalone company-reported margin fell 110 bp that year (16.45% to 15.35%), against Exide’s 130 bp on the Screener series, which is roughly equal.

Gross and net views, the database defect, and the contradiction between the call and the balance sheet

The database field for inventory is negative or erratic (FY20 −52, FY23 −52, FY22 +322 against a balance-sheet figure of Rs 1,805 Cr); Screener’s inventory defect appears in the peer pulls as well (Exide FY26 shows Rs 88 Cr). Every inventory figure here is therefore taken from the consolidated balance sheets in the annual reports, with receivables and payables from the database (which match the filings). What the calls say about competitors

Management’s view of competition has two parts. In lead-acid, telecom has “only 3 players” and the aftermarket unorganised share is “10% to 15%” and no longer falling (Aug 2025), so the structural gains from GST and recycling rules are done. In lithium it is the reverse: telecom packs have “6 to 7” makers, all importing cells, and cells are expected to be, in the CFO’s words, “at the best a 3 player to 4 player kind of a market on the cell side” with price set by Chinese imports at a 15–20% cost disadvantage. In September 2022 the chairman said, of cell making, “nobody who is actually manufacturing any cells in India today”; by August 2026 the three groups that were named as entrants (Reliance, Tata, Ola) or have since commissioned (Exide) are in or near production, and Amara’s first line is one to two years away.

Market growth

India’s lead-acid market is put by the company at USD 4.6 billion growing to 5.8 billion in five years, a 5% rate that management calls conservative (Q4 FY25 deck, call of 30 May 2025); an earlier Rs 36,500 Cr figure in the FY21 report stayed at about Rs 36,000–37,000 Cr in the FY25 report, which an analyst noticed on 18 Aug 2025 (the CFO said his own estimate was about Rs 40,000 Cr). Replacement demand lags vehicle production by about three years: “for the aftermarket business to reflect whatever OEM growth that is being happening today, it will take at least 3 years hence” (12 Feb 2026). SIAM total domestic vehicle sales fell 18%, 14% and 5% in FY20–FY22 and rose 20%, 13%, 7% and 10% in FY23–FY26, so the replacement parc growth that should come from the FY23–FY26 sales has not yet arrived; that is a real reason for expecting mid-to-high single-digit aftermarket growth to continue. The offsetting risk is electrification: two-wheeler EV share of registrations was 11.6% in Sep 2026 (EVreporter, medium confidence).

The lithium programme in its own dates

The scope has changed with the date. Phase 1 capex was Rs 1,300 Cr (May 2023), Rs 1,500 Cr (Aug 2023), Rs 2,000 Cr for three projects (Aug 2024), Rs 2,000–2,500 Cr (May 2025) and “initial risk capital that we agreed was about INR2,500 crores” for five facilities (Aug 2026). The size of Giga 1 went from 2 GWh (May 2023) to “1 gigawatt hour” (Aug 2025) to “not going to be more than 2 gigawatt hour” (Nov 2025) to a 2 GWh line (May 2026). The route to LFP, announced as a Gotion licence on 28 Jun 2024 with 4–6 GWh of capacity to follow, ended in May 2026 with “sharing of technology, licensing technology is something that's been largely discouraged by the Chinese government”. On 11 Aug 2026 an analyst said the Gotion tie-up “did not go through as planned” and the answer was that a broad arrangement with a Chinese party is not possible; the plan is now “prioritize an ESS cell over a standard EV cell”. The 80% vehicle, 20% storage plan has become two-thirds and one-third (26 May 2026).

Cell economics as guided, and what the market did

The business case has been stable in form: a loss until scale, then 10–12% EBITDA and a “low double-digit” ROCE at 8–10 GWh, with asset turns of 1.0–1.4 (May 2023 to May 2026). It was built on a cell price of USD 70–75 per kWh (Jun 2024). Imported LFP cells were USD 50–55 and NMC about USD 60 in May 2025, 20–30% below the planning price, with a day-one cost disadvantage of “China plus 15% to 20%”. Capex per GWh has fallen 20–25% from USD 55–60 million, which offsets some of it. Offtake is the open question. Asked in May 2026 who the incremental buyer is when capacity goes from 2 to 16 GWh and whether there is binding offtake, the answer was that for ESS cells “we have to see our own ESS system as the offtaker in this case”; for vehicles, safeguards “akin to” take-or-pay are the aim, and no firm order is disclosed (the Ather and Piaggio memoranda of Aug 2024 are non-binding). The Rs 9,500 Cr, 16 GWh plan remains “unchanged” in May and “can definitely change” in August.

How management frames risk, and two sidesteps

On named external risks management quantifies when it can: plastics are 10% of material cost and “almost 40% kind of price increase is possible if this kind of increasing momentum will continue”, and a further 2–3% price rise would still not recover the pressure. It does not when it cannot: warranty (above), and the China export restriction on cathode and graphite, where the answer to a direct question was “I am not aware of any specific restrictions” about a restriction the same analyst said had been announced in October 2025 and deferred. The claim to be “the number one low-voltage solution supplier in India” (26 May 2026) is a self-comparison for which no data is offered and is treated as a claim.

15Verdict: a B-minus business at a full price, and the specific numbers that would change each grade

Two grades on separate axes: quality (is this a good business and a trustworthy team?) and value (is the price low relative to what it is worth?). This is not a buy or sell call.

The four findings

  1. The problem is the operating margin, and it is the company’s own. Consolidated operating margin fell from 14.2% (FY24) to 10.9% (FY26) and 9.6% in Q1 FY27; the fall was entirely operating (warranty cost is 1.4 of the 3.3 points, the new-energy loss went from +Rs 28 Cr to −Rs 135 Cr), and Exide, the only like-for-like peer, held its margin (10.9% to 10.4%). The margin premium over Exide has gone from 3.5 points to 0.5 (section 6).
  2. Reported profit overstates earning power. FY26 reported profit after tax of Rs 896 Cr includes Rs 255 Cr of exceptional income (fire insurance settlement); without it profit after tax is about Rs 706 Cr, and pre-tax profit fell 18% (Rs 1,162 Cr to Rs 952 Cr). The stock is 14.6 times the first and 18.5 times the second (section 3).
  3. Cash is real but is all spent. Operating cash flow has been 1.35 times profit over eight years, but after capex and dividends the company kept Rs 70 Cr in total over FY19–FY26 and lost Rs 547 Cr in FY25–FY26. FY27 capex is guided at Rs 1,500–1,700 Cr against about Rs 950 Cr of cash after dividends, so external debt arrives in H2 FY27 (sections 2, 11).

Five of 42 dated statements were kept and none of the 16 about plant dates, capex or scope; the first-cell date has been re-dated eight times in eleven statements. The governance process is strong (clean opinions, vote on the demerger, AOC-2 empty), yet Rs 1,436 Cr a year is bought from promoter-group suppliers (consolidated) and promoter-directors take 14% of profit (sections 9, 12).
