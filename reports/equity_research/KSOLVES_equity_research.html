Ksolves India Ltd

A capital-light developer-time boutique that earns very high returns on almost no capital, whose profit has not grown for three years, and whose management has reset its growth and margin floors every time they were breached

NSE: KSOLVES · BSE code: 543599 · ISIN: INE0D6I01023
Prepared: 13 October 2026 · Price: ₹246.65 · Market cap: ₹585 Cr · P/E: 15.9× trailing · Dividend yield: 5.7% (trailing ₹14 a share)
The one-paragraph version
A good small business at a fair price on the assumption that revenue stops shrinking, run by a team whose multi-year promises have not held

What the ten calls show. Ksolves sells skilled developer time (Salesforce, Odoo ERP, Apache big-data work, AI/ML) to mostly US clients from India, and it needs almost no capital. Revenue rose from ₹108.6 Cr in FY24 to ₹162.7 Cr in FY26, but profit after tax went ₹34.2 Cr, ₹34.3 Cr, ₹34.3 Cr. Of 33 dated or quantified statements management made between February 2024 and April 2026, 10 were kept, 4 slipped, 13 broke and 6 are still unresolved. The pattern is a ratchet: the margin floor went 35–38% (July 2024), then “will not go below then 35%” (January 2025), then 30%, then 25% (October 2025), each reset after the previous floor was broken. The 30% growth promise for “three to five years” was called “never a guidance” fourteen weeks after it was last repeated.

What the numbers show. The 43% EBITDA margin of FY21–FY24 was partly the absence of costs: the founders drew no salary from November 2021 to 30 September 2025, and the CEO said on the May 2025 call “I was not taking the salaries and all” when asked why 40% would not return. EBITDA margin fell 13.1 points from FY24 to FY26, almost all of it operating (events, travel, ESOP, senior hires, a product bet, and now founder pay). Dividends took 99.5% of the cash left after capex and interest over FY24–FY26, and FY25 dividends of ₹36.75 Cr exceeded profit; a ₹9 Cr bank loan appeared that quarter. Receivable days rose from 54 to 70 and one client now bills ₹25–28 Cr a year.

What the price needs. At ₹246.65 the prior report's own method gives ₹259 (bear), ₹327 (base) and ₹395 (bull), the same values as before. But that method assumed FY27 growth of 19%, and Q1 FY27 revenue fell 3.7% sequentially with, per press coverage of the July call, full-year revenue guidance not reaffirmed. Re-running with a soft FY27 gives ₹143, ₹256 and ₹354. The price needs a flat 27% EBITDA margin on that path, inside the 25–30% guided band, so there is no margin of safety left beyond the dividend.

Two-axis verdict. Quality: moderate. Superb returns on capital and clean related-party history, offset by flat profit, rising concentration, weak guidance credibility and thin disclosure. Value: fair, not cheap. A price below about ₹200 would pay for the bear risks; above about ₹330 the stock would be discounting a return to 19% growth that management has just stopped promising. Triggers are in Section 15. This is analysis, not advice.

Thin history: read this before the rest

The database holds ten fiscal years (FY17–FY26) but only six carry real operating data. FY17–FY18 and FY20 have no operating profit, and FY19 revenue was ₹5.4 Cr. Ksolves listed on NSE's SME platform around 2020–21 (the company's own dates differ between calls) and moved to the main board in 2022. The ten calls span only Q3 FY24 to Q4 FY26, and the Q1 FY27 call (15 July 2026) is not in the document store. A six-year record with one up-cycle cannot support a cycle argument; every conclusion below is about a business still in its first slowdown.

What changed against the 13 September 2026 report

That report read two of the ten calls. This one reads all ten, and the difference is large: the margin-floor ratchet, the founder-salary effect, the dividend-versus-cash arithmetic, the ₹25–28 Cr single client, the 80% time-and-materials share, the AI-economics admissions, the buyback and debt reversals, the unmet hedging plan and the 565-versus-646 headcount are all new. Its verdict (“price already assumes something close to a bear-case outcome”) rested on 19% FY27 growth; Q1 FY27 undermines that premise. Its valuation numbers are reproduced here to within 1% (Section 14).

01The business: skilled developer time sold to small and mid-sized clients, with a product side-bet

Incorporated July 2014 (promoter says the business began in 2012) · Noida, Pune, Indore, Dubai · about 550–650 people · FY26 revenue ₹162.7 Cr

Ksolves bills clients for the time of engineers and consultants in technologies that are hard to hire for: Salesforce, Odoo (open-source ERP), Apache big-data tools (NiFi, Kafka, Cassandra, Spark), AI/ML and DevOps. About 97% of revenue is services; the rest is products (Dashboard Ninja on the Odoo app store, Salesforce apps, and Data Flow Manager, or DFM, a tool for operating Apache NiFi). Management split the two for the first time in the Q2 FY26 deck: services revenue ₹38.64 Cr at a 37% contribution margin, products ₹1.03 Cr (Oct 2025).

How the money is earned

  • Contract form. On 29 February 2024 the CFO gave the split as time-and-materials 65–66%, fixed cost 24%, staff augmentation 8%, and said “in future, we will increase the revenue from the fixed cost”. In July 2025 the CFO said “around 80% is T&M and the rest comes from fixed cost”. The stated direction was fixed price; the measured direction was the opposite (the board report calls it a “transition towards project-based, fixed-cost engagements”).
  • Rates. Developer rates were $31 an hour for AI/ML and $17 for Odoo in February 2024. By July 2025 the CEO said “customer does not want to pay more than $35 per hour” for AI work whose resource costs about ₹30 lakh a year (Section 04).
  • Clients. “our customers are mainly the small cap customers” (Feb 2024), who start small and grow: one client began at $3,000 a month ten years ago and pays $125,000 a month now. Deals are getting larger (a $600,000 first project in 2025; the biggest in 13 years, against $120,000 before) and slower: six months of chasing for that deal. Over 200 active clients; 15–20 give over 60% of revenue; top-five share is 40% (Section 07).
  • Technology mix. Odoo has been the largest single practice: ₹10 Cr (FY23), ₹20+ Cr (FY24), ₹33+ Cr (FY25), 120+ developers, more than 20% of revenue, with 80–85% from overseas. Salesforce, big data and open-source each ran above 18–20% on the calls, and AI/ML 10–12% (Oct 2024 to Jan 2025). The mix was not disclosed in the FY26 calls.
  • Geography. India's share of revenue rose from about 18.5% to 22–23% while the US fell from about 70% to about 60% (table below). Revenue from the US therefore grew by roughly a quarter in two years while India grew by close to four-fifths; management says it takes India work only for government projects, bank brands or good rates.

The niche and its defence, in management's words

The case for a moat is that Apache NiFi, Kafka and Cassandra work is rare below the large IT firms. The company's own evidence is partly search-engine rank (Manish Gurnani, CTO, Feb 2024: “you will see that Ksolves is coming in the top”) and partly the claim that it is “the only company in India of our size to contribute to open source product of Apache NiFi” (CTO, Jan 2026). Management declines to name a competitor. When asked directly, the answer was:

“Infosys, Persistent, LTI. These are the companies which are doing the same kind of work. I will not say that they are competitors. They are very big fish, but most of the time, customers for them, we are working, they say that they are -- for them, Persistent is handling the big chunk of the work or Accenture is handling a big chunk of the work where we have a small chunk of the work.”

Ratan Srivastava, Chairman & MD, Q3 FY25 call, 21 January 2025

That is a description of a sub-vendor, not of a firm with pricing power. The CTO conceded the same about the AI product in October 2024: “Obviously, this is something that others can also do. I'm not denying that.” For DFM the claim shifted from “there is no competitor” (May 2025) to Cloudera as “the only peer” (Jan 2026), and a 7–8 times price advantage was asserted on the same call, which the CTO confirmed with one word, “Easily”. These are company claims about itself and are carried here as claims, not facts.

A claim that did not survive

In May 2024 the case for AI/ML was margin: “we do not see that they will be able to give us that margin that AI/ML can get” (Odoo and Salesforce, the CTO said). In July 2025 the CEO reversed it: “the profitability in AI as compared to other technology is less”. The thing that was meant to carry the margin story is, on management's own account, the lower-margin line.

The credit rating agency describes the same niche more modestly: “Presence in a niche segment will continue to support revenue growth over the medium term, which will remain monitorable” (CRISIL, December 2024), and lists “intense competition constrains scalability” as a weakness.

The ROCE number, honestly

A 131% ROCE on ₹35 Cr of capital means little when the company hands almost all of its earnings out. Equity was ₹20.8 Cr in March 2025 after ₹36.75 Cr of dividends against ₹34.3 Cr of profit (CRISIL: dividends paid “out of reserves leading to negative net cash accruals”). A business that pays out more than it earns will show an ever higher return on a smaller base. The figure measures payout policy as much as economics. The comparison that matters is EBITDA per rupee of revenue, and that fell from 43 paise to 30 paise.

What management did well

It started segment reporting in October 2025 and said why (“to understand the distinct economics, growth drivers and underlying factors affecting the margin performance in each vertical”), reported ESOP-adjusted EBITDA from Q3 FY25 as promised, and in April 2026 gave a plain account of the product spend: “We have taken money from the services and spent money on DFM”. 04Margins: a 13-point fall, and what the 43% was made of

EBITDA margin 42.8% (FY24), 34.9% (FY25), 29.7% (FY26), 30.3% (Q1 FY27, press-reported)

What the 43% was made of

The FY21–FY24 margin has an obvious explanation that the Q1 FY25 call gave in the CEO's own words, when an investor asked how a developer-time business earned margins above the large IT firms:

“Because now it is compromised. Now I have compromised with the margin in this quarter. How I have managed? Answer is that I have managed with so many things I have not done, because I have not done so many things.”

Ratan Srivastava, Chairman & MD, Q1 FY25 call, 23 July 2024

He listed what had not been done: no conference booths or speaking slots, no good office, no senior hires. In May 2025 an investor asked when 40% would return. The reply:

“40%. Anyone is doing 40%? ... It was very small; base was very small. I was not taking the salaries and all. So that's why we were getting that. So now I don't think that we are going to reach that number.”

Ratan Srivastava, Q4 FY25 call, May 2025

The FY25 annual report confirms it: the chairman and whole-time director “were not drawing salaries since November 2021 and started drawing the salary from October 01, 2025”, and the annual general meeting approved pay of up to ₹2 Cr and ₹1 Cr a year respectively. The table in the same report shows ₹100 lakh and ₹50 lakh, which is where the new run-rate probably sits. For FY26 that is half a year (about ₹0.75 Cr); for FY27 the full ₹1.5 Cr is about 0.9 points of FY26 revenue, and up to 1.8 points at the cap. The footnote is the only source for the dates; the call corroborates the substance. The pipeline's related-party audit records different director amounts for FY25 (Section 12), and the converted tables are missing, so the reconciliation is left open.

The 40%+ was therefore not a measure of pricing power alone: part of it was unpaid founder labour and unspent marketing, which any listed company eventually has to pay for.

Two bridges in one call

On the Q1 FY26 call the CFO and CEO each walked an investor from 38% to 26%. The CFO's bridge (ESOP ₹0.66 Cr, leadership ₹1.24 Cr, events ₹1.93 Cr) restored the margin to about 37%. The CEO's, on the same call, counted only ESOP (₹0.3 Cr) and events (₹1.7 Cr) and arrived at 31.71%, “which I have stated that our profit margin will not go down below 30%”. Neither leaves room for the founder pay that began three months later.

Is it the company or the industry?

The AI-pricing question the company has not answered

Management's line has been consistent and unquantified. In January 2026: “we have not seen pricing pressure from clients”. In April 2026 an investor asked whether the margin loss was “because of threat of AI coming in, and we have to do some price cutting”; the reply covered spending, internal efficiency and war-related delays and never addressed price. The October 2025 call claimed “improved billing rates”, yet the only rate ever quoted fell short of the cost of the resource (AI: $35 an hour cap against ₹30 lakh a year per engineer). The CTO said on the January 2025 call that Gen AI was being used to “reduce the overall budget as well as the overall estimate so that we become more competitive”, which is a pass-through of productivity to clients in management's own words. The FY25 annual report lists price renegotiation as a risk in generic language. No realised rate per engineer, utilisation or revenue per head has ever been disclosed (Section 16).

What management said, and what held

Management has volunteered the working-capital story rather than been asked for it (no analyst asked about receivables, collections, days outstanding or cash conversion on any of the ten calls). The July 2025 statement was the most specific:

“We have been maintaining the improved working capital of less than 50 days and debtors of less than 60 days from last two years, which ensures our strong cash flow.”

Darpan Audichya, Head of Business Transformation and Consulting, Q1 FY26 call, July 2025

SLIPPED It was true to March 2025 (56 days on annual sales, 57 on Q4 sales, and the annual report gives working-capital days of 55.18 against 74.13). It was not true in March 2026 (70 and 65 days). Net operating working capital is now 13.6% of sales, about 50 days. The same speaker added that “over 95% of our revenue is actually secured through a defined insurance” (a year earlier the CEO had said about 80% of projects were covered by invoice insurance from HDFC) and that a ₹36 lakh default was recovered from the insurer. Insurance protects against loss; it does not shorten the wait, and the FY25 current ratio fell from 2.64x to 1.69x (annual report) while CRISIL reports 2.21x for March 2025 and 9.87x for March 2024 for the same company, a difference neither document explains.

Contradictions on the working-capital side

The Q4 FY25 call explained the ₹9 Cr bank loan twice. The CFO: “The working capital loan was availed during the year just to manage short-term cash flow mismatches on account of overall growth in the business”. The CEO, asked about it afterwards: “that was not a requirement. But what we did that we thought that we should have some history also with the bank”. The cash arithmetic in Section 11 points to the dividend, not growth, as the thing it funded. The CFO also said on the same call that, with receivable days of 56, “we see no significant headwinds related to ForEx volatility”; hedging was “in the plan” and never mentioned again.

Cash conversion

CFO as a share of PAT was 0.97x (FY24), 0.99x (FY25) and 0.90x (FY26). FY26 CFO fell to ₹30.8 Cr from ₹34.1 Cr as the receivables build (+₹9.9 Cr) outran the rise in other liabilities (+₹5.6 Cr). Incremental working capital over the last three years has been 11.2% of incremental sales, which is the figure the DCF charges for growth. Single-source flag: the working-capital days quoted in the annual report for FY25 and the call claims are the company's own definitions; the converted annual reports have lost their tables, so ageing, unbilled revenue and expected-credit-loss provisions could not be checked (Section 16).

  • Valuation. At 15.9 times trailing earnings and 11.4 times EBITDA, Ksolves sits mid-pack against peers at 12.6–25.6 times and 8.0–15.8 times. The market is neither paying for the 131% ROCE nor charging a distress discount.

  • Closest analogue. eClerx is the best long-run reference: margins of 34–37% in FY15–17 fell to a 22% trough in FY19 and recovered only to the mid-20s; the September 2026 peers file records a base-rate for partial recovery, not full. Nucleus Software (73.8% promoter, debt-free, margin 27% to 14% over FY23–FY26 on Screener) is the nearest in governance and the cautionary tale on how fast a niche margin can go.
  • Ownership. Insider holding of 64% against a free float of about 36% and zero institutional ownership (yfinance) puts Ksolves with Datamatics, Nucleus and Saksoft in the closely held group, and is one reason volume is thin (about 80,000 shares a day).
What the peer set cannot say

None of these companies is a Salesforce/Odoo/Apache boutique with 200 clients and 550 engineers. The two real-size matches (InfoBeans: FY26 margin up from 18% to 27% on AI-augmented delivery; Kellton: revenue up, margin at a multi-quarter low) disagree, so the AI-productivity question has no settled industry answer in this size class. Part B of the industry brief (retrieved September 2026) remains the least settled material in this report.

The base effect

Management's own account of slowing percentage growth is the arithmetic of size: “adding INR 25 lakhs in a quarter was a joke” at ₹10 Cr of annual revenue; at ₹43 Cr a quarter, 10% growth needs ₹4.3 Cr more. It explains the pace down to the mid-teens. It does not explain absolute decline.

The FY25 shortfall and the bank loan

In FY25 the company generated ₹34.1 Cr of operating cash, spent about ₹7.6 Cr on investing activities (a proxy that includes office fit-outs and investments) and ₹0.5 Cr on interest, leaving ₹26.1 Cr. It paid ₹36.75 Cr of dividends (three interims: ₹8, ₹8 and ₹15 a share before the split; ₹4, ₹4 and ₹7.50 after). The shortfall of about ₹10.7 Cr is almost the ₹9 Cr working-capital loan outstanding at 31 March 2025. That is this report's inference from the cash-flow statement, not something management said: the CFO attributed the loan to cash-flow mismatches from growth, and the CEO to wanting “some history also with the bank” (Section 05). CRISIL, reading the same year, describes dividends “out of reserves”. The loan was repaid in FY26, when payout fell to 76% of profit.

Who receives the cash matters. The promoter group holds about 59%, so roughly ₹21.6 Cr of the FY25 dividend and ₹15.4 Cr of FY26's went to promoters, against founder salaries of nil until October 2025 and about ₹1.5 Cr a year since (this report's arithmetic). A retail commentator put the other reading bluntly on ValuePickr in June 2025: “because of lack of growth opportunities they are making themselves rich through dividend”. Another poster in 2025 read the same cash going out as proof that “the business is not a fluke”. Both can be true. What the arithmetic rules out is that the business is retaining capital to fund its growth.

The other uses of cash

  • Capex is tiny. Net block ₹5.8 Cr; fixed assets are mostly leased offices (Noida, Pune, Indore, Dubai). The ten-year capex proxy is under ₹12 Cr. The CEO's reason for not owning an office, Feb 2024: “expansion is not easy” when you buy.
  • Events and branding. About ₹5–6 Cr in FY26 across ten events and ₹1 Cr for a single Dreamforce booth in FY25. The CEO's justification: “why I will invest money for the next investor if I'm not getting the ROI”. The ROI has not been measured in public: new-client counts were last given as “25, 30 customers” in a quarter (Oct 2024). The FY26 events were later called “a learning”.
  • Product. Cumulative DFM and related product spend “not more than Rs. 10 crores” (Oct 2025); about ₹2 Cr expensed in FY26 (Apr 2026); no more planned. It produced two customers and product revenue of about ₹1 Cr a quarter.
  • ESOPs. A 2.5% pool; 60,000 options (Jan 2025), then 268,000 options (about 1% of capital, May 2025); ₹60–70 lakh a quarter of expense; “we don't have any plan to grant new options” (Jul 2025). The maximum dilution of the pool is small; the cost is in the margin.
  • Acquisitions. No plan (May 2024); “the right time where I need to look for acquisition” with merchant bankers (Oct 2024); “I could not get any company” (May 2025); “we dropped that idea” (Apr 2026). The failed search is the CEO's stated reason for the spending route: “If I could have done some acquisition, maybe, then the things were different”. No deal, no cost disclosed, no named target.
  • Buyback. Promised (Jan 2025: “in next one year, you will see”; Jul 2025: “definitely buyback in future and by keeping this dividend on the lower side”), then withdrawn (Jan 2026: “No plan”). The dividend was not kept lower: ₹5 was paid in October 2025 and again in January 2026. The reversal is the clearest example of a capital-return statement made to a question rather than a decision.
What this means

Capital allocation here is simple and consistent: distribute everything. That is shareholder-friendly in cash terms (a 5.7% yield) and fragile in balance-sheet terms: net worth is ₹29 Cr, the founders took no pay for four years, the margin is being rebuilt with unfunded investments, and the rating agency has named the dividend as the risk. If revenue stays flat and the margin holds at 28%, FY27 profit of about ₹33 Cr covers the ₹14 a share trailing dividend (₹33 Cr) with nothing left. A cut is the natural release valve.
