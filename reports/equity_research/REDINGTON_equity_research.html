NSE/BSE: REDINGTON · BSE code: 532805 · ISIN: INE891D01026

The one-paragraph version
A genuinely diversified, professionally-run distributor with two AA+ credit ratings and a decade-long cash-conversion shortfall this report cannot wave away as an accounting artefact

Redington is India's number-two IT and mobility products distributor by most accounts, run without a promoter since 2017 under a Taiwanese anchor shareholder (Synnex Technology International, 24.1%), spanning 40 markets, roughly 440 brands and 70,000-plus channel partners across India, the Middle East, Turkey and Africa. Both agencies that rate it — CRISIL and ICRA — hold it at AA+/Stable/A1+. This pipeline's primary ROCE gate clears 15% in every one of nine measured years; Vijay Malik's own stricter cross-check, which does not net current liabilities out of the capital base the way the primary formula does, shows a real, sustained decline from 22.5% to under 13% over the same window — the same underlying fact read two different ways, and both readings matter. The central finding: cumulative operating cash flow over FY2017–FY2026 was ₹6,436 Cr against cumulative profit of ₹9,713 Cr, a 0.66× conversion ratio, while gross receivable days lengthened from 45 to 66 even as management's own preferred net working-capital metric kept improving — two measures of the same business, both true, telling different stories depending on which one a reader trusts. A reverse DCF finds the current price requires either an EBITDA margin at the company's own all-time decade high sustained for years, or working-capital efficiency on every future rupee of growth better than the best year Redington has ever actually delivered. A well-run, clean, real business, priced for a version of its own cash economics that has not yet happened.

01The business: distribution, not a brand

A 33-year-old middleman between global technology vendors and 70,000+ channel partners across four continents, structurally thin-margin by design

Redington was set up in 1993 and listed on the Indian exchanges in early 2007. What it does has not changed since: it buys IT hardware, mobility devices, enterprise technology and software from global vendors — Apple, Dell, HP, Lenovo, Microsoft, Samsung, Microsoft's own hyperscaler ecosystem, and dozens of others — and moves it through 70,000+ channel partners into 40 markets across India and what the company calls the META region (Middle East, Turkey, Africa). It owns almost none of the demand it serves and very little of the manufacturing capacity behind it; its job is working capital, logistics, credit and relationships, at scale, run at a margin the whole global industry treats as normal rather than a company-specific failing (Section 02).

Q1 FY2027 revenue mix across the four internally-reported business units, the freshest breakdown available at the time of this report:

Management's own concentration disclosure, from the most recent (Q1 FY2027) call, is worth recording directly: the top 10 OEM brands contributed 75% of the quarter's revenue, each with double-digit growth, and management specifically noted this now represents "a good balance of hardware, software, and networking brands" — a direct answer, management itself observed, to a question "asked about 4, 5 quarters back" on whether the brand mix was too hardware-heavy. That a recurring analyst concern from over a year earlier is now cited by management as resolved is exactly the kind of claim this report treats as worth tracking against future concentration disclosures, not accepting at face value.

No promoter, one large anchor shareholder

Redington has had no promoter since March 2017 (fully diluted, per Screener's own shareholding series) and was formally classified a "listed entity with no promoter" from September 2019 (CRISIL's own company-background note). Its largest shareholder is Synnex Technology International Corporation, a Taiwanese technology distributor, holding 24.1% as of 31 March 2026 through its investment arm Synnex Mauritius Ltd. As of June 2026, FIIs held 61.96%, DIIs 16.37% and public shareholders 21.61% — an institutionally-owned, professionally-run company with no controlling family or PE sponsor, a genuinely unusual structure among the companies this pipeline has covered and one that recurs as a theme in Sections 10 and 11.

A direct-to-retail initiative worth quoting in full

Asked on the Q1 FY2027 call about a specific annual-report disclosure, management confirmed and detailed a five-year-old strategic bet: building dedicated retail infrastructure for one premium smartphone brand's products specifically. In management's own words: "we work closely with this OEM to build now almost 6,000 outlets that are specifically selling their product. And this involves everything from supply chain automation, to getting these products, to in-store promoters, to area sales managers, to building a full go-to-market machine, getting EMIs, getting a whole lot of things together." The result, per the same disclosure: revenue share from this brand's own direct-to-retail channel rose from 1.3% to 10% over five years. This is a genuinely differentiated, quantified initiative — not distribution in the generic sense, but a purpose-built retail infrastructure investment for one strategic partner — and management's own framing is unambiguous about why it matters: "it's clearly given us a headway into something unique that we built."

Subsidiaries that matter to the numbers

  • ProConnect Supply Chain Solutions Ltd — the group's logistics arm, fully consolidated, referenced repeatedly in peer-comparison and diversification commentary.
  • Redington Gulf FZE and its regional network — the Middle East and Africa operating entities (UAE, Saudi, Egypt, Kenya, Nigeria and dozens more, per the CRISIL rating rationale's consolidation annexure, which lists 48 fully-consolidated step-down subsidiaries).
  • Arena Bilgisayar Sanayi Ve Ticaret A.S. (Turkey) — roughly 49.4%-owned, the subsidiary at the centre of the company's one clearly identified structural weak point (Section 11).
  • RedServ Global Solutions / RedServ Business Solutions — the group's financing, insurance-broking and value-added-services arm.
  • Redington Green Energy Ltd (Kenya) — a small but growing solar-distribution push, the same business line the FY2026 Third Wave Power related-party item plausibly touches (Section 11).

The structural risks of the model itself

These are not Redington-specific failings; they are what distribution economics look like everywhere, and the same list applies to Rashi Peripherals, Ingram Micro and TD Synnex (Section 06):

  • Vendor-mandate concentration. The relationship with any single large OEM — and now the hyperscalers for cloud — can be lost or repriced at the vendor's discretion; Redington does not control the terms on which it earns its margin.
  • Channel and dealer stocking risk. Management itself flagged, on the Q1 FY2027 call, that a large part of the quarter's PC growth was driven by component-shortage-induced price increases with "very little" underlying unit growth, and that roughly 2–3 months of inventory has built up across the channel — a pattern that pulls demand forward and can reverse (Section 07).
  • Direct-to-retail disintermediation. Vendors periodically shift go-to-market strategy toward selling direct, cutting out layers of distribution; this is precisely what forced the 2015 wind-down of Redington's own last-mile subsidiary, Nook Micro Distribution Ltd (Section 10), and stands in direct tension with the successful direct-to-retail build above — the same structural force can work for or against Redington depending on whether it is the one building the new channel or the one being disintermediated by it.
  • Hyperscaler marketplace risk. Cloud vendors increasingly sell and support customers directly through their own marketplaces. Management's own framing, repeated across two calls: "it is an opportunity, more than a threat" — a claim this report treats as a management assertion to monitor, not a settled fact, given it is precisely the kind of structural risk a distributor is least positioned to counter if it materialises.
  • Currency and country risk. A META-and-Turkey footprint means real exposure to currency depreciation (the Turkish lira collapse is the clearest live example, Section 11) and episodic geopolitical shocks — the West Asia conflict beginning 28 February 2026, which management says affects a region contributing roughly 30–35% of total company revenue and profit, is the most recent and by far the most material.

02The checklist, applied to a trading business

Coffee-can gates clear on a strong-looking basis; the harder cash tests need the business model in mind before they mean anything

The two mechanical gates

Over the trailing nine fiscal years, Redington's consolidated revenue cleared 10% year-on-year growth in eight of nine years — the single miss was FY2018, when sales grew just 1.19%. This pipeline's primary ROCE formula (which nets current liabilities out of the capital base) cleared 15% in every one of the same nine years, ranging 15.96–29.32%. Combined, that classifies as a Dirty 9iner: one gate at a perfect 9/9, the other at 8/9.

The same ROCE, computed the other way, tells a materially different story

Vijay Malik's own stricter cross-check formula does not net current liabilities out of the capital base. On that basis, Redington's ROCE has fallen from roughly 22.5% in FY2018 to 7.08% in FY2026 — a real, sustained decline, not a rounding difference, and zero of the nine years qualify against the 15% bar under this formula (values run 7.08–10.83%). This is the widest divergence between this pipeline's two ROCE tests found in any distribution or trading business scored this cycle.

The reason the two disagree this much is itself informative: Redington funds a large share of its working capital with trade payables and other current liabilities rather than owners' capital or borrowings. Net that out (this pipeline's primary formula) and the capital base looks small and stable, producing a consistently strong-looking ROCE. Leave it in (Malik's formula) and the true, growing size of the balance sheet the business needs to run — receivables and inventory, funded however they are funded — shows up directly, and it has been growing faster than profit. Both numbers are "correct"; they are answering different questions, and the Malik-basis decline is the more useful one for judging whether the balance sheet is getting more capital-intensive over time.

The three cash gates

Gate 1 (cumulative CFO against cumulative PAT) is the one everyone runs, and it is the central finding of this report: over FY2017–FY2026, cumulative CFO was ₹6,436 Cr against cumulative PAT of ₹9,713 Cr — a 0.66× conversion ratio, a cash-gate score of 4/10.

Gate 3, which Malik actually computes (CFO less capex less interest), reads: ₹6,436 Cr − ₹981 Cr − ₹3,114 Cr = ₹2,342 Cr positive over the decade — roughly a quarter of cumulative reported profit, once the business's own real financing cost is charged against it. The capex figure needs a caveat this pipeline flags on every distribution or asset-light business it covers: the CFI-derived capex proxy reads as literally zero in six of the ten years, because a trading business this asset-light genuinely spends very little on fixed assets. That is a real, structural feature of the business, not a data gap — and it means the binding constraint on Gate 3 for Redington is financing cost, not capital expenditure. A distributor's growth is funded by working capital and, where that is insufficient, by borrowings; the interest bill is the real toll the balance sheet pays for scale (Section 04).

The subcontracting test, reinterpreted for a distributor

This pipeline's anomaly scan flags Redington's net fixed-asset turnover rising 86% over the decade — in Malik's own manufacturing- and EPC-context framework, rising turnover paired with falling margin is the signature of a subcontracting shift. That test does not transfer cleanly to a pure distribution business. Redington's operating margin has moved in a narrow 2.0–2.9% band over the same period with no sustained decline tracking the turnover rise (Section 04), and a distributor's fixed-asset base is inherently tiny and slow-growing relative to revenue — warehouses and IT systems, not plant and machinery. This report treats it as asset-light revenue scaling, not a Malik-style red flag, though it is included here for completeness.

What both rating agencies independently flag as the real structural weakness

CRISIL and ICRA — the two agencies that rate Redington, both currently at AA+/Stable/A1+ — use almost identical language for the offsetting factor against an otherwise strong profile: "modest operating margin and large working capital requirement in the distribution business" (CRISIL's own rating rationale). That is the same finding this section's own gate analysis reaches independently, from the company's own numbers rather than the raters' opinion of them (Section 11 covers the ratings in full, including both agencies' explicit downgrade triggers).

What this pipeline's own anomaly scan flags, and what explains each one

  • Receivable days, up 46% over the decade (45.2 → 66.1 days). A sustained drift, not a single bad year — the load-bearing finding of Section 05.
  • NFAT, up 86% over the decade. Addressed in Section 02 as more likely asset-light scaling than Malik's classic subcontracting signal.
  • EBITDA margin step up, FY2021→FY2022 (2.44% → 2.94%, more than twice the series' typical year-on-year move). This coincides with the global component-shortage cycle. Management's own retrospective framing, from the Q4 FY2026 call, is directly relevant: comparing the current shortage cycle to COVID, management said "in COVID, while there was shortage, there was a pent-up demand…that also resulted in…higher gross margin in the COVID period, lower working capital in the COVID period…As we speak now, we don't think that could be visible in the current scheme of things." That is management itself drawing the line between what produced FY2022's margin spike and why it does not expect the same combination to repeat.
  • CFO turning sharply negative in FY2023 (−₹3,234 Cr), the single largest cash-flow swing in the record. This is the same year revenue grew 26.7% — the decade's fastest — and borrowings jumped by roughly ₹2,490 Cr to fund it. This pipeline's own working-capital series shows FY2023's incremental working-capital ratio at 31% of incremental revenue, roughly three times every other year in the record bar FY2025. This report was not able to find a primary-source document that explains the FY2023 build in specific, transaction-level terms (Section 15).
  • EBITDA margin step down, FY2023→FY2024 (2.77% → 2.25%). This is the window in which Arena's Turkish-lira cost pressures and rising factoring costs first became a visible drag, per later management commentary describing factoring costs as elevated in this period before falling back (Q4 FY2026 call: factoring cost "for Q4 last year [FY2025]…INR22 crores," down to "INR2 crores" by Q4 FY2026).

Q1 FY2027: the best quarter on record, and a specific answer on what is driving it

The quarter ended June 2026 was, in management's own words, "an exceptional quarter for Redington." Consolidated revenue was ₹34,966 Cr (+34% YoY), EBITDA ₹751 Cr (+67% YoY), PAT ₹486 Cr (+77% YoY) — profit growth "significantly" outpacing revenue growth. Excluding the Arena/Turkey subsidiary, revenue grew 41% and profit reached ₹517 Cr, both records. India revenue grew 63%, India PAT 60%; Middle East and Africa grew 15% despite the ongoing West Asia crisis.

Pressed directly on how much of this was genuine demand versus channel pre-buying ahead of anticipated component-driven price increases, management gave an unusually granular, specific answer rather than a general reassurance: the phenomenon is "largely focused on ESG…because in the case of Software Solutions, this does not impact price increases. Mobility…it's a velocity business…there is very little stocking…Similar in Technology Solutions…a lot of the orders are back to back." Within the PC business specifically, management stated unit growth was "more or less flat, flat to a single-digit growth," with the revenue increase driven by average selling price, and estimated roughly two to three months of inventory built up in the channel — not just at Redington but "across the channel…also with the distributors." Roughly two-thirds of the Endpoint Solutions Group's growth this quarter is attributable to price increases rather than volume, on management's own breakdown.

A specific, testable claim about the data-centre opportunity

Of the roughly ₹1,000 Cr of large-deal revenue booked in Q1 FY2027, management specified that about ₹700 Cr came from India's data-centre category alone, driven partly by neocloud operators seeking capacity as European data-centre capacity maxes out amid the West Asia disruption, and partly by an Indian government tax holiday attracting hosting demand. Management's own characterisation of the pipeline: "the funnel actually on the data center side looks pretty, pretty good, even multiple…of that INR1,000 crores for the next few quarters." This is a specific, dated claim this report can test directly against the next several quarters' large-deal disclosures (Section 07).

04Margins: a real operating decline, not a financing story

The opposite pattern from most companies this pipeline has covered — here the balance sheet has been the good news, not the disguise

The margin bridge from FY2023 to FY2026 decomposes profit-before-tax margin from 2.31% to 1.42%, an 89-basis-point decline:

This is a genuine operating decline, not a financing illusion

Almost the entire decline (−80.5bp of the −89.0bp total) is an EBITDA-margin compression — a real operating deterioration. Interest cost actually helped slightly (+4.8bp): despite the absolute interest bill rising every year in this window, revenue grew faster, so interest as a share of sales fell. A reader cannot dismiss this margin story as "just deleveraging in reverse" the way this pipeline has had to for some other companies scored this cycle — here, the operating business is genuinely where the story sits.

What sits behind the −80.5bp EBITDA-margin move, on the evidence available:

  • Arena/Turkey. Continuing losses at the Turkish subsidiary, compounded by a FY2026 impairment of the Arena trade-name intangible — management's own words: "the impact on the group PAT after minority interest is INR75.2 crores" — and, in earlier years of this window, factoring costs on Turkish receivables that ran as high as ₹22 Cr in a single quarter (Q4 FY2025) before falling to ₹2 Cr by Q4 FY2026 as the lira-denominated phone business was wound down.
  • Opex investment ahead of revenue. Management has been explicit and consistent that capability-building spend is running ahead of the revenue it is meant to eventually support: "the opex…because we want to create required capabilities…is going to be more for some time…that elevated opex for next couple of quarters, maybe 1 or 2 years would continue is our expectation."
  • Large-deal mix. Big data-centre and enterprise deals are explicitly lower-margin by management's own account, though deliberately so: "our single most objective in the large deal is no compromise on return on capital employed…the margin compromise will be there, most likely…believe me, no way the large deal profitability can match our normal profitability." That is management drawing an explicit line between the metric it protects (ROCE) and the one it is willing to sacrifice (blended margin) as large-deal volume grows — a deliberate trade-off, stated plainly, not a drift management is unaware of.
A specific, structural target for the highest-margin segment

Management has given a concrete, testable target for Software Solutions Group gross margin: "the overall SSG segment, we are expecting and tracking about 5.5% gross margin…going forward, we would like to maintain between 5.5% and 6% is our intent and plan, and we are tracking so far." As SSG's revenue share keeps growing (15% of Q1 FY2026 to 17% of Q1 FY2027, Section 01), this specific band is a genuine lever on blended margin recovery, distinct from the large-deal-driven dilution described above — a mix-shift toward SSG works in the opposite direction from a mix-shift toward large TSG deals, and both are happening simultaneously.

Two things this decline is not, on the evidence available. It is not a financing-driven illusion, per the bridge above. And it is not obviously company-specific mismanagement: Redington's operating margin has moved in a narrow 2.0–2.9% band for a decade, a range this report's peer work (Section 06) shows is close to the global norm for IT-product distribution at scale. Redington's FY2026 print, at 1.97%, sits inside that band, not below it. The decline is real; whether it is temporary (capability-building spend that eventually pays off, plus the SSG mix shift, as management argues) or a structural step-down toward the lower end of the industry's own range is the open question Section 13 has to price.

05Working capital: gross and net disagree, and the disagreement is the finding

Management's own metric has been improving. The gross number that actually measures credit risk has not. And the AR-provisioning story recurs across two straight quarters.

Redington, CRISIL and this pipeline's own database all report a working-capital metric for this company, and they do not tell the same story:

MeasureSourceDirection, recent quarters
Net working-capital days (net of payables, customer advances)Management, on quarterly callsImproving — "37 → 32 days YoY" (Q1 FY2027); "lowering our working capital days to 30 days" (Q4 FY2026, full-year basis)
Net working-capital daysCRISIL rating rationaleImproving — CRISIL's own tracked metric has moved favourably across recent rating cycles
Gross receivable daysThis pipeline's own databaseWorsening — a sustained decade-long drift from 45.2 days (FY2017) to 66.1 days (FY2026)

Both readings are true and both are computed correctly — they answer different questions. Net working capital nets receivables and inventory against payables and customer advances; it measures how much of the balance sheet the company itself has to fund. Gross receivable days measures something narrower and more important for a credit-risk read: how long the company's own customers are actually taking to pay, independent of how generous Redington's own suppliers have been in the meantime. The mechanism is visible directly in this pipeline's own series: payable days lengthened from 39.4 to 55.3 over the same decade that receivable days lengthened from 45.2 to 66.1 — Redington has been extending more credit to its channel partners and taking more credit from its vendors, roughly in step, which is exactly how a net metric can improve even as the gross number that represents real counterparty credit exposure keeps drifting the wrong way.

A recurring, not one-off, AR-provisioning pattern across two straight quarters

On the Q4 FY2026 call, CFO S.V. Krishnan disclosed elevated AR provisioning specifically in Saudi Arabia and against certain Indian government receivables, describing them as "cautionary provisions…we are still confident of recovery, but it's going to take some time." On the very next call (Q1 FY2027), an analyst raised the same theme again — AR provisioning in Saudi "slightly elevated" versus normal average — and management's answer was, if anything, more explicitly precautionary than resolved: "we want to be very cautious…if there are delays more than what we expect, we had to better provide for it…Can there be collections out of this in future? Very good chance." Two consecutive quarters of the same geography and the same "cautious provisioning, good chance of recovery" framing, without a confirmed recovery yet reported, is worth tracking as a live pattern rather than a resolved one-off (Section 09).

The incremental picture has actually been good, recently

One genuinely favourable, less-discussed number: the incremental working-capital intensity of growth — how much net working capital each additional rupee of revenue has consumed — has been much better in the last two years than the decade average. This pipeline's own three-year figure is 9.5% and five-year figure 15.6%, both well below FY2023's standalone 31% (the year revenue jumped 26.7%). Management's own Q1 FY2027 commentary on this quarter's specific improvement pointed to a combination of factors: "inventory days are going up across the board in the market, and that's true for us. Fortunately, we had some advantage in terms of the payable days and more particularly in Middle East…we could get some additional credit from the vendor." That is a genuinely different driver from prior years' improvements — extended vendor credit specifically in a crisis-affected region — and it is not obviously repeatable if vendor terms normalise once the West Asia situation resolves. If FY2024–26's efficiency is closer to the new normal than FY2023's, the balance-sheet drag from further growth is more manageable than the gross receivable-days trend alone would suggest; if FY2023 is what happens whenever growth accelerates sharply (as it has again in Q1 FY2027, +34% YoY, Section 07), the recent efficiency may not hold. This is precisely the assumption Section 13 has to take a position on.

Rashi Peripherals: the one genuine listed comparable

Rashi Peripherals is a domestic IT-product distributor roughly 13% of Redington's scale, IPO'd February 2024. Its margin band (2.7–2.9% OPM) and return profile (17.0% ROCE, 14.7% ROE) sit close to Redington's own, confirming this is genuinely the same business model at a different scale. The one place the two diverge sharply, and in Redington's favour, is working capital: Rashi's cash-conversion cycle runs 60 days against Redington's 34, driven almost entirely by inventory (62 days against Redington's 26) — a genuine, citable advantage over its closest domestic listed peer, in tension with but not a rebuttal of this report's own receivable-days concern (Section 05): the two companies' working-capital exposures sit in different parts of the cycle. The other clear divergence is governance structure: Rashi's promoter holding is 64.0% and rising; Redington has had no promoter since 2017 (Section 11).

The two biggest competitors are invisible to a public-market investor

Ingram Micro India Pvt Ltd, the Indian subsidiary of the globally-listed Ingram Micro Holding Corp, is described by multiple secondary sources as India's largest IT distributor by scale, ahead of Redington. It does not disclose India-specific financials. Savex Technologies, a large private distributor (~₹32,900 Cr estimated FY2025 revenue), is the market's third significant player and likewise unlisted. Redington's own claimed market position — management cites Omdia Group data placing it in the "top 10" of global tech distributors and #1 specifically in India and Middle East/Africa — is a third-party-sourced claim this report has not independently re-derived from Omdia's own underlying data, and should be read as management's characterisation of an external ranking, not an audited fact.

Global peers confirm the thin margin is structural

Operating margins across the world's largest listed IT distributors cluster in a narrow 1.7–3.8% band: Ingram Micro Holding 1.7%, TD Synnex 2.2–3.0%, ALSO Holding 1.9%, Arrow Electronics 3.4–3.8% (the best of the group, alongside an 11.1% non-GAAP ROIC). Redington's own FY2026 OPM of 1.97% sits squarely inside that range — direct support for the framing in Section 04 that the recent margin decline is a real operating problem but not evidence of a company running structurally worse than the global industry it competes in. Arrow's figures are the one useful upper bound: a distribution model can sustain a meaningfully better margin and capital return than the Ingram/TD Synnex/ALSO band.

Compuage Infocom: what happens when this business model goes wrong

Compuage Infocom was a genuine, long-established listed Indian IT distributor generating roughly ₹4,200 Cr of revenue as recently as FY2022. It entered Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process on 2 November 2023, and by its FY2025-26 results revenue had collapsed to approximately ₹3 Cr. This is not a valuation comparable, but it is a sourced illustration of the tail risk this exact business model carries: a working-capital-heavy distributor with concentrated vendor and channel-partner exposure can go from a real, mid-sized competitor to insolvency inside roughly eighteen months if credit lines, vendor confidence, or channel-partner receivables sour. Redington's own AA+/A1+ ratings (Section 11) and its 34-day cash-conversion cycle are the direct counter-evidence that this is not the path Redington is on — but the existence of a recent precedent in the same industry is worth stating plainly.

10Capital allocation: dividends funded from operations, one honest wind-down, and conserved cash for the next move

No buyback history, a growing dividend, and a decade-old episode of shutting down a strategically redundant subsidiary rather than propping it up

Dividends, not buybacks

Redington paid a ₹6.00 per share final dividend for FY2026 (30% of profits), approved at the 33rd AGM on 29 July 2026. No buyback was identified for FY2026 or the period since — consistent with the company's history of returning capital via regular dividends. With no controlling shareholder whose stake-dilution concerns would typically motivate a buyback preference (Section 01), the dividend-only pattern is the expected one for this ownership structure.

Nook Micro: a strategically redundant subsidiary, wound down honestly rather than propped up

Redington set up Nook Micro Distribution Ltd in 2010 to pursue last-mile/micro-distribution into smaller Indian towns. As vendors in IT and telecom shifted toward Direct-to-Retail models, Nook Micro's niche collapsed — it ended up, in the company's own words from its FY2015-16 annual report, competing "in the same market for similar opportunities" as its own parent. By FY2015, Nook Micro's accumulated losses exceeded 50% of its net worth. The Board approved a merger of Nook Micro back into Redington effective 1 April 2015, court-sanctioned by the Madras High Court on 11 March 2016, with Nook Micro formally dissolved on 15 April 2016.

This is a decade-old, fully resolved episode, included here as a data point on capital-allocation discipline: a wholly-owned venture that became strategically redundant was recognised and shut down within roughly five years of the market shift that undermined it, disclosed frankly rather than left to bleed capital indefinitely. It is also a direct, real-world instance of the same Direct-to-Retail disintermediation risk this report flags as a standing structural feature of the distribution model (Section 01) — a risk that has since worked in Redington's favour too, in the premium-smartphone direct-to-retail build described in that same section.

Minimal capex, cash conserved for optionality

Consistent with the asset-light distribution model (Section 02), Redington's cumulative capex proxy across FY2017–FY2026 is just ₹981 Cr against ₹9,713 Cr of cumulative profit. The company has not needed a large external capital raise. Management's own stated capital priority is to conserve cash for two purposes simultaneously: absorbing the working-capital and risk impact of the West Asia crisis, and funding inorganic growth the company says it is "actively going through the discussion" on, specifically in professional services around cloud and security — an announced intent, not yet a transaction.

Other income: a genuinely explained, business-linked treasury line

Asked directly on the Q1 FY2027 call why other income had grown to roughly ₹200 Cr for FY2026, management gave a specific, two-part answer rather than a vague reference: "2 key aspects will be interest that we collect from the channel partners for the delayed payment. Second, any excess money that we have, if we deposit, there is an interest that we get…whatever is a float is what we use it for depositing…you will also see a corresponding impact on the interest cost. So ideally, some of this, you should net it off with the interest cost." A specific quarterly item — a ₹147 Cr tax refund relating to a contested prior-year demand the company won — also landed in other income this quarter, management noting plainly: "we contested. We won the case, we got the money back…not an issue." This is a rare case in this report's research where a company volunteered exactly how to read a normally opaque other-income line rather than leaving it as an unexplained number.

Turkey: the one genuinely structural weak point in an otherwise clean profile

Redington's Turkish operations sit inside a macro environment CRISIL itself names as a credit-relevant headwind: lira depreciation, inflation, and local borrowing costs management describes as running "42%, 45%." Management's own internal strategic language files Turkey under "Emerging Geographies" — the company's own, higher-risk bucket — alongside Africa and South Asia.

This is a fairly ordinary pattern for a large Indian distribution business. No SEBI enforcement action, and no customs/import-duty litigation, was found — the latter is a gap, not a confirmed absence.

13Valuation: a price that needs the cash conversion this company has never actually delivered

Reverse DCF first — not because the discount rate is uncontroversial, but because the real question is what the business itself has to do

Assumptions common to every scenario below: tax rate 23% (recent three-year average), depreciation 0.18% of sales (FY2026 actual ratio), capex 0.3% of sales (above the noisy, often-zero CFI-derived proxy, deliberately conservative for a genuinely asset-light business), a 10-year decelerating growth path, terminal growth 5%, and a 12% discount rate. The one input every scenario varies deliberately is incremental working capital as a share of incremental revenue — this is the input this specific business's value is most exposed to.

At a modest 12%-declining-to-6% growth path and the company's own actual FY2026 margin (1.97%), combined with a 16% incremental-working-capital assumption reflecting a return toward FY2023-style stress, this model returns a negative implied equity value. That is the mechanical consequence of running a thin-margin, working-capital-heavy distributor through a cash-flow model that charges for growth honestly: at sufficiently thin margins, growth itself becomes cash-negative once working capital is charged against it, which is exactly the dynamic Section 02's Gate 3 discussion and Section 05's incremental-intensity finding both point toward from a different angle.

What the price actually requires, run in reverse

Holding the base-case growth path and a 10% incremental-working-capital assumption fixed, and solving for the flat EBITDA margin the current market price requires: 2.90%, sustained across the projection window. Redington's own decade-high operating margin, set in FY2022, was 2.94% — the market price is effectively asking this company to run at close to its single best-ever margin for years on end, having averaged well below that for nine of the last ten years (Section 03).

Run the other direction — holding margin at the 2.3% decade average and solving for the flat working-capital intensity the price requires — the answer is roughly 4.3% of incremental revenue. Redington's actual best year on this measure, FY2024, was 6.5%; every other year in the ten-year record was worse, several considerably so (FY2023: 31%). The price requires sustained working-capital efficiency meaningfully better than anything this company has ever actually delivered. At the 2.3% margin with no working-capital improvement assumed beyond the base case, no growth rate gets the model to the current price — the solver returns infeasible, because at this margin, incremental working capital absorbs the incremental profit faster than growth can outrun it.

The P/E cross-check, and why it understates the question

Redington trades at roughly 21× trailing P/E — not, on its face, an obviously demanding multiple for a company with an 18.4% reported ROCE and two AA+ credit ratings. But a P/E multiple silently assumes reported earnings convert to cash at whatever rate the market is used to; it does not charge, the way the DCF above explicitly does, for the fact that this company has converted only 66 cents of every rupee of profit into operating cash flow over the last decade (Section 02). The more useful question this report can ask instead is the one the reverse DCF answers directly: what does the business have to do, in cash terms, to be worth what the multiple already implies — and the answer is a margin and working-capital combination this company has not yet sustained.

14Verdict, and what would change our mind

Quality and value, kept on separate axes — this is not a buy or sell call

Quality: genuinely high on structure and governance, real but not disqualifying weaknesses on cash

Redington clears the coffee-can gates on a real, large capital base (Dirty 9iner — 8/9 revenue growth, 9/9 primary ROCE, Section 02), holds two independent AA+/A1+ credit ratings, and runs with a no-promoter, institutionally-owned structure this pipeline has not seen replicated elsewhere in its coverage. The one FY2026 personal-interest related-party item is small and disclosed, if narrowly, and sits alongside a Secretarial Auditor observation worth a direct management answer rather than silence (Section 11). None of this is disqualifying.

Set against that: cumulative cash conversion of 0.66× CFO-to-PAT over ten years is a real, structural shortfall, not a proxy artefact; Vijay Malik's own stricter ROCE cross-check shows a genuine, sustained decline the primary gate does not capture (Section 02); the recent margin decline is a genuine operating deterioration rather than a financing illusion (Section 04); and Turkey remains an unresolved, currently-worsening exposure where management's own account now questions its original exit thesis (Section 09). This report's own management-track-record read rests on a two-call window, not a decade-long ledger — a real limitation on how much confidence it can place in an otherwise encouraging cycle.

Value: priced for cash economics this company has not yet shown it can sustain

The base-case reverse DCF, built on Redington's own decade-average margin and a working-capital assumption close to its two best non-outlier recent years, values the stock 52% below the current ₹404 price. Reaching the current price requires either an EBITDA margin near the company's own all-time decade high, or working-capital efficiency on every future rupee of growth close to the single best year Redington has ever delivered — and at the company's own long-run average margin with no working-capital improvement, no growth rate gets the model to today's price at all (Section 13). This is a real, well-run business priced for a version of its own cash conversion that has not yet happened, not for the one it has actually shown across a decade.

Specific triggers, with thresholds

  • Gross receivable days. A move meaningfully past the current 66 days without a matching extension in payable days would confirm the credit-risk deterioration Section 05 flags, independent of what management's own net metric shows.
  • Incremental working-capital intensity. With growth running at 34% YoY in Q1 FY2027, whether the next few years' incremental WC ratio holds near FY2024–26's 6.5–9.4% or reverts toward FY2023's 31% is the single number this report's own valuation is most sensitive to.
  • Arena/Turkey loss trajectory and the reconsidered exit thesis. Whether the Q1 FY2027 loss and thesis complication (Section 09) prove a one-quarter event or the start of a longer re-strategising, over the next two to three quarters.
  • The recurring Saudi AR-provisioning pattern. A third consecutive quarter of the same "cautious, confident of recovery" framing without a confirmed collection would upgrade this from a watch item to a genuine concern.
  • Ex-Arena EBITDA margin against the guided 2.2–2.4% band, and SSG gross margin against its own 5.5–6% target — the two levers management itself has put numbers on for margin recovery.
  • A direct management answer on Third Wave Power and the Secretarial Auditor's RPT-approval observation, at the next AGM or earnings call.

What would change this verdict: a base-case DCF value near the current price would require the working-capital efficiency of FY2024–26 to prove durable rather than a temporary window inside a longer, worse-trending cycle — the single most important open question this report leaves unresolved, and the one worth revisiting first at the next quarterly print.
