What the whole call history changed. The earlier version of this report read two of the 45 transcripts. This one reads 44 and scores 112 quantified promises against later calls: 46 kept, 35 slipped, 14 broken, 17 still open. The split is not random. Kept: free cash flow in a named quarter, the 40% payout through FY25, no capital and no guarantee for Arena since 2018, ROCE floors, a COVID revenue forecast that was beaten by a wide margin. Slipped or broken: the ProConnect repair (promised “in the next one, maximum two quarters” in November 2019 and re-promised three more times in the next twelve months), the Arena turnaround (asked in May 2024 whether the worst was over, the CEO answered “Yes, I think so.”; in February 2026 the CFO said “next year, we might move towards a breakeven” and three months later withdrew it), the EBITDA band (stepped down three times since 2023, from 2.5–2.6% to 2.2–2.4% ex-Arena), and gross margin (“this is not the new norm. The new norm is going to be where we used to be earlier”, August 2023; 5.1% in Q1 FY26, the lowest on record). The FY26 dividend payout was cut to 30% after four years at 40%.

The cash shortfall is now explained, not just measured. Cumulative operating cash flow over FY2017–FY2026 was ₹6,436 Cr against profit of ₹9,713 Cr (0.66×). Management said in November 2022, six months before the FY23 number was filed, that growth above 10–12% at constant working-capital days “results in additional debt”; FY2023 (revenue +27%, operating cash flow −₹3,234 Cr) was exactly that, and a vendor credit rollback then took working capital to 40 days in Q1 FY24. With net debt of ₹1,720 Cr (0.17× equity) and two AA+ ratings, this is growth funded by debt and a 40% payout, not an accounting artefact. The part that is still unexplained is the size of the Q1 FY27 profit jump (+77%): the PAT margin of 1.39% sits inside the 1.33–1.41% of the previous three quarters, the year-ago base was depressed by an Arena provision, and a tax refund of ₹147 Cr plus connected interest landed in the quarter with no P&L split given.

Valuation. On a 15%-fading-to-6% growth path (FY26 grew 20%, Q1 FY27 34%), with working capital absorbing 10% of incremental revenue (about 35 days, as management guides), the reverse DCF says the price requires a flat EBITDA margin of 2.87% for ten years. Management guides 2.2–2.4%; only the two shortage years FY22 and FY23 (3.1% and 2.95% including other income) were higher. Base case ₹251 (−38%), bull ₹423, bear ₹15. The result is rate-sensitive, and this version says so: at a 10% discount rate the base case is ₹389, and an 18× exit multiple on FY31 earnings gives ₹391. So the stock is full, not absurd: fair only if you accept a 10% cost of capital or an 18× terminal multiple on a business with a 1.3% net margin.

Does the verdict move? The direction does not; the reasoning and the size do. The earlier version said the price was 52% above base-case value and blamed cash conversion; this one puts the gap at 38% at a 12% discount rate and near zero at 10%, and the weakness it rests on is management’s turnaround record and a margin band that keeps being cut, not an unexplained cash gap. The analysis would change at about ₹250 (base case at 12%) to ₹310 (base case at 11%), or on the monitorable events listed in Section 14.

01The business: distribution, not a brand

A 33-year-old middleman between global technology vendors and 70,000+ channel partners across four continents, structurally thin-margin by design — now deliberately moving toward software

Redington was set up in 1993 and listed on the Indian exchanges in early 2007. What it does has not changed since: it buys IT hardware, mobility devices, enterprise technology and software from global vendors — Apple, Dell, HP, Lenovo, Microsoft, Samsung, Microsoft's own hyperscaler ecosystem, and dozens of others — and moves it through 70,000+ channel partners into 40 markets across India and what the company calls the META region (Middle East, Turkey, Africa). It owns almost none of the demand it serves and very little of the manufacturing capacity behind it; its job is working capital, logistics, credit and relationships, at scale, run at a margin the whole global industry treats as normal rather than a company-specific failing (Section 02).

That a recurring analyst concern from over a year earlier is now cited by management as resolved is exactly the kind of claim this report treats as worth tracking against future concentration disclosures, not accepting at face value.

No promoter, one large anchor shareholder

Redington has had no promoter since March 2017 (fully diluted, per Screener's own shareholding series) and was formally classified a "listed entity with no promoter" from September 2019 (CRISIL's own company-background note). Its largest shareholder is Synnex Technology International Corporation, a Taiwanese technology distributor, holding 24.1% as of 31 March 2026 through its investment arm Synnex Mauritius Ltd. As of June 2026, FIIs held 61.96%, DIIs 16.37% and public shareholders 21.61% — an institutionally-owned, professionally-run company with no controlling family or PE sponsor, a genuinely unusual structure among the companies this pipeline has covered and one that recurs as a theme in Sections 10 and 11.

A direct-to-retail initiative worth quoting in full

Asked on the Q1 FY2027 call about a specific annual-report disclosure, management confirmed and detailed a five-year-old strategic bet: building dedicated retail infrastructure for one premium smartphone brand's products specifically. In management's own words: "we work closely with this OEM to build now almost 6,000 outlets that are specifically selling their product. And this involves everything from supply chain automation, to getting these products, to in-store promoters, to area sales managers, to building a full go-to-market machine, getting EMIs, getting a whole lot of things together." The result, per the same disclosure: revenue share from this brand's own direct-to-retail channel rose from 1.3% to 10% over five years. This is a genuinely differentiated, quantified initiative — not distribution in the generic sense, but a purpose-built retail infrastructure investment for one strategic partner — and management's own framing is unambiguous about why it matters: "it's clearly given us a headway into something unique that we built."

Subsidiaries that matter to the numbers

  • ProConnect Supply Chain Solutions Ltd — the group's logistics arm, fully consolidated, referenced repeatedly in peer-comparison and diversification commentary.
  • Redington Gulf FZE and its regional network — the Middle East and Africa operating entities (UAE, Saudi, Egypt, Kenya, Nigeria and dozens more, per the CRISIL rating rationale's consolidation annexure, which lists 48 fully-consolidated step-down subsidiaries).
  • Arena Bilgisayar Sanayi Ve Ticaret A.S. (Turkey) — roughly 49.4%-owned, the subsidiary at the centre of the company's one clearly identified structural weak point (Section 11).
  • RedServ Global Solutions / RedServ Business Solutions — the group's financing, insurance-broking and value-added-services arm.
  • Redington Green Energy Ltd (Kenya) — a small but growing solar-distribution push, the same business line the FY2026 Third Wave Power related-party item plausibly touches (Section 11).

Two points are worth drawing out. First, the concentration disclosures have been consistent for ten years: a handful of global vendors carry most of the business, and the share is now being moved by the arrival of software brands in the top ten (management: three software players, Q1 FY27). Second, management’s own account of the moat is modest and unchanged since the first call in the store: asked in May 2025 for the one insight from five years of running the business, the reply was “Be disciplined. Focus on balance sheet hygiene.” The software pivot is described the same way, as a change in the type of income rather than in the model: “Here, it is more an annuity business, recurring business.” and, on the limits of the services build, “We are not an IT/ITES company. We are not going to be doing custom services.” A reader should still treat the rank claims (#1 in India, #8 globally on Omdia’s 2025 data) as management’s characterisation of an external ranking, a point Section 06 takes up.

The structural risks of the model itself

These are not Redington-specific failings; they are what distribution economics look like everywhere, and the same list applies to Rashi Peripherals, Ingram Micro and TD Synnex (Section 06):

  • Vendor-mandate concentration. The relationship with any single large OEM — and now the hyperscalers for cloud — can be lost or repriced at the vendor's discretion; Redington does not control the terms on which it earns its margin.
  • Channel and dealer stocking risk. Management itself flagged, on the Q1 FY2027 call, that a large part of the quarter's PC growth was driven by component-shortage-induced price increases with "very little" underlying unit growth, and that roughly 2–3 months of inventory has built up across the channel — a pattern that pulls demand forward and can reverse (Section 07).
  • Direct-to-retail disintermediation. Vendors periodically shift go-to-market strategy toward selling direct, cutting out layers of distribution; this is precisely what forced the 2015 wind-down of Redington's own last-mile subsidiary, Nook Micro Distribution Ltd (Section 10), and stands in direct tension with the successful direct-to-retail build above — the same structural force can work for or against Redington depending on whether it is the one building the new channel or the one being disintermediated by it.
  • Hyperscaler marketplace risk. Cloud vendors increasingly sell and support customers directly through their own marketplaces. Management's own framing on the Q1 FY27 call: “It's definitely an opportunity, more than a threat.” — a claim this report treats as a management assertion to monitor, not a settled fact, given it is precisely the kind of structural risk a distributor is least positioned to counter if it materialises.
  • Currency and country risk. A META-and-Turkey footprint means real exposure to currency depreciation (the Turkish lira collapse is the clearest live example, Section 11) and episodic geopolitical shocks — the West Asia conflict beginning 28 February 2026, which management says affects a region contributing roughly 30–35% of total company revenue and profit, is the most recent and by far the most material.

02The checklist, applied to a trading business

Coffee-can gates clear on a strong-looking basis; the harder cash tests need the business model in mind before they mean anything

The two mechanical gates

Over the trailing nine fiscal years, Redington's consolidated revenue cleared 10% year-on-year growth in eight of nine years — the single miss was FY2018, when sales grew just 1.19%. This pipeline's primary ROCE formula (which nets current liabilities out of the capital base) cleared 15% in every one of the same nine years, ranging 15.96–29.32%. Combined, that classifies as a Dirty 9iner: one gate at a perfect 9/9, the other at 8/9.

The same ROCE, computed the other way, tells a materially different story

Vijay Malik's own stricter cross-check formula does not net current liabilities out of the capital base. On that basis, Redington's ROCE has fallen from roughly 22.5% in FY2018 to 7.08% in FY2026 — a real, sustained decline, not a rounding difference, and zero of the nine years qualify against the 15% bar under this formula (values run 7.08–10.83%). This is the widest divergence between this pipeline's two ROCE tests found in any distribution or trading business scored this cycle.

The reason the two disagree this much is itself informative: Redington funds a large share of its working capital with trade payables and other current liabilities rather than owners' capital or borrowings. Net that out (this pipeline's primary formula) and the capital base looks small and stable, producing a consistently strong-looking ROCE. Leave it in (Malik's formula) and the true, growing size of the balance sheet the business needs to run — receivables and inventory, funded however they are funded — shows up directly, and it has been growing faster than profit. Both numbers are "correct"; they are answering different questions, and the Malik-basis decline is the more useful one for judging whether the balance sheet is getting more capital-intensive over time.

The three cash gates

The shortfall is now explained from management’s own words on the calls (below the figure)

Gate 1 (cumulative CFO against cumulative PAT) is the one everyone runs, and it is the central finding of this report: over FY2017–FY2026, cumulative CFO was ₹6,436 Cr against cumulative PAT of ₹9,713 Cr — a 0.66× conversion ratio, a cash-gate score of 4/10.

Gate 3, which Malik actually computes (CFO less capex less interest), reads: ₹6,436 Cr − ₹981 Cr − ₹3,114 Cr = ₹2,342 Cr positive over the decade — roughly a quarter of cumulative reported profit, once the business's own real financing cost is charged against it. The capex figure needs a caveat this pipeline flags on every distribution or asset-light business it covers: the CFI-derived capex proxy reads as literally zero in six of the ten years, because a trading business this asset-light genuinely spends very little on fixed assets. That is a real, structural feature of the business, not a data gap — and it means the binding constraint on Gate 3 for Redington is financing cost, not capital expenditure. A distributor's growth is funded by working capital and, where that is insufficient, by borrowings; the interest bill is the real toll the balance sheet pays for scale (Section 04).

Why the cash lags the profit, in the company’s words

On 3 November 2022 the CFO was asked why a 24% return on equity had still absorbed ₹3,000 Cr of working capital in six months. His answer is the mechanism of the whole decade: “any growth rate north of 10%, 12% with the same working capital days in this business results in additional debt. So if the growth rates are more than 10%, 12%, we have to reduce the working capital days to generate the cash flow.” Nearly three years later he repeated it as the reason for the negative Q1 FY26 cash flow: “With our profitability metrics and a 40% dividend payout and if we are able to retain our working capital days, our growth can be in the range of 10% to 12%.” and “We will not have the same free cash flow, which is what we have seen the case in Q1.” The database agrees on the size of the effect: net operating working capital grew from ₹3,461 Cr (FY2017) to ₹11,139 Cr (FY2026), an increase of ₹7,678 Cr or 79% of cumulative profit, at an unchanged 8–9% of sales outside the COVID dip. A distributor that grew revenue 12.6% a year and paid out 36% of its profit (₹3,544 Cr of dividends) cannot also convert profit one-for-one into cash. That is a financing fact, not a quality-of-earnings fact; the corollary is that the 0.66× figure falls when growth slows or when working-capital days fall, and rises toward 1× only if management stops growing.

The capex caveat above also needs updating from the calls. The database proxy reads zero in six of ten years, but management has guided capex of “range between Rs. 40 crores to Rs. 60 crores per quarter” (May 2025), reported ₹112 Cr in Q3 FY26 (platforms, ProConnect warehouses and AI centres of excellence) and described the SSG investments as 80–90% opex. So Gate 3 is a little worse than the proxy shows, though still not a capital-intensity story.

The subcontracting test, reinterpreted for a distributor

This pipeline's anomaly scan flags Redington's net fixed-asset turnover rising 86% over the decade — in Malik's own manufacturing- and EPC-context framework, rising turnover paired with falling margin is the signature of a subcontracting shift. That test does not transfer cleanly to a pure distribution business. Redington's operating margin has moved in a narrow 2.0–2.9% band over the same period with no sustained decline tracking the turnover rise (Section 04), and a distributor's fixed-asset base is inherently tiny and slow-growing relative to revenue — warehouses and IT systems, not plant and machinery. This report treats it as asset-light revenue scaling, not a Malik-style red flag, though it is included here for completeness.

What both rating agencies independently flag as the real structural weakness

CRISIL and ICRA — the two agencies that rate Redington, both currently at AA+/Stable/A1+ — use almost identical language for the offsetting factor against an otherwise strong profile: "modest operating margin and large working capital requirement in the distribution business" (CRISIL's own rating rationale). That is the same finding this section's own gate analysis reaches independently, from the company's own numbers rather than the raters' opinion of them (Section 11 covers the ratings in full, including both agencies' explicit downgrade triggers).

  • NFAT, up 86% over the decade. Addressed in Section 02 as more likely asset-light scaling than Malik's classic subcontracting signal.
  • EBITDA margin step up, FY2021→FY2022 (2.44% → 2.94%, more than twice the series' typical year-on-year move). This coincides with the global component-shortage cycle. Management's own retrospective framing, from the Q4 FY2026 call, is directly relevant: comparing the current shortage cycle to COVID, management said “COVID, while there was shortage, there was a pent-up demand” ... “higher gross margin in the COVID period, lower working capital in the COVID period” ... “we don't think that could be visible in the current scheme of things”. That is management itself drawing the line between what produced FY2022's margin spike and why it does not expect the same combination to repeat. The contemporaneous calls say the same: in February 2022 the company said its gross margin “should continue to be where it is” only while the shortage lasted, and in May 2022 that supply would ease “from October onwards”. It did, and the operating margin returned to 2.77% in FY23 and 2.25% in FY24.

The calls give the cause in management's own words, which closes the gap the earlier version of this report left open: working capital had run at 7–13 days in FY22 and was rebuilt toward 27–35 days as supply normalised (Q1 FY23: part shipments in enterprise deals stocked but not yet billed; Q2 FY23: “the working cycle will be stabilized somewhere in the range of 27 days, 28 days to 35 days”), and the CFO stated the arithmetic of growing above 10–12% at constant days (Section 02). A second leg followed in Q1 FY24 (2 Aug 2023): working capital went to 40 days because vendors rolled back credit, not because receivable or inventory days rose (single source: that call). The figure −₹3,234 Cr itself is never quoted on any call; the explanation is assembled from the Q1 FY23, Q2 FY23, Q4 FY23 and Q1 FY24 calls, and the Q3 FY23 call (Feb 2023) is not in the store.
  • EBITDA margin step down, FY2023→FY2024 (2.77% → 2.25%). The Q1 FY24 call (2 Aug 2023) is where it first showed: gross margin 5.87% against 5.99% a quarter earlier, ROW EBITDA down 17%, factoring cost ₹60 Cr (₹49 Cr the quarter before) and a technology-investment run-rate of ₹25–30 Cr a quarter, which the then MD called temporary (Section 04). Turkish factoring then ran at ₹86 Cr, ₹94 Cr and ₹79 Cr in the next three quarters and fell only after the Paynet proceeds (Arena factoring ₹22 Cr in Q4 FY25, ₹2 Cr by Q4 FY26). The split between gross margin and opex is the calls' own account, not audited.

Q1 FY2027: the best quarter on record, and a specific answer on what is driving it

The quarter ended June 2026 was, in management's own words, "an exceptional quarter for Redington." Consolidated revenue was ₹34,966 Cr (+34% YoY), EBITDA ₹751 Cr (+67% YoY), PAT ₹486 Cr (+77% YoY) — profit growth "significantly" outpacing revenue growth. Excluding the Arena/Turkey subsidiary, revenue grew 41% and profit reached ₹517 Cr, both records. India revenue grew 63%, India PAT 60%; Middle East and Africa grew 15% despite the ongoing West Asia crisis.

Pressed directly on how much of this was genuine demand versus channel pre-buying ahead of anticipated component-driven price increases, management gave an unusually granular, specific answer rather than a general reassurance: the phenomenon is “the phenomena is really focused on ESG, the Endpoint Solutions Group, because in the case of Software Solutions, this does not impact price increases” ... “it's a velocity business. So there is very little stocking” ... “a lot of the orders are back to back”. Within the PC business specifically, management stated unit growth was “more or less flat, flat to a single- digit growth”, with the revenue increase driven by average selling price, and estimated roughly two to three months of inventory built up in the channel — not just at Redington but "across the channel…also with the distributors." Roughly two-thirds of the Endpoint Solutions Group's growth this quarter is attributable to price increases rather than volume, on management's own breakdown.

Reading the +77%: base effect, plus an unquantified tax item

Q1 FY27 profit of ₹486 Cr is 1.39% of revenue. The previous three quarters were 1.33% (Q2 FY26), 1.41% (Q3) and 1.40% (Q4, before the exceptional item). The jump in the year-on-year growth rate therefore comes from the year-ago base, not from a step-up in profitability: Q1 FY26 profit was about ₹275 Cr on about ₹26,100 Cr of revenue (1.05%), depressed by the US$8m Arena provision (AR provisions were 0.39% of revenue against a norm near 0.10%). Q1 FY27 EBITDA of ₹751 Cr is 2.15% of revenue on a consolidated basis that includes Arena’s losses; management’s ex-Arena guide is 2.2–2.4%. Separately, an analyst asked about a tax refund of ₹147 Cr in the quarter; the CFO answered that it related to past years, that the company had won the case, and that “there is a connected interest also had come in as part of other income”. How much reached profit, and in which line, was not given. Until the Q2 FY27 call shows margins on the same footing, treat Q1 as a strong quarter of unknown quality, not as a new run-rate. (Quarterly figures here are management’s own; Q1 FY26 profit is derived from the stated +77%.)

A specific, testable claim about the data-centre opportunity

Of the roughly ₹1,000 Cr of large-deal revenue booked in Q1 FY2027, management specified that about ₹700 Cr came from India's data-centre category alone, driven partly by neocloud operators seeking capacity as European data-centre capacity maxes out amid the West Asia disruption, and partly by an Indian government tax holiday attracting hosting demand. Management's own characterisation of the pipeline: "the funnel actually on the data center side looks pretty, pretty good, even multiple…of that INR1,000 crores for the next few quarters." This is a specific, dated claim this report can test directly against the next several quarters' large-deal disclosures (Section 07).

The opposite pattern from most companies this pipeline has covered — here the balance sheet has been the good news, not the disguise

This is a genuine operating decline, not a financing illusion

Almost the entire decline (−80.5bp of the −89.0bp total) is an EBITDA-margin compression — a real operating deterioration. Interest cost actually helped slightly (+4.8bp): despite the absolute interest bill rising every year in this window, revenue grew faster, so interest as a share of sales fell. A reader cannot dismiss this margin story as "just deleveraging in reverse" the way this pipeline has had to for some other companies scored this cycle — here, the operating business is genuinely where the story sits.

What sits behind the −80.5bp EBITDA-margin move, on the evidence available:

  • Arena/Turkey. Continuing losses at the Turkish subsidiary, compounded by a FY2026 impairment of the Arena trade-name intangible — management's own words: "the impact on the group PAT after minority interest is INR75.2 crores" — and, in earlier years of this window, factoring costs on Turkish receivables that ran as high as ₹22 Cr in a single quarter (Q4 FY2025) before falling to ₹2 Cr by Q4 FY2026 as the lira-denominated phone business was wound down.
  • Opex investment ahead of revenue. Management has been explicit and consistent that capability-building spend is running ahead of the revenue it is meant to eventually support: "the opex…because we want to create required capabilities…is going to be more for some time…that elevated opex for next couple of quarters, maybe 1 or 2 years would continue is our expectation."
  • Large-deal mix. Big data-centre and enterprise deals are explicitly lower-margin by management's own account, though deliberately so: "our single most objective in the large deal is no compromise on return on capital employed…the margin compromise will be there, most likely…believe me, no way the large deal profitability can match our normal profitability." That is management drawing an explicit line between the metric it protects (ROCE) and the one it is willing to sacrifice (blended margin) as large-deal volume grows — a deliberate trade-off, stated plainly, not a drift management is unaware of.
A specific, structural target for the highest-margin segment

Management has given a concrete, testable target for Software Solutions Group gross margin: "the overall SSG segment, we are expecting and tracking about 5.5% gross margin…going forward, we would like to maintain between 5.5% and 6% is our intent and plan, and we are tracking so far." As SSG's revenue share keeps growing (15% of Q1 FY2026 to 17% of Q1 FY2027, Section 01), this specific band is a genuine lever on blended margin recovery, distinct from the large-deal-driven dilution described above — a mix-shift toward SSG works in the opposite direction from a mix-shift toward large TSG deals, and both are happening simultaneously.

Industry or company? The management view is that the thin margin is the industry’s: at the March 2025 analyst meet it put global peers at 2.0–2.7% operating profit and 1.0–1.4% PAT, against its own guided 2.3–2.5% and 1.3–1.4%. That is a comparative claim by the subject company and is flagged as one. The independent peer evidence in Section 06 (1.7–3.8% operating margin across the largest listed distributors) supports the level but gives no time series, so this report cannot say whether the 2021–2026 decline was industry-wide. What the calls do show is a company-specific component: Arena accounts for 38bp of the 47bp gross-margin fall in Q3 FY26 and 10bp of the 60bp in Q1 FY26.

Two things this decline is not, on the evidence available. It is not a financing-driven illusion, per the bridge above. And it is not obviously company-specific mismanagement: Redington's operating margin has moved in a narrow 2.0–2.9% band for a decade, a range this report's peer work (Section 06) shows is close to the global norm for IT-product distribution at scale. Redington's FY2026 print, at 1.97%, sits inside that band, not below it. The decline is real; whether it is temporary (capability-building spend that eventually pays off, plus the SSG mix shift, as management argues) or a structural step-down toward the lower end of the industry's own range is the open question Section 13 has to price.

Both readings are true and both are computed correctly — they answer different questions. Net working capital nets receivables and inventory against payables and customer advances; it measures how much of the balance sheet the company itself has to fund. Gross receivable days measures something narrower and more important for a credit-risk read: how long the company's own customers are actually taking to pay, independent of how generous Redington's own suppliers have been in the meantime. The mechanism is visible directly in this pipeline's own series: payable days lengthened from 39.4 to 55.3 over the same decade that receivable days lengthened from 45.2 to 66.1 — Redington has been extending more credit to its channel partners and taking more credit from its vendors, roughly in step, which is exactly how a net metric can improve even as the gross number that represents real counterparty credit exposure keeps drifting the wrong way.

Fourteen forecasts of a rebound, and what the calls reported

Three readings follow. (1) Management’s “not sustainable” has been a prudence statement, not a forecast: it was wrong in the favourable direction from 2017 to 2023, and the 17 days of Q1 FY21 were admitted to be partly supplier-funded (“17 days of net working capital is a dream figure”) while the 12 days of Q3 and Q4 FY21 were not, because the CFO said the vendor credit had been “by and large discontinued by August 2020”. (2) The one adverse surprise, Q1 FY24 at 40 days, came from vendors tightening, not from customers paying later: the lever Redington does not control. (3) The guide therefore carries no information about the next quarter; the gross receivable days in the table above and the CFO line carry it instead.

The analysts’ direct questions on receivables, and the answers

On the Q4 FY25 call (20 May 2025) an Old Bridge analyst pointed to a rise of about ₹1,500 Cr in trade receivables. The answer was to move to the net number: “you need to look at the overall working capital. I am sure you will appreciate things are under tremendous control.” The CFO conceded in the same answer that receivable days had risen, saying only that “AR days had gone up and we are working on it and that's the market scenario”. Two months later (Q1 FY26, 30 Jul 2025) another fund asked why cash flow was about −₹1,300 Cr in the quarter. The CFO named three drivers and sized them: delayed collections and concordat filings in Turkey, vendor payments Arena had stretched until the Paynet money arrived, and extra buying to hold share, with “it could be 1/3, 1/3, 1/3. So we are only talking about 1/3 where there is a judgment involved.” That is a rare quantified answer, and it supports the reading in Section 02 that cash flow is a function of growth and of Turkey, not of the quality of reported profit.

A recurring, not one-off, AR-provisioning pattern across three of the last four quarters

On the Q4 FY2026 call, CFO S.V. Krishnan disclosed elevated AR provisioning specifically in Saudi Arabia and against certain Indian government receivables, describing them as "cautionary provisions…we are still confident of recovery, but it's going to take some time." On the very next call (Q1 FY2027), an analyst raised the same theme again — AR provisioning in Saudi "slightly elevated" versus normal average — and management's answer was, if anything, more explicitly precautionary than resolved: "we want to be very cautious…if there are delays more than what we expect, we had to better provide for it…Can there be collections out of this in future? Very good chance." Two consecutive quarters of the same geography and the same cautious-provisioning, good-chance-of-recovery framing, without a confirmed recovery yet reported, is worth tracking as a live pattern rather than a resolved one-off (Section 09). It is also the third provisioning episode in four quarters: the Q1 FY26 Arena charge (US$8m, 0.39% of revenue against a norm near 0.10%) was called one-time, and the US$12m balance of that exposure was reported with no further provision in February and May 2026. In November 2022 the same CFO had said bad-debt provisions would be “few far and few between”. That is the ledger row marked slipped.

The incremental picture has actually been good, recently

One genuinely favourable, less-discussed number: the incremental working-capital intensity of growth — how much net working capital each additional rupee of revenue has consumed — has been much better in the last two years than the decade average. This pipeline's own three-year figure is 9.5% and five-year figure 15.6%, both well below FY2023's standalone 31% (the year revenue jumped 26.7%). Management states the arithmetic itself: at the roughly 30–35 days it guides, each rupee of extra revenue ties up about 8–10 paise, which is the 10% used in the base case in Section 13. Management's own Q1 FY2027 commentary on this quarter's specific improvement pointed to a combination of factors: "inventory days are going up across the board in the market, and that's true for us. Fortunately, we had some advantage in terms of the payable days and more particularly in Middle East…we could get some additional credit from the vendor." That is a genuinely different driver from prior years' improvements — extended vendor credit specifically in a crisis-affected region — and it is not obviously repeatable if vendor terms normalise once the West Asia situation resolves. If FY2024–26's efficiency is closer to the new normal than FY2023's, the balance-sheet drag from further growth is more manageable than the gross receivable-days trend alone would suggest; if FY2023 is what happens whenever growth accelerates sharply (as it has again in Q1 FY2027, +34% YoY, Section 07), the recent efficiency may not hold. This is precisely the assumption Section 13 has to take a position on.

Compuage Infocom: what happens when this business model goes wrong

Compuage Infocom was a genuine, long-established listed Indian IT distributor generating roughly ₹4,200 Cr of revenue as recently as FY2022. This is not a valuation comparable, but it is a sourced illustration of the tail risk this exact business model carries: a working-capital-heavy distributor with concentrated vendor and channel-partner exposure can go from a real, mid-sized competitor to insolvency inside roughly eighteen months if credit lines, vendor confidence, or channel-partner receivables sour. Redington's own AA+/A1+ ratings (Section 11) and its 34-day cash-conversion cycle are the direct counter-evidence that this is not the path Redington is on — but the existence of a recent precedent in the same industry is worth stating plainly.

In February 2026 the CEO said it was “highly possible that volumes could be under pressure while ASPs go up and margins will remain similar”, with the industry taking 12–18 months to recover component supply. 10Capital allocation: a payout cut, a shifting capex envelope, one honest wind-down

The dividend record is stronger than the earlier version of this report said — and then it was cut. The expansion record is weaker. The cash discipline is real.

Dividends: a 40% policy held for four years, then 30%

The earlier version of this report called the dividend “growing”. It is not: the rupee dividend fell from ₹532 Cr (FY25) to ₹469 Cr (FY26), ₹6.8 to ₹6.0 a share. The history in the calls is more specific. Until about 2021 the stated policy was 20% of profit (actual payout averaged about 33% in the five years to FY21). In May 2021 the then MD set out a new steady state of about 40% and paid 60% that year (including a 20% special). In May 2021 the MD described “our stated policy in the past” as 20%, and in September 2022 the CFO confirmed the change to 40%; the database shows 40.3%, 40.4% and 39.8% in FY22–FY24, then 33.1% on reported FY25 profit (40% excluding the Paynet gain, as stated on the 20 May 2025 call) and 31.5% in FY26 (30% on the ex-exceptional profit management quotes).

The sequence that led to the cut is on the record. On 6 November 2025 a fund suggested the payout be flexed with growth rather than fixed at 40% every year. The CFO’s reply conceded the logic: “we don't want to leave growth on the table at the cost of dividend”, and the item would go to the Board. Six months later the payout was 30% with the explanation “We just want to conserve cash to make sure all these are seamlessly handled.” For a shareholder the lesson is that the 40% was a policy, not a covenant, and that it was cut at the first test of high growth: the same test that FY23 had set (Section 02) without a cut. On buybacks, the CFO told the March 2025 analyst meet that more than half the business is outside India, that dividends move tax-free through the structure and buybacks do not, and that “this is a constant debate that happens in the board”. He also said that, eleven years after the 2007 IPO, the ₹138 Cr net raised “it got returned back to the shareholders, maybe a different set of shareholders in the form of a buyback”, which contradicts the earlier statement in this report that no buyback history was found. The date is not given on the call and was not checked against the filings.

Consistent with the asset-light model (Section 02), the database capex proxy is ₹981 Cr against ₹9,713 Cr of profit across FY2017–FY2026, but the proxy understates the recent run-rate: management guided ₹40–60 Cr a quarter in May 2025 and booked ₹112 Cr in Q3 FY26. Nook Micro: a strategically redundant subsidiary, wound down honestly rather than propped up

Redington set up Nook Micro Distribution Ltd in 2010 to pursue last-mile/micro-distribution into smaller Indian towns. As vendors in IT and telecom shifted toward Direct-to-Retail models, Nook Micro's niche collapsed — it ended up, in the company's own words from its FY2015-16 annual report, competing "in the same market for similar opportunities" as its own parent. By FY2015, Nook Micro's accumulated losses exceeded 50% of its net worth. The Board approved a merger of Nook Micro back into Redington effective 1 April 2015, court-sanctioned by the Madras High Court on 11 March 2016, with Nook Micro formally dissolved on 15 April 2016.

This is a decade-old, fully resolved episode, included here as a data point on capital-allocation discipline: a wholly-owned venture that became strategically redundant was recognised and shut down within roughly five years of the market shift that undermined it, disclosed frankly rather than left to bleed capital indefinitely. It is also a direct, real-world instance of the same Direct-to-Retail disintermediation risk this report flags as a standing structural feature of the distribution model (Section 01) — a risk that has since worked in Redington's favour too, in the premium-smartphone direct-to-retail build described in that same section.

Asked directly on the Q1 FY2027 call why other income had grown to roughly ₹200 Cr for FY2026, management gave a specific, two-part answer rather than a vague reference: "2 key aspects will be interest that we collect from the channel partners for the delayed payment. Second, any excess money that we have, if we deposit, there is an interest that we get…whatever is a float is what we use it for depositing…you will also see a corresponding impact on the interest cost. So ideally, some of this, you should net it off with the interest cost." A specific quarterly item — a ₹147 Cr tax refund relating to a contested prior-year demand the company won — also landed in other income this quarter, management noting plainly: "we contested. We won the case, we got the money back…not an issue." This is a rare case in this report's research where a company volunteered exactly how to read a normally opaque other-income line rather than leaving it as an unexplained number.

Redington's FY2025-26 annual report discloses, for the first time in over a decade of this pipeline's related-party audit coverage, a transaction with an entity in which a sitting KMP has a personal interest: Third Wave Power Pte Ltd, described in the Ind AS 24 note only as "entity in which director (Mr. V S Hariharan) has interest."   • The Secretarial Auditor's language and the Board's own summary of it do not fully square.   • The commercial rationale is not disclosed anywhere this research found. Third Wave Power is an off-grid solar-hardware venture; Redington operates a growing solar-distribution business (Redington Solar, Redington Green Energy Ltd in Kenya, the April 2026 FIMER partnership) and it is plausible the transaction runs through that channel — but this is this report's own inference, not a sourced fact.

Incentives: ROCE is a gate, and the ROCE management reports is the flattering one

The pay design has not changed in ten years. In 2016 the then MD said that “we measure everyone and compensate everyone with two parameters”, one of which was return on capital employed; in February 2021 he said that “delivering on the RoCE is one key KRA on which their performance payout is hinged on”; and at the March 2025 analyst meet it was put bluntly: “ROCE is a gate.” If revenue and profit targets are exceeded but the ROCE threshold is missed, “We do not achieve the specified threshold ROCE, we get zilch.” This is the right design for a thin-margin business and explains why management accepts low-margin large deals only on a ROCE test. The analytical caution is that the ROCE management quotes (22% in Q4 FY26 and Q1 FY27; stated on a net-debt basis in June 2020 and not re-defined on later calls) is far above this pipeline’s EBIT-over-capital measure (16.7% in FY26) and above Malik’s stricter cross-check (7.08%), because a net-debt-based figure treats payables and cash as reducing the capital employed. A metric that rises when vendor credit is stretched can be improved by treasury choices as well as by operations; the Q1 FY24 episode, when vendors rolled back credit and working capital went to 40 days, is the case where it moved against management for reasons it did not control. The 16% floor stated on 5 Feb 2026 is the number to hold them to.

War-risk insurance, and credit insurance

Two insurance facts from the calls are worth weighing alongside the ratings. Credit insurance covers most of India and the Middle East (about 96% of Middle East receivables and 73–74% of India at the COVID update of April 2020, the insurer paying up to 85% of a default), but in Turkey and Africa it is thin: asked in July 2025 whether the Arena provision was insured, the answer was “There is an insurance, but the coverage has been quite small.” And in the West Asia conflict the CFO said that insurers “as the war started, the insurance companies joined together with 7 day’s notice removed this risk coverage, and it was an unilateral decision.” Management re-covered most of it and moved stock between warehouses, and says it saw only a period of “increased retention”; this is a single-source account of an event that is still live, with a region that is 30–35% of the business.

No evidence of recurrence or escalation after 2023 was found, but it belongs in the same governance picture as the Third Wave Power item — both concern the same individual, three years apart.

Turkey: the one genuinely structural weak point in an otherwise clean profile

Redington's Turkish operations sit inside a macro environment CRISIL itself names as a credit-relevant headwind: lira depreciation, inflation, and local borrowing costs management describes as running "42%, 45%." Redington has been actively de-risking — halving Arena's revenue base by deliberate choice (from roughly $1 billion to $0.5 billion) — but continuing losses, a fresh FY2026 trade-name impairment (₹75.2 Cr impact on group PAT after minority interest), and management's own newly-disclosed reconsideration of the original exit thesis (Section 09) mean this remains a live, unresolved exposure. The nine-year chain of Arena statements and outcomes is in Section 09; the relevant balance-sheet facts are that the investment is carried at about US$8m, the listed market value is about US$75m (May 2026), debt is about US$90m (Jul 2026) and Redington has said since 2018 that it has put in no capital and no guarantee. Management's own internal strategic language files Turkey under "Emerging Geographies" — the company's own, higher-risk bucket — alongside Africa and South Asia.

Litigation and regulatory: routine, not escalating

Redington has actively litigated a series of GST and income-tax disputes spanning FY2018–FY2022 assessment years, and has largely won: a ₹91.74 Cr GST demand quashed (October 2025), a ₹136.25 Cr income-tax demand quashed (February 2026), a ₹230.21 Cr income-tax demand quashed (March 2026, the source of the ₹147 Cr refund noted in Section 10), against one still-pending ₹148.33 Cr GST assessment the company states it does not expect to be material. This is a fairly ordinary pattern for a large Indian distribution business. No SEBI enforcement action, and no customs/import-duty litigation, was found — the latter is a gap, not a confirmed absence.

The discount rate, and the exit-multiple cross-check

The rate matters more than the earlier version of this report allowed. At a 10% discount rate the base case is ₹389, close to the price; at 11% it is ₹308; at 12% ₹251. A 12% WACC is defensible for a business whose cash flow depends on vendors and on the Turkish and Gulf macro, whose borrower rating is AA+, and whose net debt is 0.17× equity; 10% is what an investor accepting the AA+ rating at face value would use. Neither is wrong, which is why the value grade below says “full” rather than “expensive”.

The exit-multiple cross-check projects the same base case five years (growth 15%, 13.3%, 11.5%, 9.8%, 8%; EBITDA 2.3%; interest 0.4% of sales; tax 23%), which gives FY31 sales of about ₹205,106 Cr and profit of about ₹2,716 Cr (net margin 1.32%, consistent with the 1.3% the company reports), plus a 35% payout, discounted at 12%. At exit multiples of 14×, 16×, 18× and 21× (today’s) of FY31 earnings the value per share is ₹312, ₹351, ₹391 and ₹450. So the price corresponds to an 18–19× multiple on FY31 profit, against 21.2× on reported FY26 profit, 18.6× on the last four reported quarters (including the impairment) and 17.1× for Rashi Peripherals, the one listed Indian peer. Which to weight: the DCF charges explicitly for the working capital that growth consumes and the P/E frame embeds it only through the interest line, so the DCF is the primary frame; but a P/E of 18× on a 1.3% net margin with an AA+ rating is not an extreme price, and I give the cross-check enough weight that the value grade is “full”. Minority interest in Arena (about ₹4 a share at the market value management quoted) is not deducted.

14Verdict, and what would change our mind

Quality and value, kept on separate axes — this is not a buy or sell call

Grades, and the scale

Quality B+. (A: high and consistent returns, cash conversion, governance and a management whose forward statements hold. B: good, with named weaknesses.) Value C, “full”. (A: price well below base-case value. B: near it. C: above base-case value but inside the range of defensible assumptions. D: needs the bull case on every input.) At ₹404 the price is 61% above the 12% base case, 4.6% below the bull case, and about equal to the 10% base case.

Quality: strong on structure and money, weak on turnarounds and on the margin guide

For: two AA+/A1+ ratings; a board-led, no-promoter structure with 24% held by a strategic distributor; net debt of 0.17× equity; return on capital that clears its own 16% floor every quarter reported; a 40% payout held for four years; financial promises that were kept far more often than not (free cash flow in named quarters, no capital for Arena, a COVID forecast beaten by a wide margin); a CFO who has given the same quantified answers for ten years; and a cash conversion shortfall (0.66×) that is now explained by the mechanics of 12.6% annual growth with a 40% payout. Against: 14 broken and 35 slipped promises out of 95 decided, concentrated in anything described as a repair; an EBITDA band cut three times since 2023 while each version was called steady; a payout cut at the first test; Arena as a nine-year recurring item; an incentive metric (ROCE on a net-debt basis) that is much more favourable than the pipeline’s own measure and Malik’s; a leadership change in 2023 not explained on any call; and a first-ever director-linked related-party item that no call has touched. None is disqualifying. The grade stays where the earlier version put it, but the reasons are different: the earlier worry about unexplained cash is mostly answered, and the management-credibility worry, which the two-call window could not see, is the main reason it is not an A.

Value: full

The price requires a flat 2.87% EBITDA margin for ten years at a 12% discount rate, against a guided 2.2–2.4% and a best-ever 3.08% in the shortage year; or a 10% discount rate; or an 18–19× multiple on FY31 profit. It does not require anything absurd. It requires either the margin recovery that management has been walking back or an acceptance of a lower return than most investors would demand from a distributor whose cash flow rests on vendors. The analysis changes at about ₹250 (the base case at 12%) to ₹310 (the base case at 11%); below ₹310 the value grade moves to B. Above about ₹450 (a 21× exit multiple on FY31 earnings) the price exceeds every central case.
