The one-paragraph version
A sound fastener business inside a group that has not earned its capital back, with a price that already pays for the recovery: quality C, value stretched

Sterling Tools is India's second-largest automotive fastener maker (87% of FY26 revenue of ₹828 Cr) wrapped around an electric-vehicle components group that was 37% of FY25 revenue and is about 13% now. The fastener operating margin has fallen from 20.5% (FY17) to 14.4% and was 11.3% in the quarter reported today; group return on capital employed has fallen from 32.9% to 8.5%. We read all 17 earnings calls and tested 43 dated statements against what followed: 6 kept, 10 slipped, 22 broken, 5 unresolved. Management is reliable on money it controls and on one early warning (the FY26 revenue fall) and unreliable on fastener margin, on customer concentration and on the dates of new products.

The subsidiary's anchor customer built the part itself and revenue fell about ₹270 Cr in a year; the company's filing then described the rating agency's outlook cut as an upward rating. In six months standalone borrowings rose from ₹90 Cr to ₹154 Cr, operating cash flow turned negative, the third finance head in under 11 months took office today, and debt to operating profit (2.8 times) is above the agency's 1.5 times downgrade trigger ahead of its November review. Related-party amounts are small; the exposure is a ₹111 Cr guarantee and ₹100 Cr of cash in loss-making subsidiaries with no stated return.

Valuation leads with the reverse DCF: the price needs a flat 16.1% operating margin on all group revenue from FY27 (FY26: 9.3%; fasteners: 14.4%). Scenario DCF: bear below zero, base ₹62, bull ₹118 (probability-weighted ₹57); exit-multiple cross-check at 10 times: ₹131, ₹221, ₹297; book value ₹145. The value grade would move to fair at or below about ₹150. This is not a buy or sell call.

01The business: India’s second-largest automotive fastener maker, 87% of sales, wrapped around an EV subsidiary group that was 37% of FY25 revenue and is about 13% now

What it sells, who buys it, what the moat is in management’s own words, and which half of the company carries the profit

Sterling Tools Ltd (Faridabad, founded 1979) makes high-tensile cold-forged fasteners — engine, transmission, chassis and specialised parts — for Indian vehicle makers. ICRA, the only rating agency covering it, calls it “the second-largest automotive fastener manufacturer in India” behind Sundram Fasteners (2019 to 2025 rationales). It runs plants in Faridabad, Palwal (Haryana) and Bengaluru (commissioned in 2019), and reports consolidated revenue of Rs 828 Cr for FY2026 (year to March 2026), of which the fastener business (the parent company, “standalone”) is Rs 717 Cr or 87%. On the company’s own measure, which adds other income back, standalone EBITDA was Rs 111 Cr (15.3%); on ours, operating profit before other income was Rs 101.8 Cr (14.2%) — section 4 reconciles the two. At Rs 210.20 on 27 October 2026 the market capitalisation is Rs 766 Cr, 1.45 times consolidated book value of Rs 145 a share.

The company has a second, separate story. Since 2020 it has built an electric-vehicle components group on licensed Chinese, Korean and British technology: Sterling E-Mobility Solutions Ltd (SEM, formerly Sterling Gtake E-Mobility; motor control units, chargers, DC/DC converters, motors), Sterling Tech-Mobility Ltd (STML; high-voltage DC contactors and relays, built with Kunshan GLVAC), and Sterling Advanced Electric Machines Pvt Ltd (rare-earth-free motors licensed from the UK’s Advanced Electric Machines). All three are 100% owned. The next two sections show that these are two businesses with opposite economics, and that the second has so far produced the growth and then taken it back.

Who buys the fasteners, and how concentrated it is

ICRA’s FY2025 split of standalone revenue is commercial vehicles about 24%, passenger vehicles 27%, two-wheelers 26% and tractors 10%; the largest customer is about 20% of standalone revenue and the top five about 61% (ICRA, 10 November 2025). Management’s own FY2024 split, given on the Q4 FY24 call, was passenger vehicles 27%, two-wheelers 24%, commercial vehicles 22%, farm equipment 13%, retail and aftermarket 12% and exports 2%. Exports were 7–9% of consolidated revenue in FY2020–FY2022 and are now 1% (ICRA). The named customers are Maruti Suzuki, Honda Motorcycles and Scooters India (HMSI), Tata, Ashok Leyland and Mahindra; Hyundai-Kia and Tata Passenger Vehicles are recent additions (on the Q4 FY25 call of May 2025 the Managing Director referred to the “SOP starting with Hyundai this year”, that is FY26; Tata Passenger Vehicles entry in FY26 per the Q4 FY26 call).

Management is unusually candid about the hole in this customer list. On the Q3 FY24 call (2 February 2024) the Managing Director explained why standalone sales had stopped growing:

“It is in passenger vehicle, Hyundai Kia and Tata PCBU has grown dramatically. Unfortunately, they do not part from us. They are not part of our customer portfolio which is what we are trying to address.”

— Atul Aggarwal, Managing Director, Q3 FY24 earnings call, 2 February 2024 (the transcript’s own text) KEPT

That is the structural point about the fastener franchise: it is deep with the incumbents who are growing slowly (Maruti, Honda, tractors, commercial vehicles) and was, until FY2026, absent from the fastest-growing passenger-vehicle and two-wheeler accounts. The Hyundai-Kia and Tata PV wins are the repair, and management says they take time to show: “these opportunities take about 24 months to crystallize into revenue” (Q3 FY25 call).

What the moat is, in management’s words

The claims are three, and each needs a scepticism flag because none has an independent source. First, scale and rank: “we are anywhere between 25% and 30% of market share in the country” for domestic automotive fasteners, stated with the caveat that “there's no hard published data on market shares in India” (Q4 FY26 call, 18 May 2026). Second, sticky supply positions: on the Q2 FY23 call the Managing Director said of displacing a competitor that “it's not easy to dislodge a competitive player completely”. That cuts both ways — it protects Sterling’s accounts and keeps it out of Hyundai’s and Tata’s. Third, pricing mechanics: steel is passed through with a lag and conversion costs are not (ICRA, December 2023 and November 2024), and in May 2026 management said “we don't have any price reduction contracts, nowhere. Prices remain constant.” Section 4 tests that against the Q4 FY25 call, where the same management took provisions against steel price reductions.

Capacity is the practical limit. Management put installed capacity at about Rs 800–850 Cr of revenue (Q3 FY25) and utilisation at “90% to 95% utilization” in August 2026; a Rs 80 Cr capex programme is meant to lift that towards Rs 900–1,000 Cr. Standalone return on capital is 13% (Screener), against 18% at Sundram.

Two businesses with opposite economics

The fastener business is capital-light by Indian auto-component standards (standalone net-block turnover of 2.9 times in FY26), earns a 14% operating margin, generates cash (standalone operating cash flow Rs 83.3 Cr in FY26 per the Q4 FY26 call) and has been profitable every year. The EV group is the opposite. SEM buys the electronic components, which are imported (“We are at about 50% import and 50% domestic value add” on its best-selling models, Aug 2022) and assembles, tests and customises software on top, at an operating margin of 6.8% in FY25 (ICRA) falling to 1.2% in Q1 FY26. It is working-capital heavy and, until FY26, sold almost entirely to one customer. As of the Q2 FY27 results (27 October 2026) the gap between consolidated and standalone profit before exceptional items is about Rs 9 Cr a quarter. Everything else in this report is a statement about which of these two businesses the price is paying for.

Part one, FY2017–FY2019: a high-margin compounder that spent its balance sheet

Revenue went from Rs 371 Cr to Rs 512 Cr and net profit from Rs 39 Cr to Rs 49 Cr in FY18, with an operating margin above 20% and a return on capital of 33%. Two things changed in FY19. Raw-material inflation, passed on with a lag, took the operating margin to 16.8% (ICRA, September 2019, single-source: there was no management call until May 2022), and the company spent Rs 95 Cr of capex, most of it on the Bengaluru greenfield plant, taking borrowings from Rs 34 Cr to Rs 99 Cr and return on capital from 28% to 19%. The Bengaluru plant is the capital the rest of the record has to earn a return on: management put the investment at “close to Rs. 200 crores, it is about Rs. 180 odd crores right now” (Q3 FY24 call), and said in August 2024 that it was running at “about 45%, 50% levels of where it should be”.

Part two, FY2020–FY2021: the stress years

The BS-IV to BS-VI transition and then the lockdown cut sales 28.9% in FY20 and a further 2.4% in FY21 (standalone −28.9% and −2.5% against SIAM total domestic units of −18.0% and −13.6%). Margins held: standalone EBITDA margin was 16.8% and 18.6%, and ICRA wrote in August 2021 that revenue was down only 3% for the year “despite the sharp 81% contraction reported in Q1 FY2021”. Debt/EBITDA reached 1.7 times in FY21, above ICRA’s 1.5 times tripwire, without a downgrade (section 10). ICRA moved the outlook to Negative in May 2020 and back to Stable in August 2021. This is also the period of Marcellus Investment Managers’ exit, on accounting-quality flags (section 12), and of the Gtake joint-venture announcement.

Part three, FY2022–FY2025: the EV boom

Consolidated revenue went from Rs 355 Cr to Rs 1,026 Cr in four years; Rs 342 Cr of the Rs 516 Cr added between FY22 and FY25 came from the EV subsidiary. Net profit doubled (Rs 25 Cr to Rs 58 Cr) but return on capital only recovered from 8.6% to 13.2%, because the added revenue earned a roughly 7% margin on a working-capital-heavy model, and because the operating margin fell from 17.5% to 10.7%. Management framed the first fall as a mathematical artefact of passing steel through:

“But I am not unduly worried on the fall of margin numbers. We have seen this in the past also I believe and things start softening up, margins will go back dramatically up again”

— Atul Aggarwal, Q4 FY22 earnings call, 19 May 2022 (the first call the company ever held) BROKEN

They did not. Standalone margin was 14.4%, 14.0% and 13.8% in FY23, FY24 and FY25 (Screener), and the consolidated margin kept falling as the lower-margin business grew faster. Part of the FY25 profit was also carried by large non-cash provisions: the consolidated cash-flow statement adds back Rs 25.6 Cr of inventory provision (net) and Rs 22.8 Cr of warranty provision created in FY25. The retrieved filings do not say which entity or product they relate to; the Q4 FY25 call says only that “last quarter is also a quarter where we take a lot of one-time provisions”. Cause not found beyond that.

Part four, FY2026: the customer leaves

Consolidated revenue fell 19.3% to Rs 828 Cr and EBITDA margin to 9.3%. Reported profit after tax of Rs 29.3 Cr is flattered and depressed by three items that largely cancel: a Rs 9.5 Cr pre-tax Delhi Metro Rail land-compensation receipt booked as an exceptional item (Rs 6.2 Cr of it interest; the land was acquired “in an earlier year”, the FY26 report says), a Rs 14.4 Cr provision against a disputed SEM receivable, and a Rs 8.8 Cr reversal of earlier inventory provisions. Profit before tax excluding the Delhi Metro item is Rs 38.2 Cr; adding back the receivable provision gives Rs 52.6 Cr, which is the closest this report can get to underlying FY26 earnings. Operating cash flow fell to Rs 56 Cr and, as section 5 shows, turned negative in the first half of FY27.

04Margins: the standalone fall since FY19 is labour and consumables, not steel; the consolidated fall is a 7% business losing its customer; and Q2 FY27 broke the “straight pass-through” story for a quarter

The margin bridge, a cost-line test of management’s explanation, pricing power, and the quarter just reported

Did the business get worse, or the balance sheet?

The question the bridge answers is whether the profit margin fell because the operating business got worse or because depreciation and interest moved. Over FY18–FY22 it was operating: of a 982 bp fall in the pre-tax margin, 707 bp was the EBITDA margin, and depreciation and interest took the rest. Over the last two years, FY24 to FY26, the pre-tax margin fell 199 bp: the operating line -229 bp and depreciation -127 bp, partly offset by other income (+174 bp, of which about 115 bp is a one-off). This is not a deleveraging story. Interest is only 1.2% of sales; the margin fell because the operating profit did.

Management’s explanation, and a test of it

Between 2022 and 2026 the company explained its margin on nearly every call with one argument, the “numerator-denominator effect”: steel is passed through to customers at cost, which raises revenue without raising profit, so the percentage margin falls even though absolute profit is unchanged. It put the effect at “more than 3% on this is on account of mathematics only”, then “I think that impact of that numerator denominator closer to 2.5%”, and in August 2023 stated the practical conclusion in terms that moved the goalposts:

“from a pure mathematical basis, that 18% benchmark what we had earlier is coming down to 15% on a like-to-like basis.”

— Atul Aggarwal, Q1 FY24 earnings call, 8 August 2023

The argument is right for FY22 and FY23: standalone raw-material cost rose from 34.4% of revenue in FY21 to 40.3% in FY22 and 42.6% in FY23, and management said so at the time (“Typically, our raw material content before price increase used to be about 37%, has gone up to about 41%-42% now”, Q1 FY24). But the cost-line table below puts it to the test over the longer period and it fails. By FY26 raw material is 38.5% of revenue, 1.9 points lower than in FY19 (40.4%), yet the operating margin is 2.6 points lower (14.2% against 16.8%) and, measured on revenue less raw material so that the steel pass-through drops out of the denominator, it has fallen from 28.1% to 23.1%. The gap to FY19 is a cost-to-convert gap.

What the cost lines do support

Management’s claim that energy, chemicals and wages were not recovered is borne out: permanent and contract labour together were 9.5% of revenue in FY19 and are 13.4% in FY26; stores and consumables 8.4% to 9.3%. Job-work (outsourcing) is flat at 4.5% against 4.7% in FY19, so the margin has not been given away to subcontractors. The Q1 FY27 call confirms wage pressure in its own words and adds that for the first time the company has ‘taken up this matter up strongly with all our customers’ — four years after the cost increases began.

Two measures of margin

Management’s EBITDA includes other income, which for the fastener company is mostly interest on bank deposits. That flatters the margin by about 1.1 points and explains why the company reports 15.3% for FY26 where the operating margin on revenue is 14.2%. The pattern

Three things are consistently delivered. Statements about money the company controls come true: the capex budgets for FY24 and FY25, the clearance of the export-obligation liability that Marcellus flagged, and, three years late, the inventory improvement. Warnings given early come true: the “down year” call on 14 May 2025 was a day after the year-end and exactly right. Growth in the fastener business has come in near or above what was said when the guide was modest (FY23 and FY26).

Three things are consistently not. The fastener margin has been guided back to 16–19% on six calls over three years and has not exceeded 15.3% on management’s own basis; the goalposts moved from “back to 18%” (May 2022) to a 16–19% range (November 2022), to 17–18% for FY24 and FY25 (February 2023), to 16% described as “I would not say it is a change in guidance” (May 2023), to 15% on a “like-to-like” basis (August 2023), and now to “15% plus”. The tripwires, and where the company stands against them

The downgrade trigger, unchanged in wording since October 2022, is “total debt/OPBITDA above 1.5 times on a sustained basis”. The scenarios are our assumptions, not forecasts. 15Verdict: quality C, value stretched, and the points at which each would change

Two grades, kept separate; not a buy or sell call

The two axes

Quality: C. A good fastener franchise inside a group whose return on capital is 8.5%, whose management has missed on margin, customers and dates in most of what it said, and whose balance sheet has turned in the last six months. Value: stretched. The price needs the whole group to earn a 16% margin; the cash-flow value is a fraction of the price and the exit-multiple value is at it. A high-quality business can be expensive and a low-quality one cheap; here the two grades agree in direction without being one judgement.

The case in four findings

  1. The fastener business is sound and no longer exceptional. It is the second-largest in India, with a stable customer base and collections better than any peer, but its operating margin fell from 20.5% to 14.4% as steel was passed through and labour and consumables were not, and in Q2 FY27 it fell to 11.3% in the quarter after management said it did not see EBITDA getting weaker. The peers lost much less over the same period.
  2. The EV diversification has so far cost more than it has earned. One customer was over 94% of the subsidiary’s sales and built the part itself; revenue fell from about Rs 380 Cr to about Rs 100 Cr in a year. Management said the opposite repeatedly (section 9). Break-even now needs roughly Rs 245 Cr of revenue from a base of about Rs 110 Cr, while Rs 100 Cr of cash and a Rs 111 Cr guarantee are already committed.
  3. The balance sheet turned in six months. Standalone borrowings rose from Rs 90 Cr to Rs 154 Cr, consolidated operating cash flow was Rs −21 Cr, and debt to operating profit is 2.8 times against ICRA’s sustained-breach trigger of 1.5 times, with ICRA’s review due in November. The description of the fastener business as net debt free was true of March and not of September.
  4. The price already pays for the recovery. It needs a flat 16.1% group margin, or the base case with a 9.6 times exit multiple. The company’s own year-end multiples have a median of 10.8 times; today it trades at 11.5 times a profit that is a third below its FY25 peak.

What would change each conclusion

Quality rises to C+ or B- if the fastener margin holds at or above 14.5% through the steel cycle with price increases visible, both subsidiaries show revenue on the path to break-even and operating cash flow turns positive in H2, and the finance function is stable for a year. It falls to D if debt to operating profit stays above 2.5 times at March 2027 with an ICRA outlook cut, or if the disputed receivable is provided further. The value grade moves to fair at or below about Rs 150, which is the price where the fastener business plus the subsidiaries at cost covers the price on our base margin, and toward attractive only if the price falls with the margin evidence intact. Above about Rs 300 the model would need a bull-case margin and a 12 times exit together.
