The one-paragraph version

Quality: good, with the margin and the water receivable still unproven. Value: rich. The price needs an 11.4% EBITDA margin; the company has reported 8.2–9.2% for five years running.

What the ten calls show. Management has delivered what sits inside its working-capital and balance-sheet discipline: net working capital under 100 days, finance cost under 2% of sales, PBT margin up 120 bp in FY26, order inflow above guide in FY25. But across 37 dated or quantified statements made between May 2024 and August 2026, 11 were kept, 11 slipped, 11 broke and 4 are still open. The broken ones are not only third-party forecasts: the EBITDA path, the capex trajectory (“should start going down” then ₹917 Cr), “no dilution” (then a ₹1,000 Cr QIP two months later) and the ECL “one-off” were all inside KPIL’s control. On water, the stock of dues has sat at roughly ₹1,400–1,600 Cr for six quarters while the collection date was re-set at least six times.

What the numbers show. The FY24–26 PBT margin rose 148 bp; −28 bp came from EBITDA and +176 bp from interest, depreciation and other income. On the company’s own definition EBITDA margin was 8.3%, 8.2%, 8.3%: flat. The 13% to 9% fall since FY19 happened in one step in FY2022 and has not recovered in four years, for KPIL or its peers. Cash conversion is real (cumulative CFO 1.76× PAT), but charged with capex and interest the decade is −₹1,823 Cr (Gate 3), −₹2,615 Cr using company-disclosed capex for FY21–26.

What the price needs. At ₹1,414.80 the reverse DCF requires 11.4% EBITDA margin for ten years (10.7% if growth runs at management’s 15%). The base DCF is ₹984, the weighted value ₹1,021 (28% below market), bull ₹1,517. These match the prior report’s methodology exactly (its ₹984 and 11.5% reproduce to the rupee). On an exit-multiple frame the stock returns about 11.6% a year at 15% earnings growth and a 20× exit: fair, not cheap.

Two axes. Quality: B+ (cash conversion, balance sheet, execution on controllables good; EBITDA flat; Gate 3 negative; promoter-pledge and related-party questions unresolved). Value: unattractive at this price; the analysis changes near ₹1,000, where the price needs the 9.5% margin the base case already assumes. Triggers with thresholds are in Section 15. This is analysis, not advice.

01The business, and what the transcripts say the money depends on

What you are buying, in the company’s own words, and where the transcripts say the money is made

Kalpataru Projects International is an engineering-procurement-construction contractor: it builds infrastructure to order, mostly on other people’s balance sheets. It is the merged entity of Kalpataru Power Transmission (towers, lines and substations, with its own tower plants at Raipur and Gandhinagar) and JMC Projects (buildings, water, urban infrastructure), amalgamated with effect from 4 January 2023 with an appointed date of 1 April 2022 (CRISIL, July 2024). The legal merger did not change the consolidated accounts, because JMC had been a consolidated subsidiary since at least FY2018 (the FY2019 annual report: “Your Company holds 67.19% equity shares of JMC”). The promoter family also controls Kalpataru Limited, a separately listed real-estate developer, which matters for governance (Section 12).

Is there a moat? Three narrow advantages, one missing one

EPC is structurally a poor industry: work is won by tender, the contractor carries fixed-price execution risk, and the client is often a government body that pays late. KPIL’s advantages are narrow and the transcripts describe them better than the annual report does.

  • Owned equipment and plant. The CEO’s stated philosophy, May 2026: “We as an organization are a firm believer that in the EPC business, having your own capex is a big advantage, at least on large-scale projects because these are projects where you start everything at the same time.” Five-year capex was nearly ₹3,000 Cr. The cost of that belief is in Section 11: capex ran above guide in FY26 by 22% over the raised ceiling. Backward integration now extends to a rolling mill at Raipur, announced on the Q1 FY27 call with no cost, capacity or timeline.
  • Design and scale. A design centre of “more than 500 people” (May 2026; 300 to 400 a year earlier), average order size up from ₹280 Cr (FY24) to ₹390 Cr (FY25) to above ₹500 Cr, and eight to ten orders above ₹1,000 Cr that make up about half the book. The CEO’s argument is about returns, not margin: “margin percentages do not vary significantly… What is different is your ROCE numbers.” (Q4 FY25).
  • International qualification. The CEO says international T&D earns double-digit margins and 35–40% ROCE in Latin America and Africa, with Middle East ROCE “very low because the retention terms are very high” (Q4 FY24). International sites run at 50–60 days of working capital against 85–100 days domestically (Q4 FY25). These are management claims; no audited geographic margin exists.
  • Not a moat: pricing power. Asked whether PGCIL contracts allow cost pass-through, the CEO answered on 5 February 2026:

“I think majority of our transmission projects are fixed price projects, and we have to load the expected increase in cost at a tender level itself.”

Manish Mohnot, MD & CEO, Q3 FY26 call (5 Feb 2026)

Concentration, stated twice and not reconciled

Management has said two incompatible things about customer concentration in B&F, its largest-order-book business after T&D. In August 2025, asked about client spread:

“We do not see any client concentration risk in any form because majority of the projects which we’re executing have already been launched and significant sales are visible in those projects.”

Manish Mohnot, Q1 FY26 call (Aug 2025). He named Prestige, DLF, Godrej, Puravankara and Brigade as examples of the clients.

Two months later, asked about the outlook for the same business:

“There are 5 or 6 clients who constitute more than 75% to 80% of our order book.”

Manish Mohnot, Q2 FY26 call (Oct 2025)

Both can be true: the clients are large and the risk is that few. At group level the May 2026 call adds “more than 50% of our order book, if not 60% comes from our top 10 clients”, one of them a 25-year relationship. The protection management cites is RERA, which holds buyer money in escrow, so the developer’s sales rather than its balance sheet fund the contractor. That protects against payment default; it does not protect against one developer slowing launches. No analyst has asked which developer the 12 to 14 million sq ft order is with.

Labour is the stated binding constraint

Management returns to labour on most calls, from Q4 FY24 to Q1 FY27. The strongest statement:

“So today, if you ask me the number one challenge is not order book, not revenue, not growth, not profitability, it’s only about resources, resources and resources.”

Manish Mohnot, Q1 FY25 call (Aug 2024)

CRISIL puts employee attrition at about 22% in FY24 and 20% in FY25; by May 2026 the CEO listed keeping the team together against attrition as his fourth and largest priority. That is the constraint behind the growth guidance in Section 07, and it is a cost that does not show up in a capex line.

Management makes comparative claims that cannot be tested from the corpus and are recorded here as claims: that KPIL is “among the top 2, 3 globally in all markets, except China in the T&D space” (Q3 FY26), and “among the top 3 EPC contractors” in Sweden (Q1 FY25). Across all ten calls neither management nor any analyst named a single competitor; analysts refer to “peers” and “competitors” without names. Peers are therefore built externally (Section 06).

Why the Gate 3 figure widened twice

The module’s default capex is the investing outflow, which nets disposals against purchases. In the year of the Vindhyachal sale that reads zero against ₹917 Cr actually spent. The same distortion runs through earlier years: the CEO said in May 2024 that “in the last four years… we have invested closer to INR 1,700 crores in capex”, while the proxy sums to ₹809 Cr for those four years, because divestments and investment sales sit inside investing flow. Replacing the proxy with the company’s own numbers moves Gate 3 from −₹906 Cr (the module default and the first revision) to −₹1,823 Cr (second revision, FY26 only) to −₹2,615 Cr. Add ₹1,000 Cr of dividends over the decade and the shortfall is ₹3,615 Cr. It was funded by the ₹1,000 Cr QIP of December 2024, the Vindhyachal proceeds, the Indore real-estate collections and borrowings that peaked in FY25 at ₹4,314 Cr. The Screener “interest” line used here (₹693 Cr in FY26) is larger than the finance cost management reports (1.8% of sales, ~₹490 Cr), so Gate 3 charges KPIL for more than its headline finance cost; even at the lower figure FY26 is about break-even, not positive.

The asset-turnover tell does not survive the perimeter check

The previous revision called a rise in consolidated turnover from 2.3× to 8.5× against a falling margin “textbook” subcontracting. The annual reports say otherwise about the denominator. Consolidated net block plus capital work in progress was ₹3,100–3,800 Cr through FY17–22, but the standalone company’s net property, plant and equipment was ₹578 Cr in FY2019, ₹633 Cr in FY2020 and ₹618 Cr in FY2022 (FY2019, FY2020 and FY2022 annual reports, management discussion). The rest was concession assets: the transmission BOOT and IPTC assets that the FY2020 report says were agreed for sale (“we took significant steps to sell four T&D BOOT/IPTC Assets”), road SPVs, and warehouses. The FY2019 report shows ₹922 Cr of consolidated debt “pertaining to Assets held for sale”, and the FY2020 report ₹786 Cr. As those assets were sold or reclassified, consolidated turnover rose without any change in how work was executed.

On the standalone numbers asset turnover was roughly flat at 11–12.5× across FY2019–22 while margin fell, so the Malik tell does not fire on them either. What the corpus does show about subcontracting is narrower: management said the Aramco project is “everything… into subcontracting and there’s no bought out there” (Q4 FY25), and, asked on Q1 FY27 whether international projects are subcontracted, the CEO said “project as a whole, we never subcontract” but components of civil, fabrication and erection are. The honest verdict is inconclusive: the cost-line notes that would settle it (materials, erection, subcontract) were stripped from the annual-report markdown (Section 16).

03The ten-year record, and a cause for every anomaly

What the screen found, what it missed, and a cause for every metric the anomaly scan flagged

The pipeline’s coffee-can screen scores KPIL a Dirty 9iner: revenue growth above 10% in eight of nine scored years and ROCE above 15% in all nine on the Mukherjea definition (debt plus equity). The one miss is FY2021, when revenue grew 2.2% through COVID. On the screen’s own terms that is close to a perfect decade, and it is the reason this company is on the list at all.

The screen tests growth and return. It does not test the price of that growth, and the same decade produced a fall in operating margin from 13% to 9%.

Three EBITDA margins for the same year

Before reading any margin in this report, know which one it is. For FY2025 the Screener operating-profit margin is 9.10% (₹2,031 Cr). The company reports EBITDA of ₹1,834 Cr, 8.2% (Q4 FY25 call, confirmed in the FY2026 annual report’s comparative: “EBITDA of INR 2,240 Crore as against INR 1,834 Crore… 8.3% as against 8.2%”). CRISIL’s adjusted figure is 9.4%, and CARE’s PBILDT margin is 9.1% for FY24. The gap between Screener and the company has been ₹186 Cr (0.95 pp) in FY24, ₹197 Cr (0.88 pp) in FY25 and ₹192 Cr (0.71 pp) in FY26; Screener’s line appears to include some operating income the company classifies below EBITDA, but the annual-report cost notes that would show which were stripped from the markdown (Section 16). The consequence matters for one number: the −28 bp that EBITDA contributes to the FY24–26 PBT bridge (Section 04) is entirely the narrowing of that gap. On the company’s definition the margin was 8.3%, 8.2%, 8.3%. The conclusion, no operating expansion in two years, holds on every basis; the sign of the small change does not.

A correction to the previous revision

The previous revision wrote that the FY2022 step (12.15% to 8.77%) was “the JMC consolidation diluting a higher-margin transmission business”. JMC was already consolidated: KPTL held 67.19% of it in FY2019 and the FY2022 annual report speaks of “notable order booking for KPTL and JMC” and reports consolidated revenue of ₹14,777 Cr against a standalone KPTL revenue of ₹7,062 Cr; the difference is mostly JMC. The FY2022 report’s own explanation is “Our EBITDA margin remained healthy at 9% in 2021-22, which was lower than 2020-21 primarily due to cost inflation”, plus provisioning for loans in a subsidiary. A second, partial, cause is visible in the arithmetic: in FY2020 the consolidated margin (12.17%) sat 1.3 points above the standalone company’s (10.9%, ₹860 Cr on ₹7,904 Cr), consistent with high-margin concession income in the consolidation; by FY2022 consolidated (8.8%) sat below the standalone core (9.2%, ₹653 Cr on ₹7,062 Cr) after the transmission BOOT assets were sold. The size of that effect cannot be measured from the corpus.

Why this matters more than anything else in the report

Deleveraging is a one-time gain. Net debt on management’s basis is ₹917 Cr (0.1× equity), interest is about ₹270 Cr a year, and Q1 FY27 shows the benefit still arriving. There is little left. Management said so in May 2026: the 75 bp PBT guide is “a mix of margins coming from the business segment as well as some balance sheet improvement because, as you rightly said, our debt levels are low.”

  • Legacy low-margin orders are finished. “on the legacy order, I don’t think I have anything left now, maybe on INR 63,000 crores, maybe INR 1,000-odd crores” (Q3 FY26).   • Brazil is gone, roads are mostly gone. Fasttel consolidated revenue is zero and the ₹515 Cr standalone investment is fully provided. Removing a drag of roughly one point is worth a visible step in FY27 EBITDA, partly offset by losing road income.
  • Water’s drag is sized at 100–200 bp of EBITDA and is mostly interest on unpaid bills: “The dent is only coming because of interest cost, interest costs typically with all those delays get hit at a project level.” (Q3 FY26). Reversible only if the money arrives (Section 05).

  • Backward integration. The Raipur rolling mill, announced without numbers.

Against that: every item has been argued in some form for two years while the company-basis margin printed 8.3, 8.2, 8.3. India T&D margins are “much better than what they were in the past” but “I don’t see a significant improvement” (Q2 FY26); international T&D at 10–12% is higher but, as the CEO said in August 2026, carries higher risk; and Q1 FY27 steel and diesel are the stated watch items. In August 2026 he said Water “at EBITDA level has delivered reasonably good margins, but because of interest costs, they’re getting hit at a PBT level.” Both cannot describe the same ledger, and neither the annual report (cost notes stripped) nor an analyst resolved it.

One more item that flatters the PAT, not the margin: the FY26 tax rate was 24.8% (₹340 Cr on ₹1,371 Cr) against the 28–30% consolidated rate management guided in May 2025, May 2026 and elsewhere. 05Working capital: gross, net, and the Water receivable

The industry’s binding constraint, KPIL’s reported answer, and a fix to which number CRISIL’s 264 days should be compared with

Contract terms are milestone-based with retention: the CEO said in October 2024 “We always have the 10% retention, but we also have milestone-based payments”, and CRISIL notes that sizeable retention money “remains blocked in projects till the end of the performance guarantee period” on execution cycles of 2.0–2.5 years. Revenue is booked as costs are incurred, so profit appears before cash. Management’s own account of the cash cycle, given in October 2024 when it explained the QIP: milestone payments are “back dated” and the company funds about 40% of free cash flow into working capital, 30–35% into capex and 30% into dividends and interest.

Net, gross and the perimeter that makes them comparable

Management’s figure is net working capital after payables, advances and retention payable: 94 days standalone, 79 days consolidated in FY25; 90 and 75 in FY26. CRISIL tracks receivables including net unbilled revenue: 257 days (Mar 23), 255 (Mar 24), 264 (Mar 25). CARE counts gross current asset days: 314 (FY23), 311 (FY24). The previous revision set 264 against 75, which compared a standalone-perimeter gross figure with a consolidated net one.

CRISIL’s “operating income” for FY24 is ₹16,760 Cr, which is exactly KPIL’s standalone revenue (Q4 FY24 call), and its FY25 figure of ₹19,004 Cr is close to standalone revenue of ₹18,888 Cr rather than consolidated ₹22,316 Cr. Its analytical approach lists Shree Shubham Logistics, Energy Link and the road SPVs, not LMG or Fasttel. The like-for-like comparison is therefore 264 days gross against 94 days net, a 170-day gap that is funded by payables, customer advances and retentions owed to subcontractors. On CRISIL’s base that is about ₹13,700 Cr of receivables including net unbilled revenue (derived: 264 days on ₹19,004 Cr), against about ₹7,800 Cr of trade receivables on the Screener consolidated base (128 days); the difference of roughly ₹5,900 Cr is the unbilled and contract-asset layer that Screener’s receivables line leaves out.

Asked in May 2025 to reconcile the jump in parent cash from ₹850 Cr to ₹1,920 Cr, the CEO volunteered the mechanism:

07Growth outlook

The cycle is real; the questions are the pace it arrives at, who captures it, and what the company itself now guides

The demand case, and the delivery gap that moderates it

The National Electricity Plan (Transmission) 2023–32 commits about ₹9.15 lakh crore of transmission investment, with HVDC capacity planned to double from 33.5 GW to about 66.75 GW (Ministry of Power, PIB, Oct 2024; research/industry/india-td-epc.md). Roughly ₹6 lakh crore was still to be awarded at that date. The plan’s delivery has run behind: India commissioned about 8,830 circuit-km in FY2025 against a target near 15,000. Refreshed this run from trade-press reports of CEA data: the FY2026 target was cut by CEA to 15,382 ckm from 24,440 originally (37% lower), and April–December 2025 delivered 5,077 ckm, 47.5% of the period target of 10,696 ckm, with 765 kV the weakest. ICRA's commentary reports a recovery to about 12,139 ckm for the full year; its basis could not be checked against the CEA release and the figure is not used. The most important thing management said about growth

On the Q4 FY26 call, Amit Anwani of PL Capital did the arithmetic on the flagship segment: “the order inflow, which I can see for full year was roughly about INR 13,000 crores and last year, it was about INR 14,400 crores. So definitely there is a decline. And even if I take 45% to 50% of the INR 30,000 crores, INR 31,000 crores which you are expecting, it still will be about INR 14,000 crores. So there is no meaningful growth… Is that the correct understanding?” The CEO: “Sure. So Amit, yes, you’re right in terms of absolute inflow. FY25 T&D was higher than FY 26… Our order inflow on T&D might not grow at 20%, which is what our business is growing, or at 15%, which is where the business is growing.”

Twelve months earlier, on the Q4 FY25 call: “We assume that a growth of 20% plus in terms of order book and T&D would be easy.” It fell 10%. In the middle of the largest transmission programme in Indian history, KPIL’s own T&D intake peaked in FY2025.

The domestic leg is the weaker one. Management said in February 2025 that KPIL typically takes 20–25% of the market it addresses: ₹7,000–7,500 Cr of an estimated ₹35,000–40,000 Cr awarded in six months. Domestic T&D inflow was about ₹7,300 Cr in FY25 and about ₹4,500 Cr in FY26, while management's own estimate of the annual addressable market rose to ₹1.0–1.25 lakh crore (Q1 FY27: for at least the next five years, with one or two HVDC projects a year). On those management figures KPIL’s domestic share of the addressable market was in the single digits in FY26. The share claim is management’s own and is not independently verifiable; the shift in the numbers it cites is. International T&D carried FY26: Latin America ex Brazil, Europe through LMG, the Middle East, then Africa as number four because of “a lot of competition, whether Indian competition or even Chinese” (Q1 FY27).

The segments, in management’s own ranking

  • T&D (~41% of the book): 2.5 years of visibility; plant 90% booked for next year at 275,000 t (Feb 2026); the new rolling mill is the only disclosed backward-integration step. “we are among the top 2, 3 globally in all markets, except China in the T&D space” is a claim, not a fact.
  • B&F (~29%): inflow ₹11,460 Cr in FY26, +40%, with half now design-build. SKT, the deputy MD, on the outlook: “we can see the 80% to 85% of current traction to continue over the next 2 years” (Q4 FY25). New: data centres (two done, a third under way) and PSU industrial capex (NMDC, NALCO). Execution, not orders, is the limit: design-build projects take until Q4 to convert to revenue (Q1 FY26).
  • Oil & Gas: one project. Bids of $100–500 Mn are in; tenders are delayed; management expects orders in Q3 FY27 “if not late part of Q2”. The Aramco gas pipeline is most of the Oil & Gas book; its completion in 15–18 months from February 2026 creates a revenue cliff unless a second order lands.
  • Water: domestic bidding stopped until dues clear; the first overseas order is ₹344 Cr. JJM-2 tenders “not so much traction seen” (May 2026).
  • Urban Infra: six machines, all deployed; “not seeing many tenders” recently (Aug 2026).
  • Railways: run down; 95% of the network is electrified. Other new areas named on the Q1 FY27 call: pumped storage, nuclear, international roads; a BESS bid for PGCIL was lost.
  • LMG (Sweden): Q1 FY27 revenue +8% (₹833 Cr) against 30–40% guided for FY26, order book ~₹4,200 Cr, and the CEO: “current year we do not see them doing extremely good”. Listed in September; Section 11.
  • Solar EPC, Middle East: a ~₹2,000 Cr project “included in L1” and expected to conclude “in May, June” (Q4 FY26). The coverage retrieved of the 24 September 2026 order announcement (₹2,025 Cr across T&D, B&F and Oil & Gas) does not mention a solar order; treat it as an open dated commitment.

Why the cycle is not simply extrapolated

Entry barriers are low: listed T&D rivals are bidding the same HVDC packages at lower valuations and higher returns (Section 06), and management itself caps what the cycle can pay: “while we definitely see margins improve from what we have seen in the past. But it can’t go beyond a point because whoever the developer you are writing with, they also TBCB environment have to fight against the large developers… You now see differences which are more 2%, 3%, 4% and healthy competition is very good for all of us.” (Q2 FY25; spreads were 20–30%). The empirical test has run: across the seven years in which this ordering cycle built, the EBITDA margin did not rise (Section 03). One management claim does check out against the filings: that “even with growth, which has happened over the last 10 years of around 15% per annum, our net debt number nearly stays the same” (Q3 FY26). Screener net debt was ₹2,591 Cr in FY17 and ₹1,700 Cr in FY26.

What was never asked

A full-text search of all ten transcripts found: “related part*” 0 hits; “Munot” 0; “contingent” 0; “corporate guarantee” 0; “bid success” or “hit ratio” 0; “reversal” or “write back” 0; “ageing” 0.   • Related-party dealings with the promoter group.   • The Fasttel guarantee. The FY2026 report notes receivables and an expected credit loss “pursuant to invocation of corporate guarantee” for Fasttel. The calls discuss the ₹515 Cr investment write-off, not the guarantee.

  • The ex-Fasttel, ex-roads like-for-like EBITDA margin. Q1 FY27’s +30 bp is a net of two removals and nobody asked for the base.

Narrative management

The Q4 FY26 call opened with: “We previously established clear targets for FY26, starting with revenue growth of 20% to 25%, improvement in consol PBT margins of 100 basis points, a net working capital cycle under 100 days and order inflows of INR 26,000 crores to INR 28,000 crores… Today, I’m proud to report that we have fully delivered on each of these promises.” The statement is true against the targets of May 2025 and not against the ones management itself set later:

  • Revenue was upgraded to 20–25% (Aug 2025), “25% plus” (Oct 2025) and “approx 25%” (Feb 2026), then delivered 22%, scored against the original band.
  • The PBT guide was given as levels in May 2025 (standalone 5.25–5.5%, consolidated 4.5–4.75%), restated in October 2025 as “improving PBT by a minimum of 50 basis points”, and in May 2026 scored against the level. The 5.9% standalone figure is quoted “excluding exceptional items and a one-off dividend income from VEPL”.
  • Order inflow was cut to “25,000 plus” in October 2025 and restored to “26,000-plus” in February 2026.
  • Q1 FY27 revenue growth was headlined as +9% “adjusted for the base effect of our Brazilian business and Road SPVs”; the reported consolidated figure is +4%.
  • ROCE is quoted as “within our targeted range of 21% to 22%” (Aug 2026); on the year-end debt-plus-equity definition used in this report it is 18.2%, and on Malik’s asset-side formula 7.8%. The 2022 merger-announcement target was a 22% ROCE; management's measure says it has been met, the two outside measures say it has not.

The same management also volunteers hard facts that cost it. It volunteered the Water plan cut (from ~₹3,700 Cr to ₹1,600–1,700 Cr, with ~₹1,000 Cr invested over nine months) in its opening remarks in February 2025; it said Fasttel was “a setback for us” and that it was “one area where we have not been able to achieve on whatever projections we did” (Q3 FY26); and in February 2026 it said “we get this hope several times in the last 6 to 7 quarters” about Water cash. That is unusual candour and it is why the grade below is not lower.

1. The dilution reversal. August 2024, Parikshit Kandpal asks whether the balance sheet needs fresh funds. The CEO:

11Capital allocation and the balance sheet

Where the cash went, what it earned, and the numbers that look nothing like the headline

ROCE of 18% on one formula and 8% on another

KPIL earned 18.2% on debt plus equity in FY2026 (the module, year-end basis; 18.6% in the pipeline’s coffee-can table), and cleared the screen’s 15% gate in all nine scored years. EBIT margin is only 7.6%. The return is high because 59% of the ₹27,702 Cr balance sheet is funded by liabilities that are not debt: trade payables, customer advances and retentions owed to others. Shareholders and lenders fund only ₹11,319 Cr. Malik’s own asset-side formula, which does not net out those liabilities, gives 7.8% for FY2026 and 6.7–9.5% across FY18–26. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. The first asks what the owners and lenders earn on their money; the second asks how productive the whole asset base is. The investment case depends on the first remaining high, which requires the float to continue.

Part of that float costs money (₹600–700 Cr of interest-bearing advances at 9–9.5%, Section 05) and all of it is conditional on the order cycle staying open: advances arrive with new orders and stop when ordering slows, while payables and retention still must be paid. The order book to sales ratio, 2.98× to 2.41× in two years, is the leading indicator.

Leverage: three numbers

Management leads with consolidated net debt of ₹915 Cr at March 2026 and ₹917 Cr at June 2026, 0.1× equity, down more than 50% in a year. The module’s borrowings less all cash is ₹1,700 Cr (0.22× net, 0.46× gross). TOL/TNW is 2.56× on the module, 2.18× on CRISIL’s adjusted basis for March 2025. A holder who thinks the company carries no obligations because net D/E is 0.1× has misread the balance sheet; one who thinks it is over-geared at 2.56× has misread the industry. The valuation uses ₹1,700 Cr because the lower figure benefits from year-end cash that is partly restricted (Section 05).

Flag 1: the promoter’s other company builds what KPIL builds for

The structural conflict, narrowed but not resolved

Parag Munot is a promoter-director of KPIL and Managing Director of Kalpataru Limited, the family’s separately listed Mumbai and Thane developer (FY2026 annual report, director biography). KPIL’s Buildings & Factories division is its second-largest business, ₹19,600 Cr of order book, and it builds residential and commercial property for developers. The FY2026 report calls its largest-ever order a “14+ Million sq. ft. residential project in Delhi” (the call says 12 million) and does not name the client. The standalone related-party note (Note 40) lists 17 promoter-affiliated enterprises as “having significant influence and having transactions with the group”, including Kalpataru Limited, Kalpataru Properties, Gurukrupa Developers, Agile Real Estate, Abacus Real Estate and Argos Arkaya Power Solutions LLP. The rupee value of every transaction is missing from the pipeline’s annual-report markdown (all tables were stripped; Section 16).

What can be said. The pipeline’s related-party audit (FY2015–FY2026, score 1, headline “large RPT volume but almost all routine subsidiary treasury support; genuine promoter payments are trivial”) reports that transactions with the Kalpataru-group entities outside the subsidiary tree run to “at most a few crore rupees a year”, that Form AOC-2 shows nothing non-arm’s-length in any year, and that CARO opinions on the standalone company are unqualified. The overlap is also old: KPIL was incorporated in 1981 and Kalpataru Limited in 1988. What cannot be said. That audit is marked human-authored (pre-automation), carries no document citations, and states “all 12 annual reports were cleanly downloaded”, while four of those twelve (FY2015, FY2016, FY2021, FY2025) are 5–15 KB stubs in the store. Its figures could not be re-derived from the markdown. The right reading is that the audit is unverified, not that it is wrong. The CEO’s own statement is clear and untested: “we are 2 different organizations and Kalpataru Limited is a separate organization in every form” (Q3 FY25). A January 2018 forum post asking whether KPTL’s promoters run a similar business through Kalpataru Limited has never been answered (ValuePickr thread 90).

Flag 2: the promoter sold down while funding the real-estate company

Source-by-source timeline

Promoter holding fell from 56.49% (March 2021) to 33.52% (December 2024), CARE. One step is documented in exchange data: on 18 June 2024 Kalpataru Constructions Pvt Ltd, a promoter entity, sold 86.96 lakh shares (about 5.1% of capital) at ₹1,153.99, about ₹1,003 Cr (bulk-deal records; the combined promoter holding fell from 40.59% to 35.24% per one report, a figure that differs elsewhere). The pledge itself has fallen: 51.84% of the promoter holding (March 2022) to 24.59% (December 2024, CARE), and “below 25%” in August 2025 (CEO). Loans against the shares were about ₹500 Cr at roughly four times cover (August 2024) and “1:4”, “less than 1/3 or 25%” of value (May 2026). Against that trend, a July 2025 exchange filing, as summarised by a news service, shows K C Holdings pledging 14.81 lakh shares (0.87% of capital) to Aditya Birla Capital in July 2025; the summary is inconsistent on the amount (₹750 Mn in the body, “$90m” in the headline), so the fact of a fresh pledge is medium-confidence and the size is not used. The cleanest statement is that the direction of the pledge is right, the level is still about a quarter of the promoter holding, and the CEO has twice disowned the more ambitious phrasings (Section 09). Position

Good business. Wrong price. No position at ₹1,414.80.

The base case implies 30% downside and the bull case 7% upside, a poor asymmetry for a competitively bid contractor with no pricing power. The analysis changes near ₹1,000, where the price needs a 9.5% margin — the margin the base case already assumes — or about 9.0% if growth runs at management’s 15%. Between ₹1,000 and ₹1,100 the price is justified by the margin management says it is heading to; at ₹1,300 it needs 10.9% on the old growth path, or 10.25% on management’s.

An existing holder faces a different question from a new buyer. On the exit-multiple frame the stock returns about 11% a year, which is defensible, and the balance sheet is better than it has ever been. We would not sell a long-held position on this analysis alone and would not initiate one. This is analysis, not advice.

The one-paragraph version

Kalpataru Projects International is a well-run contractor with the best cash conversion in its listed peer set, a repaired balance sheet, a record order book and a management that delivers what it measures at the PBT and balance-sheet level. It is also a price-taking business in an industry with low entry barriers, whose EBITDA margin stepped down in FY22 and has not recovered; whose flagship segment’s order intake fell in FY26; whose Water receivable has not shrunk in six quarters despite six dated promises; whose guidance ledger is not clean on the things management controls; whose reported PBT improvement is overwhelmingly financial and nearly spent; and whose price, 21.7 times trailing earnings after a year in which most of its sector fell by 30 to 60%, assumes a margin it has not printed. The quality is real; the price assumes that the quality will show up as an operating margin, and that has not happened in the figures yet.

The stored audit says all 12 annual reports were “cleanly downloaded and page-crawled” and quotes amounts (for example a ₹6.48 lakh security deposit with Kalpataru Limited in FY2026). The pipeline holds 10 transcripts and 14 failed downloads for calls from May 2016 to February 2021, all dead links on the pre-rename kalpatarupower.com domain.
